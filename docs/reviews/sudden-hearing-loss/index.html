Sudden hearing loss

A time-sensitive hearing emergency. Separate conductive loss, recognize possible SSNHL, and preserve the treatment window.

Adult pathway · Original illustrations · Not implemented or published

First: identify an emergency beyond the ear

New focal neurologic findings, acute severe imbalance, concerning acute vertigo, or other stroke features require emergency neurologic assessment. Trauma, barotrauma, severe infection, vesicles with facial weakness, and bilateral or recurrent loss need cause-specific evaluation. Do not postpone indicated emergency imaging for an idiopathic SSNHL pathway.

Proposed decision flow

  1. Establish onset and baseline: sudden change, side, prior hearing, tinnitus, fullness, vertigo, medication exposure and neurologic symptoms.
  2. Otoscopy: look for cerumen, effusion, perforation or infection. Reassess after addressing a conductive explanation. A normal ear examination does not exclude an emergency.
  3. Classify at bedside: 512-Hz Weber and Rinne support conductive versus sensorineural classification. Audiometry confirms the type and degree. Uncertain or conflicting fork findings do not exclude SSNHL.
  4. Arrange urgent ENT and audiometry: aim for same-day coordination. Sudden unexplained loss developing within 3 days and beginning within the past 30 days warrants specialist assessment within 24 hours under NICE guidance. AAO-HNS recommends audiometry as soon as possible, within 14 days at the latest.
  5. Discuss early corticosteroids: an initial treatment option within 2 weeks of onset. When SSNHL is suspected, discuss empiric treatment promptly if audiometry is unavailable; do not wait for routine MRI.
  6. Investigate and reassess: MRI for retrocochlear pathology, salvage options if incomplete recovery, repeat audiometry and hearing rehabilitation.

Typical research definition: ≥30 dB sensorineural loss across ≥3 consecutive frequencies developing within 72 hours. Do not use this audiometric definition to postpone referral for suspected sudden loss.

Differential

Conductive: cerumen, effusion, otitis media, perforation, ossicular injury. Sensorineural: idiopathic SSNHL, acoustic trauma, ototoxicity, Ménière disease, autoimmune disease, infectious causes including otosyphilis or zoster, retrocochlear lesions, and vascular disease.

Directed history, examination and targeted testing determine the alternate pathway. New hearing loss with acute vestibular symptoms can reflect posterior-circulation ischemia.

Fork Lab

Use a gently activated 512-Hz tuning fork. Explain the task, use a quiet environment, and compare both ears.

Ask: “Is the sound in the middle, louder on your right, or louder on your left?” Midline can occur with normal hearing or symmetric hearing loss.

Compare bone conduction at the mastoid with air conduction beside the canal. Ask which is louder; repeat on the other ear. AC > BC is normal or sensorineural, not proof of normal hearing.

Interpret the pattern

Educational aid for unilateral symptoms. Formal audiometry remains necessary.

Profound SNHL trap: bone-conducted sound can cross to the opposite cochlea and produce an apparently negative Rinne. Contradictory findings, equal loudness, absent responses or uncertainty require audiometry, not a reassuring conductive label.

Early steroid discussion

AAO-HNS: clinicians may offer corticosteroids within 2 weeks of onset. Discuss uncertain benefit, spontaneous recovery and treatment risks. Early access matters; this is not a guaranteed hearing-restoration treatment.

Systemic reference regimen

Prednisone 1 mg/kg orally daily, usual maximum 60 mg/day. AAO-HNS Table 10 describes full dose for 7–14 days, then taper over a similar period. Duration and taper should be individualized with ENT; published protocols vary. Standard low-dose steroid packs are inadequate for this regimen.

Before prescribing

Assess glucose control, active infection, prior severe psychiatric steroid effects, gastrointestinal bleeding risk, concurrent immunosuppression and other patient-specific risks. Diabetes requires an explicit monitoring plan, not automatic dismissal of treatment.

Discuss intratympanic therapy with ENT when systemic treatment is unsuitable. Persistent incomplete recovery: offer or refer for intratympanic salvage within 2–6 weeks of onset.

Do not substitute antivirals

Do not routinely prescribe antivirals, thrombolytics, vasodilators or vasoactive drugs for idiopathic SSNHL. Identified zoster, syphilis or other specific causes need their own treatment pathway.

Protect the treatment window

  • Today: document onset, exclude emergencies, perform otoscopy/fork tests, coordinate ENT and audiometry; discuss early treatment.
  • Within 24 hours: NICE specialist target for unexplained sudden loss developing within 3 days, onset within the last 30 days.
  • Within 14 days: AAO-HNS outer limit for diagnostic audiometry and initial steroid option. Do not treat this as permission to wait.
  • 2–6 weeks: intratympanic steroid salvage for incomplete recovery.
  • HBOT with steroids: optional initial therapy within 2 weeks or salvage within 1 month, subject to availability and shared decision-making.
  • After treatment and within 6 months: repeat audiometry. Address residual hearing loss, tinnitus and rehabilitation.

Late presentation still warrants assessment. NICE recommends review within 2 weeks when sudden loss began over 30 days ago, or when loss worsened rapidly over 4–90 days.

Workup without delaying care

Audiometry: establish sensorineural loss and baseline. Bedside forks cannot replace it.

MRI: arrange an appropriate internal auditory canal/brain protocol to evaluate retrocochlear disease such as vestibular schwannoma. Hearing recovery does not eliminate this evaluation. ABR is an AAO-HNS alternative when MRI cannot be obtained, but a negative ABR does not fully exclude retrocochlear disease. MRI is preferred when feasible; coordinate follow-up with ENT.

No routine head CT or broad laboratory panels for presumed idiopathic SSNHL. Focal neurologic findings, trauma or chronic ear disease may justify targeted imaging; these exceptions require an appropriate separate workup.
