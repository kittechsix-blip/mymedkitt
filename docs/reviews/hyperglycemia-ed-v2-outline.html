Hyperglycemia v2: "Who is this patient, and what exactly do I prescribe?"

The current consult tells you what to think about. It doesn't tell you what to do. v2 turns it into prescriptions: pick the patient in front of you, adjust for their kidneys, heart, food, money and prior meds, and get a complete discharge prescription with doses, cash cost and follow-up. Every jargon word links to a plain-English explainer, and every choice has a "Why?" link for a deeper dive.

2. The flow (3 taps to a prescription)

  1. Safety screen (1 screen). Red flags for DKA/HHS: vomiting, abdominal pain, Kussmaul breathing, altered mental status, ketones/acidosis, glucose ≥600, or weight loss plus ketosis. If any → DKA/HHS consult. Not type 2 or not routine: pregnant, under 18, type 1, pump, or "looks like type 1" (thin, young, rapid weight loss, ketones, autoimmune history) → specialist branch. Plain-English link: How to spot insulin deficiency.
  2. Who is this patient? Five buttons:
    • A. New diagnosis, never treated
    • B. Known diabetes, ran out or stopped meds
    • C. Taking pills, still high
    • D. Taking insulin: still high, having lows, or can't afford it
    • E. Steroid-induced or illness-driven hyperglycemia
  3. How severe? (for A, B and C) Uses A1c (or the ED glucose if no A1c) plus symptoms:
    • Mild: A1c under 8.5 (about 1.5 points above goal), no symptoms → one pill
    • Moderate: A1c 8.5–10 → two pills
    • Severe: A1c over 10, glucose ≥300 with symptoms, or weight loss → basal insulin plus metformin. Once the glucose is under control, many of these patients can drop the insulin later; the explainer says why.
  4. Modifier check (one checklist screen). eGFR band, heart failure, prior heart attack or stroke, liver disease, age 65+ or frail, unreliable meals, heavy alcohol, can't inject or can't see the syringe, uninsured. The modifiers change the recipe; they don't add more screens.
  5. Prescription card, then the discharge bundle (supplies, teach-back, sick-day rules, follow-up).

3. The core prescriptions (proposed doses; the label check happens in the build)

A or B, mild or moderate: start with metformin

Metformin ER 500 mg PO with dinner. Increase by 500 mg every week as tolerated, up to 2000 mg once daily. IR alternative: 500 mg twice daily with meals, titrated to 1000 mg twice daily. Cash: about $4/month (Walmart list) to verify.
Why metformin first: it's cheap, it doesn't cause lows on its own, its safety record goes back decades, and it's weight-neutral. Don't use if eGFR is under 30. Don't start if eGFR is under 45 (ADA 2026). Patients already taking it continue at a reduced dose of 1000 mg/day max, or during AKI, dehydration, hypoxia, decompensated liver disease or heavy alcohol use. GI side effects: take with food and titrate slowly; ER is gentler.

Severe: basal insulin plus metformin

Start 10 units once daily (or 0.1–0.2 units/kg) plus metformin if eligible. Patient self-titrates: +2 units every 3 days while fasting glucose is above 130. If any reading is under 70, cut the dose by 10–20% (often 4 units). Stop titrating and call if the dose passes 0.5 units/kg.

InsuredGlargine (or the formulary basal analog)
Why basal, not a sliding scale: a discharge sliding scale chases yesterday's number and stacks doses, so lows are the main danger. One background dose that the patient titrates is safer and simpler. Why bedtime NPH: NPH peaks 4–12 hours after injection. Given at bedtime, the peak covers the overnight liver glucose release that drives the morning high. A plain-English insulin-curve diagram shows it.

D. Already on insulin

  • Can't afford analogs: a conversion card. Glargine → NPH: same total units, split 2/3 AM and 1/3 bedtime. Lispro → regular insulin: same units, injected 30 minutes before the meal. Or 70/30 premix: 80% of the total daily dose, split 2/3 before breakfast and 1/3 before dinner. Each conversion lists the meal-timing rules and a warning that lows are more likely.
  • Lows: cut the responsible dose 10–20%. Fasting lows point to basal; lows after a meal point to the mealtime dose. Stop sulfonylureas.
  • Fasting high: basal titration (existing calculator).
  • Fasting OK but high after meals: add one mealtime injection at the biggest meal, 4 units or 10% of basal. Don't keep raising basal; the "overbasalization" explainer covers why.
  • Missed doses, ran out: restart the old regimen. If more than a few days were missed and intake is lower, restart at about 80%.

E. Steroid-induced

Lower confidence; your call: no guideline consensus. This rule comes from educational sources; an inpatient RCT used about half the dose (0.15 units/kg for prednisone 10–40 mg). I propose the more conservative RCT dosing for discharge. With once-daily morning prednisone: NPH given with the steroid dose, 0.1 units/kg per 10 mg prednisone, up to 0.4 units/kg. Taper the NPH as the steroid tapers, and stop it when the steroid stops. Short burst and A1c normal: often no treatment; check glucose and return if symptoms.

4. Plain-English explainers ("What does that mean?")

Each one is short: what it is, a real-world analogy, why it matters for the ED decision, and the key numbers. Terms are linked inline wherever they appear, and a 📖 Terms toolbar button lists them all.

  • Basal vs mealtime (prandial) vs correction insulin. The pilot light, the burner you turn up when you cook, and the fix for a pot that's already boiling over.
  • Analog vs human insulin. Glargine and lispro are lab-tuned, more predictable and pricier. NPH and regular are older, cheaper, peak harder and need meal timing. Includes the action-curve diagram.
  • NPH, regular and 70/30 premix: when each one fits the uninsured patient.
  • TDD (total daily dose): why basal alone is not TDD.
  • ICR and ISF (carb ratio and correction factor): the 500 and 1800 rules.
  • A1c: the 3-month average, the 6.5 diagnosis cutoff, and when it misleads (anemia, transfusion, CKD, hemoglobinopathy).
  • Spotting insulin deficiency: type 1, LADA and ketosis-prone type 2.
  • eGFR and diabetes drugs: one table of every drug by eGFR band.
  • Sulfonylureas and lows: why glipizide and not glyburide.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: heart and kidney benefit, euglycemic DKA, sick-day hold, genital infections.
  • GLP-1 drugs: weight and CV benefit, why not to start them in the ED, cost.
  • Why no discharge sliding scale, and why "overbasalization" happens.
  • Fasting vs after-meal pattern: which dose to change.
  • Glucose targets: fasting 80–130 for most patients, and looser for elderly or frail patients or those with lows.

5. New tool: Bridge Plan Builder (calculator)

Inputs: patient type (A–E), A1c or glucose, symptoms or weight loss, eGFR, HF / ASCVD / liver, age 65+ or frail, meals reliable, alcohol, uninsured, weight, and for E the prednisone dose.

Output:

  • The recommended regimen, with exact starting doses and the titration rule
  • The cheapest safe version
  • One line for each choice ("Glipizide avoided: meals unreliable")
  • The supply list and the follow-up interval

Hard stops: crisis flags, pregnancy, type 1 clues and eGFR under 15 withhold a regimen and route to the right branch. Unit tests cover every branch and each modifier combination. The existing basal-start, basal-titrate and meal + correction calculators stay, with the same safety gates.
