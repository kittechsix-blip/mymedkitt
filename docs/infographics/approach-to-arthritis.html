Approach to Arthritis — ED Evaluation

ED evaluation of adults with acute joint pain/swelling — monoarticular, oligoarticular, or polyarticular. Covers distinguishing septic arthritis from crystal arthropathy (gout, CPPD) and systemic rheumatic disease (RA, SLE, psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, other infectious arthritis), and determines ED disposition.

Initial assessment

Arthritis = joint inflammation. The ED approach hinges on two questions: is this septic arthritis (an orthopedic emergency), and what is the joint-count pattern (guides the differential).

  • Monoarticular: 1 joint
  • Oligoarticular: 2-4 joints
  • Polyarticular: 5+ joints
  • Acute: <6 weeks duration
  • Chronic: >6 weeks duration
Key principle
Acute monoarticular arthritis is septic until proven otherwise. Time to diagnosis matters — cartilage destruction begins within 8 hours of bacterial infection.

Count involved joints and characterize the pattern. Monoarticular points toward septic arthritis, crystal arthropathy, trauma, or early RA/reactive arthritis. Oligoarticular (2-4 joints) points toward reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, gonococcal, or spondyloarthropathy. Polyarticular (5+ joints) points toward RA, SLE, viral arthritis, or (rarely) polyarticular gout.

  • Infectious (MUST rule out): bacterial (septic arthritis), gonococcal (DGI), Lyme arthritis (endemic areas), tuberculous (chronic)
  • Crystal: gout (urate), pseudogout/CPPD (calcium pyrophosphate), basic calcium phosphate
  • Traumatic: hemarthrosis, internal derangement, occult fracture
  • Other: osteoarthritis flare, avascular necrosis, tumor (rare)
Critical teaching point
Crystals do NOT rule out infection. Dual diagnosis (gout + septic) occurs in 1-2% of cases. Always aspirate if uncertain — send for cell count, Gram stain, culture, AND crystals.
  • Spondyloarthropathies: reactive arthritis (post-infectious GI/GU), psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis with peripheral involvement, IBD-associated arthritis
  • Infectious: gonococcal (migratory, then settles), Lyme arthritis, viral arthritis (early), endocarditis (rare)
  • Crystal: polyarticular gout flare, CPPD (can be polyarticular)
  • Connective tissue: early RA, early SLE, sarcoidosis

Pattern clues: asymmetric large joint → spondyloarthropathy; migratory → gonococcal, rheumatic fever; additive → RA, viral; lower-extremity predominant → reactive, spondyloarthropathy.

  • Inflammatory (symmetric small joints): RA, SLE, viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B/C, HIV)
  • Inflammatory (asymmetric): psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, polyarticular gout (chronic tophaceous)
  • Crystalline: chronic tophaceous gout, CPPD/chondrocalcinosis
  • Other: serum sickness, drug reaction, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis

Pattern clues: symmetric MCP/PIP/wrist → RA, SLE, viral; DIP involvement → OA, psoriatic; large AND small joints → psoriatic, reactive; morning stiffness >1 hour → inflammatory.

Key questions: how long is morning stiffness (>60 min = inflammatory); does movement help or hurt; is there swelling, warmth, or redness; any fevers, weight loss, fatigue. Inflammatory markers are supportive but not diagnostic — a normal ESR/CRP does not exclude inflammatory arthritis.

  • Sudden onset (hours): crystal, septic, trauma
  • Gradual onset (days-weeks): inflammatory arthritis, infectious
  • Insidious (months): OA, chronic inflammatory
  • Skin: psoriasis (PsA), rash (SLE, gonococcal, Lyme)
  • Eyes: uveitis (spondyloarthropathy), conjunctivitis (reactive)
  • GI: diarrhea preceding (reactive), IBD
  • GU: urethritis (reactive, gonococcal), recent STI
  • Constitutional: fevers, weight loss (septic, malignancy, SLE)

Medications: diuretics precipitate gout; quinolones cause tendinopathy; checkpoint inhibitors cause inflammatory arthritis; anticoagulants cause hemarthrosis. Social history: IVDU (septic, endocarditis), sexual history (gonococcal), travel (Lyme, viral), recent infection (reactive).

  • Inspection: swelling (soft tissue vs bony), erythema (crystal/septic), deformity (chronic), skin changes (psoriasis, tophi, nodules)
  • Palpation: warmth (compare contralateral), effusion (ballottement/bulge sign for knee), tenderness (joint line vs periarticular), crepitus (OA)
  • Range of motion: painful passive ROM = intra-articular pathology; painful active ROM only = periarticular (tendon, bursa)
  • Specific patterns: 1st MTP = gout (podagra); knee/wrist = CPPD, septic; MCP/PIP/wrist symmetric = RA; DIP = OA, psoriatic; sacroiliac = spondyloarthropathy
  • Extra-articular: tophi (chronic gout), rheumatoid nodules, nail pitting (psoriatic), oral ulcers/rash (SLE)
  • Basic labs (all acute arthritis): CBC with differential, BMP, ESR, CRP, uric acid if gout suspected
  • Directed labs: blood cultures if septic suspected (positive in up to 66%); RF/anti-CCP if RA suspected (not emergent); ANA if SLE suspected (not emergent); HLA-B27 for spondyloarthropathy (not emergent); Lyme serology in endemic areas; GC/Chlamydia NAAT if gonococcal suspected
Interpretation cautions
Uric acid may be NORMAL during an acute gout flare (20-40% of cases). RF is positive in 5% of the healthy population. ANA is positive in 15-20% of healthy individuals. ESR/CRP are non-specific — elevated in many conditions.

The definitive test for monoarticular arthritis is ARTHROCENTESIS.

Septic arthritis

  • Fever >38.5C (>101.3F)
  • Joint erythema with warmth
  • Inability to bear weight or move joint
  • Rapidly progressive symptoms (hours)
  • Recent joint surgery or injection
  • Prosthetic joint
  • Immunocompromise (DM, RA, HIV, transplant)
  • IVDU
  • Overlying skin infection
Orthopedic emergency
Septic arthritis is an orthopedic EMERGENCY. Joint destruction begins within 8 hours. Mortality is 5-15%, higher if treatment is delayed. If ANY high-risk feature is present, proceed with arthrocentesis.
  • Indication: any acute monoarthritis with uncertain etiology
  • Indication: suspicion for septic arthritis
  • Indication: therapeutic drainage
  • Indication: prior to steroid injection (rule out infection)
Relative contraindications
Overlying cellulitis (approach from uninvolved skin), coagulopathy (INR >1.5 or therapeutic anticoagulation — relative), prosthetic joint (defer to orthopedics if possible).
Do not delay
Do NOT delay arthrocentesis for labs to return, imaging, or anticoagulation reversal (unless severe coagulopathy).

Critical points
WBC >50,000 with >90% PMNs is highly suggestive of septic arthritis, BUT 39% of culture-positive septic arthritis has WBC <50,000. Crystals do NOT rule out infection (1-2% dual diagnosis). Gram stain is only 29-50% sensitive but highly specific.
Synovial lactate
Synovial lactate ≥10 mmol/L is highly specific (~97%) but poorly sensitive (~27%) — helps rule IN, cannot rule OUT. Do not withhold treatment on a low lactate.

All patients with suspected or confirmed septic arthritis require admission.

  • Orthopedic surgery consult (all cases)
  • Infectious disease consult (recommended)

  • Serial CRP (best trend marker)
ICU admission criteria
Sepsis/septic shock, polyarticular with bacteremia, suspected endocarditis (S. aureus bacteremia).

Expected course: initial IV antibiotics 2-3 weeks, transition to oral when clinically improved, total duration 3-4 weeks (non-GC) or 7-14 days (GC). Mortality 5-15%; worse outcomes with delayed treatment, hip involvement, virulent organisms, comorbidities. Functional impairment in up to 50%.

Crystal arthropathies

Gout: monosodium urate (MSU) crystals, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent. Classic sites: 1st MTP (podagra), midfoot, ankle, knee. Risk factors: hyperuricemia, diuretics, alcohol, renal disease, metabolic syndrome.

Pseudogout (CPPD): calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate crystals, rhomboid-shaped, positively (or weakly negative) birefringent. Classic sites: knee, wrist, large joints. Associated: elderly, OA, hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia.

Key teaching point
Crystal disease does NOT exclude infection. Gout flare can be triggered by sepsis. When in doubt, treat for BOTH until cultures are negative.

See the full Gout consult for comprehensive gout management.

Acute gouty arthritis: rapid onset (peaks 12-24 hours), exquisitely tender joint, erythema extending beyond the joint (can mimic cellulitis), may have low-grade fever, self-limited (resolves 7-14 days untreated). Podagra (1st MTP) occurs in 50% of first attacks, 90% lifetime.

  • Dietary: alcohol (especially beer), red meat, shellfish, fructose
  • Medications: diuretics (thiazides), low-dose aspirin
  • Medical: surgery, trauma, dehydration, hospitalization
  • Paradox: starting urate-lowering therapy can trigger a flare
Serum uric acid caution
May be NORMAL during an acute flare (20-40% of cases) — urate shifts into the inflamed joint. Recheck 2-4 weeks after resolution if needed. Definitive diagnosis = MSU crystals on synovial fluid analysis.

Choose a first-line agent based on patient factors; can combine NSAID + colchicine for severe attacks.

Systemic rheumatic disease

Evaluate for systemic rheumatic disease if: polyarticular involvement, symmetric joint pattern, morning stiffness >60 minutes, extra-articular features present, or inflammatory markers elevated without infection.

  • Skin: rash, nodules, psoriasis
  • Eyes: dry eyes, uveitis, scleritis
  • Oral: ulcers, dry mouth
  • Pulmonary: dyspnea, pleuritis
  • Cardiac: pericarditis
  • Renal: proteinuria, hematuria
  • Neurologic: peripheral neuropathy

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): chronic symmetric inflammatory polyarthritis of MCPs, PIPs, wrists with DIP-sparing (unlike OA and psoriatic). Morning stiffness >1 hour, improves with activity. Synovial thickening (boggy swelling).

  • Rheumatoid nodules (20-30%)
  • Interstitial lung disease
  • Pericarditis, vasculitis
  • Felty syndrome (RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia)
  • Increased cardiovascular risk

RF: 70-80% sensitive but non-specific (positive in 5% healthy). Anti-CCP: 70-80% sensitive, >95% specific. ESR/CRP elevated in active disease.

ED role
Do NOT start DMARDs in the ED — requires rheumatology guidance. Recognize the pattern, refer to rheumatology, manage acute flare, and rule out septic joint in acute monoarthritis.

First, rule out septic arthritis — RA patients are at increased risk (immunosuppression, joint damage). Tap if a single joint is significantly more inflamed than the others.

SLE is a multisystem autoimmune disease. 90% of SLE patients have arthralgia/arthritis — symmetric polyarthritis (RA-like pattern) but non-erosive (unlike RA). Jaccoud arthropathy (reducible deformities) can occur.

  • Skin: malar (butterfly) rash, discoid lesions, photosensitivity
  • Renal: nephritis (proteinuria, hematuria, casts)
  • Hematologic: cytopenias (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia)
  • Serositis: pleuritis, pericarditis
  • Neurologic: seizures, psychosis, stroke

ANA >95% sensitive (screening). dsDNA specific for SLE, correlates with nephritis activity. Complement (C3, C4) low in active disease. Anti-Smith highly specific but only 30% sensitive.

ED red flags
New lupus nephritis (proteinuria, rising creatinine), neuropsychiatric lupus, severe cytopenias, pericarditis with effusion.

Reactive arthritis: sterile joint inflammation triggered by infection elsewhere. Classic triad (complete in only 1/3): arthritis, urethritis/cervicitis, conjunctivitis.

  • GI triggers: Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, C. difficile
  • GU triggers: Chlamydia trachomatis (most common), less often Ureaplasma
  • Onset: 1-4 weeks after infection
  • Articular: asymmetric oligoarthritis, lower-extremity predominant (knees, ankles), enthesitis (heel pain common), dactylitis

Other features: keratoderma blennorrhagicum (palms/soles), circinate balanitis, oral ulcers, cardiac (aortitis, conduction abnormalities — rare). HLA-B27 positive in 60-80%. Screen for Chlamydia (NAAT). Stool culture if recent diarrhea.

Gonococcal arthritis (DGI): young, sexually active patients; migratory polyarthralgia then localizes; tenosynovitis (wrists, fingers, ankles); dermatitis (pustular/vesicular lesions). Treatment: ceftriaxone 1g IV/IM + doxycycline for Chlamydia.

Viral arthritis: parvovirus B19 (symmetric polyarthritis, RA-like, adults), hepatitis B/C (polyarthritis, may have urticaria), HIV (can mimic RA, reactive, or psoriatic) — treatment is supportive, NSAIDs. TB arthritis: chronic monoarthritis (hip, knee, spine); risk factors: endemic area, immunocompromise; may have pulmonary TB; requires biopsy for diagnosis.

Crystal arthropathies

Psoriatic arthritis — Asymmetric oligoarthritis (70%), dactylitis, enthesitis, nail changes. RF/anti-CCP usually negative.

Reactive arthritis — Sterile arthritis post-infection (GI or GU). Classic triad complete in only 1/3. HLA-B27 positive 60-80%.

Other infectious arthritis (GC, Lyme, viral, TB) — Gonococcal (migratory then localizes + dermatitis), Lyme (knee monoarthritis, endemic areas), viral (parvovirus B19, hepatitis, HIV), TB (chronic monoarthritis).

RA flare: rule out septic, treat with prednisone — Tap if one joint significantly worse. Prednisone 10-20mg daily; continue DMARDs. Watch for atlantoaxial instability.

SLE flare management (mild → severe) — Mild: NSAIDs/topical steroid. Moderate: prednisone 0.5-1mg/kg/day. Severe (nephritis/CNS): pulse methylprednisolone 1g IV x3 days, admit.

Reactive arthritis treatment — NSAIDs first-line; treat triggering Chlamydia with doxycycline. Most resolve in 3-12 months.

Disposition overview — Admit: septic, severe flare needing IV therapy, organ-threatening systemic disease, cannot ambulate. Discharge: crystal or inflammatory flare responding to oral therapy with reliable follow-up.

Discharge: rheumatology referral — New inflammatory arthritis, SLE without organ threat, reactive arthritis. Urgent referral 1-2 weeks.

Discharge: PCP follow-up — Gout/CPPD with good treatment response. Complete course, recheck uric acid 2-4 weeks, dietary counseling.

Admission required — Septic arthritis, PJI, severe flare needing IV steroids, lupus with organ involvement, vasculitis, cannot self-care.

Reference: technique, imaging & differential summary (not tied to the decision flow)

  • Knee (lateral approach): patient supine, knee extended or slightly flexed; 1cm proximal and 1cm lateral to superior-lateral patella; direct needle horizontally toward intercondylar notch; 18-20G needle, 10-20mL syringe
  • Ankle: anteromedial approach, medial to tibialis anterior tendon; patient supine, foot plantar flexed
  • Wrist: dorsal approach between 3rd and 4th extensor compartments; "Lister tubercle" palpable on distal radius
  • 1st MTP: dorsomedial approach, avoid extensor tendon
  • Ultrasound guidance improves success rate 20-30%, especially for hip, shoulder, small joints, and reduces complications
  • Fluid volumes: cell count 1-2mL, Gram stain/culture 1-2mL, crystals 1-2mL (no anticoagulant); minimum 2-3mL total for basic analysis

Plain radiographs: first-line for all acute arthritis; useful for fracture, chondrocalcinosis (CPPD), erosions, OA changes. Ultrasound: confirms effusion, guides aspiration, detects synovitis/erosions/crystal deposits, no radiation. MRI: most sensitive for early changes (synovitis, bone marrow edema, erosions); not needed emergently in most cases; useful for suspected osteomyelitis or diagnostic uncertainty. CT: complex anatomy (SI joints, sternoclavicular), procedural planning, dual-energy CT for gout. When to image: all monoarticular arthritis gets a plain film at minimum; septic suspected — imaging should NOT delay aspiration.
