Pediatric Osteomyelitis: Recognition to Disposition

Children 0-18 years in the ED/inpatient setting with suspected or confirmed acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO). Excludes adults, chronic/relapsed osteomyelitis (symptoms >3 months or relapse after treatment), and post-surgical/hardware-associated osteomyelitis (different pathogen spectrum and management).

⚠ Concurrent septic arthritis is an orthopedic emergency — synovial WBC >50,000/mcL (often >100,000) with >90% PMNs requires emergent joint washout/arthrotomy. Send Kingella kingae PCR for children <5 years (culture is insensitive for this fastidious organism).
⚠ Start empiric antibiotics within 1 hour of suspicion in ill-appearing children — obtain blood cultures first, but do NOT delay treatment waiting on culture results.
⚠ Kingella kingae is resistant to vancomycin and clindamycin — do NOT use clindamycin alone as empiric therapy in children under 4 years; cefazolin covers both Kingella and S. aureus.
⚠ Rifampin must NOT be used as monotherapy for MRSA osteomyelitis — always pair with TMP-SMX.
⚠ Avoid NSAIDs until the infection is controlled — they can mask fever and delay recognition of treatment failure.

1 · Clinical recognition

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) is the most common bone infection in children, peaking at ages 12-24 months. Definitions: acute (<4 weeks), subacute (1-3 months, often insidious), chronic (>3 months or relapse, often with sequestrum). Start empiric antibiotics within 1 hour of suspicion in ill-appearing children — obtain blood and/or bone cultures first, but do NOT delay treatment.

Toddlers/preschool (1-5 years)Limp or refusal to bear weight, localized pain/swelling/warmth, fever present in ~78% of AHO; Kingella kingae predominates
School-age/adolescentsLocalized bone pain and tenderness, fever, malaise, point tenderness over metaphysis, may have overlying soft tissue swelling

Most common sites: femur, tibia, humerus (metaphyses of long bones). Weight-bearing as tolerated — immobilization is NOT required.

Kocher criteria (originally validated for the hip) distinguish septic arthritis from transient synovitis: fever (Tmax >38.5°C), non-weight bearing, ESR >40 mm/hr, WBC >12,000/mcL. The Caird modification adds CRP >20 mg/L.

2 · What to do now

Concern for septic arthritis — treat as orthopedic emergency
Joint effusion with adjacent bone infection requires arthrocentesis. Synovial WBC 50,000/mcL or higher, emergent orthopedic consultation, joint washout. Send Kingella PCR if age <5 years.
Sickle cell disease — cover Salmonella AND S. aureus
Salmonella causes 50-80% of SCD osteomyelitis. Empiric coverage must include BOTH Salmonella and S. aureus. MRI differentiates infection from bone infarction (vaso-occlusive crisis) — fever >72 hours and a single asymmetric site favor infection.
Immunocompromised — broaden empiric spectrum
Cover MRSA, gram-negative bacilli (including Pseudomonas), and consider fungi/mycobacteria; add antifungal coverage if neutropenic. Lower threshold for bone biopsy; ID consultation recommended. MRI preferred; consider atypical sites (vertebrae, pelvis).
Puncture wound through shoe — cover Pseudomonas
Add an anti-pseudomonal agent — Cefepime or Piperacillin-tazobactam — PLUS S. aureus coverage. Small bones (metatarsals, calcaneus) typically involved; surgical debridement is frequently required.

Bone infarction (vaso-occlusive crisis) mimics osteomyelitis. Fever >72 hours favors osteomyelitis; multiple symmetric sites favor infarction; a single asymmetric site favors infection. MRI is the imaging modality of choice to differentiate. Empiric coverage must include BOTH S. aureus AND Salmonella. See the Sickle Cell Consult for comprehensive management.

Immunocompromised host — broader spectrumfungi, mycobacteria

Broader pathogen spectrum: S. aureus (including MRSA), gram-negative bacilli (including Pseudomonas), fungi (Aspergillus, Candida), mycobacteria. Empiric regimen: broad-spectrum coverage including an anti-pseudomonal agent; consider antifungal coverage if neutropenic; ID consultation recommended. Imaging: MRI preferred; consider atypical sites (vertebrae, pelvis). Lower threshold for bone biopsy.

Puncture wound through shoe — Pseudomonas riskanti-pseudomonal

Puncture wounds through footwear (especially rubber-soled shoes) carry specific risk for Pseudomonas aeruginosa osteomyelitis. Typical presentation: puncture 1-3 weeks prior, progressive pain/swelling, often small bones (metatarsals, calcaneus). Empiric coverage must include an anti-pseudomonal agent — Cefepime or Piperacillin-tazobactam — PLUS S. aureus coverage. Surgical debridement is frequently required due to retained foreign body and poor tissue penetration.

5 · Laboratory workup

CRP is most useful for monitoring response — may be normal early or in walled-off S. aureus infections; peaks at 2-4 days, normalizes in 9-12 days with appropriate treatment. ESR is less dynamic and remains elevated longer. Procalcitonin >2.0 ng/mL is highly specific for invasive bacterial infection. WBC count is often normal (mean ~10,000) — poor sensitivity for osteomyelitis. Blood cultures: obtain in ALL patients before antibiotics — positive in ~40-66% of AHO cases and may be the only positive culture if bone biopsy is not performed.

6 · Imaging

Plain radiographs are first-line but insensitive early (changes take 10-14 days). MRI is the gold standard: 97% sensitive, 92% specific — detects bone marrow edema, abscess, subperiosteal collections; best for surgical planning. Bone scan (Tc-99m) is useful when MRI is unavailable or for multifocal disease (84-100% sensitive). Ultrasound can detect subperiosteal abscess and joint effusion but is operator-dependent.

MRI findings & surgical triggers

T1: decreased signal in affected bone marrow (loss of normal fatty marrow signal). T2/STIR: increased signal (edema) — best for extent. Contrast-enhanced: rim-enhancing abscess; non-enhancing areas suggest necrosis; subperiosteal collections. Features requiring surgical intervention: subperiosteal abscess, intraosseous abscess >1 cm, soft tissue abscess, sequestrum formation. Pitfall: MRI may overestimate extent due to reactive edema — clinical correlation essential.

Alternative imaging when MRI unavailable/unstable

Plain radiographs (AP + lateral): soft tissue swelling is the earliest sign; periosteal elevation/lytic changes appear at >10-14 days. Ultrasound detects subperiosteal fluid and can guide aspiration; limited for intraosseous pathology. Bone scan (Tc-99m MDP): sensitive within 24-48 hours, useful for multifocal screening, less specific than MRI. CT: better cortical detail, detects sequestrum well, higher radiation. Start empiric antibiotics regardless; obtain MRI once the patient stabilizes for surgical planning.

Multifocal osteomyelitis — ~7% of cases

Associated with virulent pathogens (MRSA, PVL+ strains), disseminated infection, immune compromise. Imaging: bone scan (Tc-99m MDP) best for whole-body screening, followed by MRI of affected regions; consider PET/CT if occult malignancy is a concern. Higher morbidity: longer antibiotic courses, more surgical interventions, greater risk of chronic osteomyelitis. Pathogen identification is critical — consider bone biopsy at multiple sites if feasible.

9 · Surgical consultation

Orthopedic consultation is recommended for ALL cases of AHO. Indications for urgent surgical intervention: subperiosteal or intraosseous abscess, sequestrum (dead bone), concurrent septic arthritis, failure to improve after 48-72 hours of appropriate antibiotics, or need for pathogen identification (bone biopsy). Role of surgery: drainage of abscess/pus, debridement of necrotic tissue, culture/pathogen identification, decompression of intraosseous pressure.

Surgical management details

Bone aspiration/biopsy (fluoroscopic or ultrasound-guided; consider if blood cultures negative). Debridement: removal of necrotic bone/tissue, curettage of abscess cavity, may require multiple procedures. Drainage: subperiosteal abscess drainage, joint washout if concurrent septic arthritis. Considerations: minimize damage to the growth plate (physis), preserve blood supply, consider wound VAC for complex cases. Post-operative: continue IV antibiotics, monitor drain output, repeat imaging if poor response.

Monitoring treatment responseCRP, fever curve

Clinical markers of improvement: resolution of fever (typically 24-72 hours), decreased pain/swelling, return of limb function, improved appetite/activity. CRP is the most useful laboratory marker — should decrease by 50% within 2-4 days of effective treatment and return to normal in 9-12 days; ESR is slower to respond and less useful for acute monitoring. Poor response indicators: persistent fever >72 hours, rising or plateaued CRP, clinical deterioration, new abscess formation. If poor response, consider repeat MRI, surgical drainage/debridement, or a resistant/alternative pathogen.

10 · Transition to oral therapy

PIDS/IDSA guidelines recommend early transition to oral therapy. Criteria: significant clinical improvement, afebrile for 24-48 hours, tolerating oral intake, CRP decreasing (by 50% or to <20-30 mg/L), reliable oral option based on pathogen/sensitivities, compliant family with reliable follow-up. Typical IV duration: 2-7 days (median 3-4 days in recent studies). Benefits of early oral transition: reduced IV complications (CLABSI, thrombosis), shorter hospitalization, lower costs, similar outcomes to prolonged IV therapy.

11 · Disposition

Discharge criteria met: afebrile >24-48 hours, clinical improvement (decreased pain, improved mobility), CRP decreasing or normalized, tolerating oral intake, appropriate oral antibiotic available, family demonstrates medication administration understanding, follow-up arranged. Discharge instructions: complete the full antibiotic course (typically 3-4 weeks total), take with food if GI upset, activity restrictions per orthopedics, return precautions for fever/increased pain/redness/swelling. Follow-up: orthopedics 1-2 weeks; primary care or ID 1 week; CRP at 1-2 weeks.

Continue hospital admission for ongoing IV antibiotic therapy, clinical monitoring, pending culture results, pain management, or surgical intervention if needed. Daily assessment: fever curve, pain score and analgesic requirements, limb exam (swelling, erythema, ROM), CRP trending every 2-3 days. Reassess surgical need if no improvement after 48-72 hours, rising CRP, or new symptoms suggesting abscess. Consults: orthopedics (all cases), infectious disease (complex cases, MRSA, multifocal).

OPAT may be appropriate when clinically improved but an oral option is not available/suitable, the patient is stable for discharge, family and home infusion support are reliable, and a PICC line can be placed. Common OPAT regimens: ceftriaxone 50-100 mg/kg IV q24h, or vancomycin via home infusion (requires monitoring). Considerations: PICC line care/monitoring, weekly labs (CBC, CMP, drug levels), regular clinic follow-up, risk of line complications. Recent evidence suggests early oral transition has similar outcomes to prolonged IV therapy with fewer complications.

Other pitfalls5 items
Do NOT delay empiric antibiotics waiting on culture results in an ill-appearing child — start within 1 hour of suspicion.
Do NOT use clindamycin alone as empiric therapy in children <4 years — Kingella kingae is resistant to both vancomycin and clindamycin.
Do NOT use rifampin as monotherapy for MRSA osteomyelitis — always pair with TMP-SMX.
Do NOT give NSAIDs until the infection is controlled — they can mask fever and delay recognition of treatment failure.
Do NOT immobilize the limb by default — weight-bearing as tolerated; immobilization is not required for uncomplicated AHO.

Puncture wounds through footwear (especially rubber-soled shoes) carry specific risk for Pseudomonas aeruginosa osteomyelitis. Typical presentation: puncture 1-3 weeks prior, progressive pain/swelling, often small bones (metatarsals, calcaneus). Empiric coverage must include an anti-pseudomonal agent — [Cefepime](#/drug/cefepime/adult meningitis) or [Piperacillin-tazobactam](#/drug/piperacillin-tazobactam/sepsis) — PLUS S. aureus coverage. Surgical debridement is frequently required due to retained foreign body and poor tissue penetration.

5 · Laboratory workup

**CRP** is most useful for monitoring response — may be normal early or in walled-off S. aureus infections; peaks at **2-4 days**, normalizes in **9-12 days** with appropriate treatment. **ESR** is less dynamic and remains elevated longer. **Procalcitonin >2.0 ng/mL** is highly specific for invasive bacterial infection. **WBC count** is often normal (mean ~10,000) — poor sensitivity for osteomyelitis. **Blood cultures**: obtain in ALL patients before antibiotics — positive in ~**40-66%** of AHO cases and may be the only positive culture if bone biopsy is not performed.

6 · Imaging

Plain radiographs are first-line but insensitive early (changes take 10-14 days). MRI is the gold standard: **97%** sensitive, **92%** specific — detects bone marrow edema, abscess, subperiosteal collections; best for surgical planning. Bone scan (Tc-99m) is useful when MRI is unavailable or for multifocal disease (84-100% sensitive). Ultrasound can detect subperiosteal abscess and joint effusion but is operator-dependent.

MRI findings & surgical triggers

T1: decreased signal in affected bone marrow (loss of normal fatty marrow signal). T2/STIR: increased signal (edema) — best for extent. Contrast-enhanced: rim-enhancing abscess; non-enhancing areas suggest necrosis; subperiosteal collections. Features requiring surgical intervention: subperiosteal abscess, intraosseous abscess >1 cm, soft tissue abscess, sequestrum formation. Pitfall: MRI may overestimate extent due to reactive edema — clinical correlation essential.

Alternative imaging when MRI unavailable/unstable

Plain radiographs (AP + lateral): soft tissue swelling is the earliest sign; periosteal elevation/lytic changes appear at >10-14 days. Ultrasound detects subperiosteal fluid and can guide aspiration; limited for intraosseous pathology. Bone scan (Tc-99m MDP): sensitive within 24-48 hours, useful for multifocal screening, less specific than MRI. CT: better cortical detail, detects sequestrum well, higher radiation. Start empiric antibiotics regardless; obtain MRI once the patient stabilizes for surgical planning.

Multifocal osteomyelitis — ~7% of cases

Associated with virulent pathogens (MRSA, PVL+ strains), disseminated infection, immune compromise. Imaging: bone scan (Tc-99m MDP) best for whole-body screening, followed by MRI of affected regions; consider PET/CT if occult malignancy is a concern. Higher morbidity: longer antibiotic courses, more surgical interventions, greater risk of chronic osteomyelitis. Pathogen identification is critical — consider bone biopsy at multiple sites if feasible.

7 · Pathogens by age & MRSA risk

Age group | Most common pathogens

<3 months (neonates) | S. aureus, Group B Streptococcus, gram-negative bacilli (E. coli, Klebsiella)

3 months - 4 years | Kingella kingae (predominant in many regions), S. aureus, S. pyogenes (GAS)

>4 years | S. aureus (most common overall), MRSA in high-prevalence areas, S. pyogenes

MRSA risk factors: prior MRSA infection/colonization, local community MRSA prevalence >10-15%, recent hospitalization, recurrent skin/soft tissue infections, household contact with MRSA. MRSA osteomyelitis features more severe illness, higher rates of DVT/pulmonary emboli, more surgical interventions, and longer hospital stays.

8 · Empiric antibiotics

Vancomycin: 15 mg/kg IV q6h IV — Max 60 mg/kg/day. Trough goal 15-20 mcg/mL for serious MRSA infections. myMedKitt drug-store has no matching "osteomyelitis" indication for vancomycin — this dose is taken directly from the consult tree.

Clindamycin per dose (10 mg/kg low end, IV q8h): 200 mg

Cefazolin per dose (40 mg/kg, capped at 2000 mg): 800 mg

Nafcillin per dose (50 mg/kg, per consult tree): 1000 mg

Add gram-negative coverage if: neonate, sickle cell disease (Salmonella coverage), puncture wound (Pseudomonas coverage), or immunocompromised.

Kingella kingae is the most common pathogen in children **6 months - 4 years** in many regions — fastidious (requires PCR or enriched culture; standard cultures often negative), mild presentation, excellent prognosis. Kingella kingae is susceptible to beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones, but **Resistant to: Vancomycin, clindamycin**. Cefazolin covers both Kingella AND S. aureus — do NOT use clindamycin alone in this age group.

9 · Surgical consultation

Orthopedic consultation is recommended for ALL cases of AHO. Indications for urgent surgical intervention: subperiosteal or intraosseous abscess, sequestrum (dead bone), concurrent septic arthritis, failure to improve after **48-72 hours** of appropriate antibiotics, or need for pathogen identification (bone biopsy). Role of surgery: drainage of abscess/pus, debridement of necrotic tissue, culture/pathogen identification, decompression of intraosseous pressure.

Surgical management details

Bone aspiration/biopsy (fluoroscopic or ultrasound-guided; consider if blood cultures negative). Debridement: removal of necrotic bone/tissue, curettage of abscess cavity, may require multiple procedures. Drainage: subperiosteal abscess drainage, joint washout if concurrent septic arthritis. Considerations: minimize damage to the growth plate (physis), preserve blood supply, consider wound VAC for complex cases. Post-operative: continue IV antibiotics, monitor drain output, repeat imaging if poor response.

Monitoring treatment response

Clinical markers of improvement: resolution of fever (typically 24-72 hours), decreased pain/swelling, return of limb function, improved appetite/activity. CRP is the most useful laboratory marker — should decrease by 50% within 2-4 days of effective treatment and return to normal in 9-12 days; ESR is slower to respond and less useful for acute monitoring. Poor response indicators: persistent fever >72 hours, rising or plateaued CRP, clinical deterioration, new abscess formation. If poor response, consider repeat MRI, surgical drainage/debridement, or a resistant/alternative pathogen.

10 · Transition to oral therapy

PIDS/IDSA guidelines recommend early transition to oral therapy. Criteria: significant clinical improvement, afebrile for 24-48 hours, tolerating oral intake, CRP decreasing (by 50% or to <20-30 mg/L), reliable oral option based on pathogen/sensitivities, compliant family with reliable follow-up. Typical IV duration: **2-7 days** (median 3-4 days in recent studies). Benefits of early oral transition: reduced IV complications (CLABSI, thrombosis), shorter hospitalization, lower costs, similar outcomes to prolonged IV therapy.

Cephalexin (MSSA oral step-down): 25-30 mg/kg PO q8h PO — High bioavailability, well-tolerated; first-line oral step-down for MSSA. myMedKitt drug-store's cephalexin entry is for pediatric UTI (75 mg/kg/day), not osteomyelitis — this dose is taken directly from the consult tree.

Cephalexin per dose (25 mg/kg low end, PO q8h): 500 mg

Clindamycin (MRSA oral step-down): 10-13 mg/kg PO q8h PO — For clindamycin-susceptible MRSA; ~90% oral bioavailability. Check D-test sensitivity — avoid if D-test positive (inducible resistance).

Clindamycin PO per dose (10 mg/kg low end, PO q8h): 200 mg

Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Kingella coverage, <4 years): 90 mg/kg/day PO divided q12h (amoxicillin component) PO — Alternative oral step-down for Kingella kingae in children <4 years; cephalexin is also acceptable. myMedKitt drug-store's amoxicillin-clavulanate entry is for pediatric UTI (40 mg/kg/day), not osteomyelitis — this dose is taken directly from the consult tree.

Amoxicillin-clavulanate per dose (45 mg/kg, PO q12h): 900 mg

Rifampin per dose (10 mg/kg, PO q12h): 200 mg

Total antibiotic duration: typically **3-4 weeks**. Shorter courses (2-3 weeks) may be adequate for uncomplicated cases with rapid response.

11 · Disposition

Discharge criteria met: afebrile >24-48 hours, clinical improvement (decreased pain, improved mobility), CRP decreasing or normalized, tolerating oral intake, appropriate oral antibiotic available, family demonstrates medication administration understanding, follow-up arranged. Discharge instructions: complete the full antibiotic course (typically 3-4 weeks total), take with food if GI upset, activity restrictions per orthopedics, return precautions for fever/increased pain/redness/swelling. Follow-up: orthopedics 1-2 weeks; primary care or ID 1 week; CRP at 1-2 weeks.

Continue hospital admission for ongoing IV antibiotic therapy, clinical monitoring, pending culture results, pain management, or surgical intervention if needed. Daily assessment: fever curve, pain score and analgesic requirements, limb exam (swelling, erythema, ROM), CRP trending every 2-3 days. Reassess surgical need if no improvement after 48-72 hours, rising CRP, or new symptoms suggesting abscess. Consults: orthopedics (all cases), infectious disease (complex cases, MRSA, multifocal).

OPAT may be appropriate when clinically improved but an oral option is not available/suitable, the patient is stable for discharge, family and home infusion support are reliable, and a PICC line can be placed. Common OPAT regimens: ceftriaxone 50-100 mg/kg IV q24h, or vancomycin via home infusion (requires monitoring). Considerations: PICC line care/monitoring, weekly labs (CBC, CMP, drug levels), regular clinic follow-up, risk of line complications. Recent evidence suggests early oral transition has similar outcomes to prolonged IV therapy with fewer complications.

Other pitfalls

**Do NOT delay empiric antibiotics** waiting on culture results in an ill-appearing child — start within 1 hour of suspicion.

**Do NOT use clindamycin alone** as empiric therapy in children <4 years — Kingella kingae is resistant to both vancomycin and clindamycin.

**Do NOT use rifampin as monotherapy** for MRSA osteomyelitis — always pair with TMP-SMX.

**Do NOT give NSAIDs** until the infection is controlled — they can mask fever and delay recognition of treatment failure.

**Do NOT immobilize** the limb by default — weight-bearing as tolerated; immobilization is not required for uncomplicated AHO.
