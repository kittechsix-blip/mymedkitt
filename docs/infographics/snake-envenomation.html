Snake Envenomation (North American): Grade, Antivenom, Disposition

Adults and children in the ED with a suspected or confirmed North American snake bite (pit viper — rattlesnake/copperhead/cottonmouth — or coral snake). Covers grading, antivenom selection/dosing, allergic-reaction management, pediatric/pregnancy/head-neck special populations, and disposition. Excludes exotic/non-native and marine envenomations, and pre-hospital field first aid beyond the immediate-actions list.

⚠ Do NOT apply ice, tourniquets, or incise/suction the bite ("cut and suck"); do NOT give NSAIDs (bleeding risk).
⚠ Severe envenomation: treat coagulopathy with ANTIVENOM first, not blood products — FFP/platelets/cryoprecipitate are for active significant bleeding only and only temporarily correct coagulopathy without addressing the underlying venom effect.
⚠ No maximum antivenom dose — for inadequate response or persistent coagulopathy without active bleeding, give MORE antivenom, not blood products.
⚠ Head/neck/face bites: consider early intubation for ANY airway concern — do NOT wait for respiratory distress; progressive edema can make later intubation impossible.
⚠ Do NOT withhold antivenom in pregnancy — untreated envenomation poses greater fetal risk than antivenom; standard dosing applies.
⚠ Coral snake envenomation: do NOT wait for neurotoxicity symptoms to develop — once present, the presynaptic neurotoxin's effects may be irreversible. All suspected coral snake bites require minimum 24-hour ICU observation regardless of symptoms.
⚠ North American Coral Snake Antivenin (NACSA): do NOT give empirically before symptoms — give only at first sign of neurotoxicity (ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, limb weakness, respiratory depression).
⚠ Antivenom allergic reaction: STOP the infusion and give epinephrine IM immediately (preferred over SC) — antivenom benefit usually outweighs reaction risk, so restart at a slower rate with premedication once stable rather than abandoning treatment.

1 · Initial assessment

North American venomous snakes: Pit vipers (Crotalinae) cause 99% of US venomous bites — rattlesnakes (50-55%), copperheads (30-45%), cottonmouths/water moccasins. Coral snakes (Elapidae) cause ~1%. Pit vipers cause significant LOCAL effects (pain, swelling); coral snakes cause minimal local effects but DELAYED neurotoxicity. of pit viper bites are "dry" — no venom injected.[1][2]

  1. Remove jewelry / constrictive items immediately
  2. Immobilize the extremity at heart level
  3. Mark the leading edge of swelling with a time stamp
  4. Large-bore IV access
  5. Baseline labs: CBC, PT/INR, PTT, fibrinogen, BMP, CK, type & screen
Do NOT
Apply ice or cold water. Use a tourniquet or constrictive bands. Incise and suction ("cut and suck"). Give NSAIDs (bleeding risk).

6 · Special populations

Pediatric
Same antivenom dose as adults — dosed on venom amount, NOT weight. Use smaller dilution volume (100-150 mL) for infants to avoid fluid overload. Do NOT withhold for theoretical CroFab thimerosal concern.
Pregnant
Do NOT withhold antivenom — untreated envenomation poses greater fetal risk. Continuous fetal monitoring, OB consultation, monitor for placental abruption. Higher threshold for discharge; OB follow-up mandatory.
Head / neck bite
Early airway assessment — do NOT wait for stridor or respiratory distress. Prepare for a difficult airway; have a surgical airway available. Aggressive antivenom (8-12 vials CroFab). ICU admission.
