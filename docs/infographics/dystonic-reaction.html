Acute Dystonic Reaction: Recognize, Treat, Prevent Recurrence

Adults and children in the ED with sudden focal or generalized muscle spasm after a dopamine-blocking drug (antipsychotic or antiemetic). Excludes chronic tardive dyskinesia/akathisia and Parkinson disease.

⚠ Stridor, dysphonia, dyspnea, or throat tightness = possible laryngeal dystonia: treat now and prepare the airway (equipment, suction, BVM, RSI backup at bedside) — do not call this anxiety until it improves after anticholinergic therapy and the airway is clearly safe.
⚠ Classic story (recent dopamine blocker, sudden sustained/repetitive spasm, clear sensorium) = treat now rather than waiting for labs: diphenhydramine 50 mg IV/IM OR benztropine 1-2 mg IV/IM.
⚠ No meaningful improvement after adequate anticholinergic therapy means acute dystonia is LESS likely — reset the differential immediately for NMS, serotonin syndrome, seizure, angioedema/anaphylaxis, tetanus, hypocalcemia/hypomagnesemia, or CNS infection.
⚠ Do NOT give more antipsychotic/dopamine blocker for nausea or agitation while treating the reaction, and do NOT give more when the diagnosis may be NMS.
⚠ If anaphylaxis is possible (true swelling, urticaria, wheeze, hypotension): give epinephrine first — diphenhydramine does not treat shock or bronchospasm.
⚠ ICU or an airway-capable monitored setting is required for persistent stridor/hypoxia, recurrent laryngeal dystonia, or concern for a dangerous mimic (NMS, serotonin syndrome, tetanus, status epilepticus, CNS infection).

1 · Recognition

Core pattern: sudden painful sustained or intermittent muscle contraction after a dopamine-blocking medication. Typical timing: minutes to hours after an ED dose, or within the first 4-5 days after starting/increasing an antipsychotic or antiemetic. Typical clues: clear sensorium, anxiety/fear, normal temperature, focal craniocervical spasm, recent haloperidol/droperidol/metoclopramide/prochlorperazine/promethazine/risperidone exposure. First move: screen for airway involvement before debating the exact label.

High-confidence diagnosis when ALL are true: sudden sustained/repetitive spasm or abnormal posture; recent dopamine-blocking drug exposure or dose increase; onset within minutes-hours or within 4-5 days; mental status otherwise clear; no fever, clonus, meningismus, true loss of consciousness, or focal CNS deficit. Treatment is both diagnostic and therapeutic — if the story fits, treat now rather than waiting for labs.

2 · What to do now

Airway symptoms — treat now, prepare airway
Move to resus bay; airway equipment, suction, BVM, RSI backup at bedside. Stop the suspected culprit. Give diphenhydramine 50 mg IV/IM OR benztropine 1-2 mg IV/IM now. Oxygen as needed; prepare anesthesia/ENT if stridor or hypoxia persists.
Classic dystonia — treat now
Stop the offending drug. Adults: diphenhydramine 50 mg IV (preferred) or benztropine 1-2 mg IV/IM. Peds: diphenhydramine 1 mg/kg IV/IM, max 50 mg. Expect visible improvement within 5-30 min.
Atypical — work the differential before treating
Fever, AMS, generalized rigidity, loss of consciousness, true swelling, or no clear dopamine-blocker story: pick the dangerous mimic before anchoring on dystonia.
Fever / AMS / rigidity — NMS or serotonin syndrome
Clear focal spasm favors dystonia; fever + altered mental status + lead-pipe rigidity or clonus favors NMS/serotonin syndrome. Do NOT give more antipsychotic when NMS is possible.
LOC / rhythmic convulsion — seizure mimic
Oculogyric crisis can look like seizure, but awareness is usually preserved. LOC, rhythmic clonic movements, tongue bite, incontinence, or postictal state favor seizure. Check glucose now.
True swelling / wheeze / hypotension — anaphylaxis first
Buccolingual dystonia often feels like tongue swelling but is not truly edematous. If anaphylaxis is possible, give epinephrine first — diphenhydramine does not treat shock or bronchospasm.
Tetanic spasm / electrolyte risk
Evaluate for tetanus (trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonos, wound/immunization gap), hypocalcemia/hypomagnesemia (perioral numbness, tetany, QT prolongation), or strychnine-like toxicity when there is no dopamine-blocker story.
Partial response — repeat / add benzodiazepine
Recheck airway, voice, swallowing, oxygenation. Repeat the initial anticholinergic once if safe. Add a benzodiazepine if severe spasm, panic, or residual painful contraction. Observe longer — recurrence is more likely.
No response — reset the differential
If adequate anticholinergic therapy does not help, acute dystonia is less likely. Re-check for seizure/nonconvulsive status, tetanus, hypocalcemia/hypomagnesemia, serotonin syndrome/NMS, CNS infection, or true angioedema/anaphylaxis.
Response confirmed — prevent recurrence
Continue oral anticholinergic 24-72 h because the culprit drug often outlasts IV/IM diphenhydramine or benztropine: diphenhydramine 25-50 mg PO q6h OR benztropine 1-2 mg PO BID for 2-3 days.
Discharge criteria
Symptoms resolved, voice/swallowing/breathing normal, vitals stable, no recurrence during observation, oral anticholinergic plan in place, offending drug stopped or avoidance plan documented.
Observe / admit criteria
Laryngeal symptoms even if improved, repeated IV/IM anticholinergic or benzodiazepine needed, long-acting/depot antipsychotic exposure, recurrent symptoms, persistent dysphagia, significant sedation, or diagnostic uncertainty. Typical monitoring: 12-24 h.
ICU / airway-capable setting
Persistent stridor, hypoxia, or respiratory distress; recurrent laryngeal dystonia; severe generalized spasms with hyperthermia/acidosis/rhabdo; concern for NMS, serotonin syndrome, tetanus, or status epilepticus.

No routine test confirms acute dystonia — diagnosis is clinical, often confirmed by rapid response to anticholinergic therapy. No labs needed for a classic, medication-linked, rapidly resolving reaction. Target tests when atypical: glucose (seizure/AMS); BMP + calcium + magnesium (tetany/spasm); CK/creatinine/UA if prolonged severe spasms or restraint/agitation; temperature + infectious workup if fever/meningismus/sepsis concern; ECG/QTc if antipsychotic/antiemetic exposure or electrolyte concern; CT/LP/EEG only for focal neuro deficit, persistent AMS, seizure, or meningitis/encephalitis concern. Other pitfalls3 items
Do NOT anchor on anxiety when airway symptoms are present — treat and prepare the airway first; reclassify only after the airway is clearly safe.
Do NOT let workup delay treatment when the story is classic or airway symptoms are present — treatment is both diagnostic and therapeutic.
Do NOT discharge without an oral anticholinergic plan — the culprit drug commonly outlasts IV/IM diphenhydramine or benztropine, and recurrence can happen after ED discharge.

8 · Disposition and documentation

Document as a medication adverse reaction, not a vague allergy: offending drug, dose, route, time; phenotype (oculogyric/torticollis/jaw/tongue/laryngeal/etc.); treatment response and time to response; discharge anticholinergic plan. Avoid the same medication and close dopamine-blocking relatives when reasonable. Highest-risk patient: prior dystonic reaction plus re-exposure.

Reasonable discharge when ALL are true: symptoms resolved/near-resolved after anticholinergic; voice, swallowing, breathing normal; vitals stable, no fever/AMS/focal deficit; no recurrent spasm during ED observation; oral anticholinergic plan in place; offending drug stopped/avoided or psychiatry/pharmacy plan documented; return precautions given.

Observe/admit for: laryngeal symptoms even if improved; need for repeated IV/IM anticholinergic or benzodiazepine; long-acting/depot antipsychotic exposure; recurrent ED symptoms; persistent dysphagia/jaw/tongue spasm or aspiration concern; significant sedation or anticholinergic toxicity; diagnostic uncertainty; unsafe discharge. Typical monitoring: 12-24 h.

ICU/airway-capable setting when: persistent stridor, hypoxia, or respiratory distress; recurrent laryngeal dystonia; need for airway intervention or continuous airway watch; severe generalized spasms with hyperthermia/acidosis/rhabdomyolysis; concern for NMS, serotonin syndrome, tetanus, strychnine-like toxicity, CNS infection, or status epilepticus.

ICU/airway-capable setting when: persistent stridor, hypoxia, or respiratory distress; recurrent laryngeal dystonia; need for airway intervention or continuous airway watch; severe generalized spasms with hyperthermia/acidosis/rhabdomyolysis; concern for NMS, serotonin syndrome, tetanus, strychnine-like toxicity, CNS infection, or status epilepticus.
