Ear Foreign Body Removal — ED Management

ED patients presenting with an external auditory canal (EAC) foreign body. Covers triage for emergent findings (button battery, live insect with distress, suspected TM perforation), object-type classification, technique selection by object type, the removal attempt itself, complication management, and disposition. Not a substitute for ENT evaluation when removal fails, TM injury is suspected, or the object is pushed deeper.

Overview

External auditory canal (EAC) foreign bodies are common ED presentations, especially in children. The first removal attempt is the most successful — 65-78% — so plan technique before starting. Children most often present with beads, toys, food, insects, or paper; adults with cotton swabs, hearing-aid parts, or insects.

  • Adequate visualization (otoscope, largest speculum that fits)
  • Cooperative patient (or appropriate restraint/sedation)
  • Correct technique matched to object type
  • Limit to 1-2 attempts before ENT referral

Triage — emergent findings?

Screen for a button battery, a distressed live insect, or suspected TM perforation before attempting removal. Any of these routes away from routine extraction.

Button battery — EMERGENCY

Button battery causes liquefaction necrosis in 1-2 hours
Do NOT irrigate — water accelerates the reaction. Keep the ear dry and obtain emergent ENT consultation. If ENT is unavailable, attempt gentle removal with alligator forceps or a magnetic tool. Do not delay for imaging if the diagnosis is clear.
  • Electrical current generation between poles
  • Hydroxide production → tissue necrosis
  • Can erode through the EAC within hours
  • Complications: canal stenosis, TM perforation, ossicular damage, facial nerve injury
Disposition
ENT follow-up within 24 hours even after successful removal.

Live insect — kill before extraction

Kill the insect first
Fill the canal with mineral oil drops — the preferred agent. The insect dies quietly (no thrashing), reducing distress and further injury. Wait 1-2 minutes before extraction.

Suspected TM perforation

Do NOT attempt removal in the ED
Refer to ENT. Signs: vertigo/nystagmus, sudden hearing loss, bleeding from the canal, severe pain at time of insertion, object appears to be touching/impacting the TM, or poor visualization of depth.

If ototoxic drops are needed: avoid aminoglycosides (neomycin, tobramycin) and polymyxin B. Ofloxacin or ciprofloxacin/dexamethasone are safe.

Assessment before removal

  • Otoscope with the largest speculum that fits, adequate lighting
  • Pull pinna up and back (adults) or down and back (children)
  • Document object type, size, location, and TM status
  • Contraindication check: TM perforation, object touching/impacting TM, sharp object adjacent to TM, multiple prior failed attempts, uncooperative patient without a sedation option

Position supine or sitting with head turned; consider a papoose board for young children; procedural sedation (ketamine preferred) if needed; ensure suction and backup equipment are ready.

Insect removal technique

  1. Kill the insect first (mineral oil, if alive)
  2. Irrigation (after killing) — insect may float out with gentle, body-temp, low-pressure water
  3. Alligator forceps — grasp leg, wing, or body segment; slow gentle withdrawal; may need to remove in pieces
  4. Suction (soft-tipped catheter) — good for fragmented insects
  5. Right-angle hook — if body intact, scoop gently behind and rotate to position behind the FB

Have suction ready for fragments; expect partial removal — may need multiple passes.

Hard sphere / bead removal

Right-angle hook or curette — avoid forceps
Advance the hook/curette behind the object, rotate to position posterior to the FB, apply gentle posterior-to-anterior traction, and scoop slowly. Alligator forceps risk slippage that pushes round objects deeper — avoid.

Suction may work for small (<3 mm), smooth, loose objects. Cyanoacrylate (glue) on a cotton-swab tip, held steady 60 seconds, is an option for stuck objects but has a lower success rate (~19%).

Soft object removal

Alligator forceps — NEVER irrigate
Cotton, foam, paper, and sponge are best grasped with alligator forceps using gentle traction — avoid crushing. Suction (soft-tipped catheter) is an alternative for loose, fluffy material. Irrigation is contraindicated: spongy materials absorb water and swell, converting an easy removal into an impaction.

Organic material removal

Dry extraction only — NEVER irrigate
Seeds, beans, food, and plant material absorb water and swell — beans can swell 2-3x their original size if wet. Use alligator forceps (grasp firmly, avoid crushing) or a right-angle hook behind the object. Suction is useful for fragments.

Popcorn kernels may fragment; seeds may have sharp edges. Organic FBs in place >24 hours may already have significant swelling.

Small particle removal

  1. 20-50 mL syringe with a 14-16 gauge plastic catheter
  2. Body-temperature water or saline (cold water causes vertigo)
  3. Patient head tilted, affected ear up; direct stream along the superior canal wall
  4. Low pressure — avoid a Waterpik (too forceful); multiple passes may be needed
  5. 65% first-pass success rate
Contraindications to irrigation
TM perforation (known or suspected), soft/organic material (will swell), button battery (water accelerates damage), prior ear surgery with tympanostomy tubes.

Complex / irregular objects

Assess whether the object contains a battery (treat as button-battery emergency if so), whether it is soft or hard, whether you can grasp an edge, and how deep it is. Hearing-aid components are usually plastic (alligator forceps) but may be custom-molded. Earbud tips are soft silicone (forceps; may tear). Irregular hard objects: forceps if there is a graspable edge, hook/curette to get behind it, cyanoacrylate for flat smooth surfaces.

Low threshold for ENT referral
Complex objects often need an operative microscope if unable to remove in the ED.

Removal attempt

Limit to 1-2 attempts
Each subsequent attempt has lower success due to edema, bleeding, and patient distress. Before each attempt: confirm visualization, confirm patient cooperation/sedation, have backup instruments ready, and know your bail-out plan.

Post-removal care

  • Inspect canal for laceration or abrasion
  • Visualize TM — document integrity
  • Test hearing (finger rub or whisper test)

Keep the ear dry for 24-48 hours. Otic antibiotic drops (ofloxacin or ciprodex) if canal trauma is present. Pain control: acetaminophen or ibuprofen PRN. ENT referral if canal laceration, TM concern, or hearing change; not routinely needed if uncomplicated. Counsel against inserting objects (including cotton swabs) into the canal — ear candles are ineffective and dangerous.

Partial removal

Some material was removed but fragments remain — assess whether the residual is minimal (may self-clear), significant (needs further removal), or whether another pass is reasonable given good visualization and patient tolerance.

Observation for residual fragments

Minimal residual material may clear spontaneously. Document the residual material, prescribe otic antibiotic drops x 5-7 days, keep the ear dry, and arrange ENT follow-up in 1-2 weeks (may need cerumen removal to assess). Most small organic fragments exfoliate with cerumen over 1-2 weeks.

Failed removal

Stop — do NOT continue
Each attempt causes more edema and bleeding; patient cooperation deteriorates; risk of pushing the object deeper or injuring the TM/canal increases. First-attempt success is 65-78%; after 2+ failed attempts it drops below 20%.

75% of EAC foreign bodies are removable in the ED; 23% require operative removal under microscopy.

Complication assessment

Identify the complication type: canal laceration/bleeding, suspected TM perforation, object pushed deeper, or patient unable to tolerate the procedure.

Canal laceration management

Apply gentle pressure with a cotton ball; assess depth/extent; visualize TM if possible. Minor laceration: otic antibiotic drops (ofloxacin or ciprodex) x 7 days, keep ear dry x 1 week, pain control PRN, ENT follow-up if not healing at 1 week. Significant laceration: stop the removal attempt, refer to ENT, continue antibiotic drops until seen. If the FB is still present, refer to ENT — do not continue attempts through active bleeding.

Suspected TM perforation (intra-procedure)

Stop removal immediately
Document symptoms (vertigo, tinnitus, hearing loss) and note time of suspected injury. Keep the ear dry — no drops unless ENT-directed; no ototoxic drops (aminoglycosides, polymyxin); if drops are needed, ofloxacin or ciprofloxacin/dexamethasone only. Urgent ENT referral within 24-48 hours; audiometry will be needed. Small perforations often heal spontaneously; large ones may need tympanoplasty. Do NOT continue attempts — ENT will remove under controlled conditions.

Object pushed deeper

STOP — do not attempt further removal
The FB is now in the medial canal or against the TM. Continued attempts risk TM perforation, ossicular chain injury, hearing loss, and entry into the middle ear. Document the new position, keep the ear dry, and refer urgently to ENT, who will use an operative microscope and controlled environment (possible tympanomeatal flap approach). The deeper the object, the higher the complication risk.

Patient intolerance

Options: procedural sedation (ketamine preferred — maintains airway reflexes; consider if the object is easily accessible), general anesthesia via ENT referral (pediatric patients, anxious/uncooperative adults, complex/deep objects, or failed sedation), or delay and retry in select stable, non-urgent cases (not appropriate for batteries or sharp objects).

Pediatric considerations: a papoose board alone is often insufficient, parental assistance has limited value, sedation is preferred if more than one attempt is needed, and there should be a low threshold for ENT referral.

Disposition

Determine discharge with or without ENT follow-up based on whether canal trauma or other complications occurred.

Discharge — uncomplicated

Keep the ear dry for 24-48 hours; no swimming or submerging the ear; avoid inserting anything (including Q-tips) into the canal. No routine antibiotic drops needed if the canal is intact. Return for increasing pain, drainage (pus/blood), hearing change, dizziness/ringing, or fever. No routine follow-up needed; see PCP at 1 week if concerns. Prevention: no objects in the ear, Q-tips for the outer ear only, hearing protection when appropriate.

Discharge with ENT follow-up

Indications: canal laceration or significant trauma, concern for residual material, hearing complaint post-procedure, or abnormal TM appearance. Otic antibiotic drops (ofloxacin 0.3% or ciprofloxacin/dexamethasone) BID x 7 days plus pain control PRN. Keep ear dry; no swimming until cleared by ENT; return for worsening symptoms. ENT appointment within 1-2 weeks, sooner if symptoms worsen.
