Psych Medical Stability: Clinical Assessment Drives Testing, Not Routine Labs

Adults presenting to the ED with acute psychiatric symptoms who need a medical assessment before psychiatric evaluation, admission, or discharge. Excludes pediatric patients and involuntary-hold legal/commitment criteria — this pathway covers medical stability only, not psychiatric commitment law.

⚠ Red flags = organic cause until proven otherwise. Historical: Age >40 with first psychiatric symptoms; acute onset (hours-days vs weeks-months); no prior psychiatric history; recent head trauma; known medical illness (cancer, HIV, thyroid); new medications or recent changes. Clinical: visual hallucinations (vs auditory); fluctuating consciousness; disorientation (time, place, person); abnormal vital signs; focal neurologic findings; signs of intoxication/withdrawal.
⚠ Check vitals — including fingerstick glucose, O2 saturation, and temperature — on ALL psychiatric patients. Any abnormal vital makes an organic cause more likely: HR >120 or <50; SBP >180 or <90; RR >25 or <10; Temp >38.5°C or <35°C; O2 sat <94%.
⚠ Delirium = medical emergency. CAM positive (Feature 1 + Feature 2 + (Feature 3 OR 4)) mandates a full organic workup for the underlying cause using I WATCH DEATH: infection, withdrawal, acute metabolic derangement, trauma, CNS pathology, hypoxia, deficiencies, endocrine, acute vascular, toxins/drugs, heavy metals.
⚠ Do not clear a patient for psychiatry while an active medical problem is untreated — delirium, infection, metabolic derangement, intoxication/withdrawal, or acute organ dysfunction requires medical admission first; psychiatry evaluates once medically stabilized.
⚠ Consider anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis in a young patient with a first psychotic break plus prodrome, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability, or decreased consciousness — do not anchor solely on a psychiatric diagnosis.

2 · Psychiatric history

Does the patient have a known psychiatric history with a SIMILAR presentation? Known + similar routes to a focused re-check. First episode/different pattern, or an unobtainable history, routes directly to the red-flag screen.[1]

With a known psychiatric history: normal vitals, normal exam, and no red flags → low-risk pathway. Any concerning feature (abnormal vitals, new symptoms, a red flag) → red-flag screen.

4 · Delirium vs psychosis — the key distinction

FeatureDeliriumPsychosis
OnsetAcute (hours-days)Gradual (weeks-months)
CourseFluctuatingStable
AttentionIMPAIREDIntact
OrientationDisorientedOften oriented
HallucinationsVisualAuditory
VitalsOften abnormalUsually normal
Sleep-wakeDisruptedVariable

Confusion Assessment Method (CAM): Feature 1 acute onset AND fluctuating course; Feature 2 inattention (cannot focus on interview); Feature 3 disorganized thinking (rambling, illogical); Feature 4 altered level of consciousness (not alert). DELIRIUM = Feature 1 + Feature 2 + (Feature 3 OR 4). Inattention test: serial 7s, spell WORLD backwards, or months backwards — cannot perform = delirium.[3]

5 · What to do now — workup by scenario

Low risk — known, stable
Known psychiatric history + similar presentation + normal assessment. Required: fingerstick glucose; pregnancy test if applicable. Not required: routine CBC/BMP, toxicology screen (unless clinically indicated), imaging. Routine testing in this population rarely changes management and prolongs ED boarding.
Moderate risk — no red flags, but first episode or unknown history
Reasonable workup: fingerstick glucose, CBC, BMP, toxicology screen, UA (especially elderly), TSH. Clinical judgment drives additional testing — an intermediate approach, more than minimal but not as extensive as a delirium workup.
Delirium — medical emergency
DELIRIUM = medical emergency. Find the cause with I WATCH DEATH: Infection, Withdrawal, Acute metabolic, Trauma, CNS pathology, Hypoxia, Deficiencies, Endocrine, Acute vascular, Toxins/drugs, Heavy metals. Standard panel — always: CBC with differential, BMP (Na+, K+, glucose, BUN, Cr), urinalysis, toxicology screen. Consider: LFTs, ammonia, TSH, lactate, troponin, B12/folate, blood cultures, VBG/ABG. Imaging: CT head if trauma, focal findings, anticoagulation, or new-onset; CXR if hypoxia, cough, or fever. LP if meningitis concern.
Primary psychosis (CAM negative) — still screen for organic mimics
Still consider: anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis (young women, ovarian teratoma), stimulant intoxication (meth, cocaine), medication toxicity (steroids, anticholinergics), thyroid storm, autoimmune encephalitis. First psychotic break workup: vitals, glucose, CBC, BMP; TSH when thyroid symptoms, mood syndrome, or unclear cause; toxicology screen when substance-induced symptoms are plausible; UA if urinary symptoms, pregnancy concern, elderly, or delirium concern; consider HIV, syphilis (RPR), B12 based on risk factors. Known psychosis, similar presentation → labs often unnecessary.
First psychotic episode
Labs: CBC, BMP, glucose; TSH when clinically indicated; toxicology screen when substance-induced symptoms are plausible; UA if urinary symptoms, pregnancy concern, elderly, or delirium concern; RPR/HIV/B12/folate based on risk factors or atypical features. Imaging: ED CT for focal deficits, headache/seizure, trauma, delirium features, anticoagulation/immunosuppression, or age >40; MRI is preferred for non-emergent structural evaluation when stable. Consider: EEG if seizure concern, LP if encephalitis concern, anti-NMDA antibodies when a young patient has prodrome, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability, or decreased consciousness.
Known psychosis, typical presentation
Routine labs are NOT required. Check fingerstick glucose; check pregnancy if applicable; address specific concerns only. Studies show routine labs rarely change management and reduce ED length of stay.
Atypical presentation or unclear etiology — full workup
Full panel: CBC, BMP, LFTs, ammonia, TSH, toxicology screen, UA with culture, lactate, VBG/ABG. Imaging: CT head (or MRI if available), CXR. Consider: LP if encephalitis/meningitis concern, EEG if seizure concern, HIV, RPR, B12.

6 · Disposition

Disposition follows from the workup: medical admission (active medical problem), psychiatry once medically stable (no active medical issue, psych evaluation needed), or discharge (stable, safe, outpatient follow-up).

Medical admission
Active medical problem identified: delirium requiring treatment, infection (UTI, pneumonia, sepsis), metabolic derangement, intoxication/withdrawal requiring monitoring, or acute organ dysfunction. Admit to medicine for treatment of the underlying medical condition; continue psychiatric assessment in parallel if safe to do so — psychiatry can evaluate once medically stabilized.
  • Vital signs reviewed and stable
  • Physical exam performed
  • Mental status exam documented
  • Labs reviewed (if obtained)
  • No acute medical condition requiring admission
  • Patient stable for psychiatric evaluation

Communicate to psychiatry: pertinent medical findings, medications given in the ED, labs and imaging results, and ongoing medical issues (stable). Patient is medically stable for psychiatric evaluation/admission.[1]

Discharge
Criteria: no acute medical condition, no imminent safety risk, stable psychiatric condition, safe disposition (home, with family), follow-up arranged. Provide: outpatient psychiatry follow-up, crisis resources (988 Lifeline), return precautions.
