1 · First question: monocular vs binocular

Diplopia represents ~50,000 US ED visits annually. 64% have a primary (microvascular) cause; 36% have a secondary cause (stroke 45%, MS 18%, tumor 12%, aneurysm 7.5% of that secondary group).[1][2]

The first question
Ask: does the double vision go away when you cover EITHER eye? Binocular (resolves when either eye is covered) = ocular misalignment — needs the neurologic workup below. Monocular (persists with one eye covered) = ocular pathology, generally non-emergent.[1]

Lens subluxationRecent trauma, Marfan syndrome — may need urgent ophtho

Monocular disposition
Not a neurologic emergency. Outpatient ophthalmology referral. Return if binocular symptoms develop or new neurological symptoms appear.[1]

2 · Localize the nerve

Horizontal diplopia suggests CN III or CN VI. Vertical diplopia suggests CN III or CN IV. Oblique/diagonal may be CN III, IV, or multiple nerves. Variable/fluctuating — think myasthenia gravis.[1][3]

  1. Pupil: size, reactivity, afferent pupillary defect.
  2. Extraocular movements (H-pattern): note which direction worsens diplopia; look for nystagmus.
  3. Eyelids: ptosis (CN III, MG, Horner), lid lag (thyroid), fatigable ptosis (MG).
  4. Cover-uncover test: movement to take up fixation = tropia.
  5. Associated findings: proptosis, V1/V2 sensory loss, Horner syndrome.
  6. Fundoscopy: papilledema = elevated ICP.

3 · CN III (oculomotor) — the emergent one

CN III controls the superior/inferior/medial recti, inferior oblique, levator palpebrae, and pupillary constriction. Complete palsy: eye "down and out", complete ptosis, +/- dilated pupil, loss of accommodation.[4][6]

The pupil rule is NOT reliable enough to skip imaging
Pupillomotor fibers run on the dorsomedial surface of CN III — external compression (aneurysm) hits these fibers first; ischemia (diabetes) spares them. But up to 20% of ischemic CN III palsies involve the pupil, and up to 36% of compressive lesions are initially pupil-sparing; pupil involvement may be delayed 5-7 days in an evolving aneurysm. ALL acute CN III palsies get an emergent CTA head regardless of pupil status.[3][4][6]

4 · CN IV (trochlear)

Longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve; the only CN that exits dorsally and the only one that crosses to supply the contralateral muscle — most vulnerable to trauma. Vertical diplopia with a compensatory head tilt to the opposite shoulder. Common causes: trauma (most common acquired), microvascular, congenital (decompensated childhood palsy), rarely mass lesion.[5][7]

  1. Which eye is higher (hypertropic)?
  2. Does hypertropia worsen in right or left gaze?
  3. Bielschowsky head-tilt test: hypertropia worse tilting toward the higher eye = superior oblique palsy of that eye.

5 · CN VI (abducens) — the false localizing sign

Abduction only. Esotropia with a face turn toward the affected side. CN VI has the longest intracranial course, tethered at Dorello's canal — it can be stretched by downward brainstem displacement, making it a false localizing sign of elevated ICP.[3][8]

Always check fundoscopy in isolated CN VI palsy
CN VI is a false localizing sign in ~33% of IIH patients — papilledema changes the whole workup. ALL children with CN VI palsy need emergent MRI (higher malignancy risk, posterior fossa tumors) — do not rely on a "microvascular" diagnosis in a child.[3][8]

6 · INO, multiple CN palsies, and myasthenia gravis

INO = medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) lesion coordinating conjugate horizontal gaze: impaired adduction of the ipsilateral eye with nystagmus of the abducting contralateral eye; convergence usually preserved. ~33% MS, ~33% stroke; INO present in ~23% of MS patients — young patients trend MS (often bilateral), older patients trend stroke (usually unilateral).[9][10]

INO workup — MRI, not CT
MRI with DWI is the gold standard; CT/CTA sensitivity for posterior circulation stroke is only 10-41%. Stroke suspected → full stroke workup, may benefit from thrombolysis if within window. MS suspected → full MRI brain and spine, LP for oligoclonal bands if diagnosis unclear. ~50% resolve within 1 year.[9][10]

Multiple CN palsies localize the lesion: cavernous sinus syndrome (CN III, IV, V1, V2, VI +/- Horner syndrome); orbital apex syndrome (same nerves plus CN II — vision loss differentiates it); superior orbital fissure syndrome (CN III, IV, V1, VI, no vision loss).[3][11]

Cavernous sinus thrombosis — can't miss
Fever (50-90%), headache (50-90%), periorbital swelling/chemosis, proptosis, multiple CN palsies, +/- Horner syndrome, can become bilateral. Septic causes: sinusitis, orbital cellulitis, dental infection. Aseptic: hypercoagulable state, OCP, pregnancy, malignancy. Workup: CT venography, MR venography if CTV negative, blood cultures, source identification. Anticoagulation is controversial but often used. Mortality: 20-30% even with treatment.[11]

9 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls6 items
Do NOT skip CTA in a pupil-sparing CN III palsy — up to 36% of compressive lesions are initially pupil-sparing. All acute CN III palsies get emergent CTA regardless of pupil status.
Do NOT let a normal ESR/CRP reassure you in a patient >50 with new diplopia — both are normal in only 4% of GCA. Start steroids before biopsy if vision is threatened.
Do NOT treat isolated CN VI palsy as benign without fundoscopy — it can be a false localizing sign of elevated ICP in ~33% of IIH patients.
Do NOT miss multiple cranial nerve palsies as isolated findings — think cavernous sinus or orbital apex pathology, and start empiric antibiotics if cavernous sinus thrombosis is septic.

Diplopia, Dysarthria, Dysphagia, Dysmetria, Dystaxia. Posterior strokes are missed 2-3x more often than anterior strokes.{{ref14}}

Diplopia is a presenting feature of GCA (LR+ 3.4) and can cause permanent vision loss (AAION) — treatable if caught early. Features: age >50 (mean 70), new headache (often temporal), scalp tenderness, jaw claudication (highly specific), polymyalgia rheumatica symptoms, constitutional symptoms. ESR typically >50 (often >100). CRP: Sensitivity 98.6%, Specificity 75.7%. Both normal: only 4% of GCA patients — a normal ESR/CRP does NOT exclude GCA.{{ref15}}

IV Methylprednisolone 500-1000 mg daily for 3 days, then oral prednisone taper. **OR** Prednisone 1 mg/kg/day PO if IV unavailable. Urgent temporal artery biopsy within 2 weeks; ophthalmology consult.{{ref15}}

Methylprednisolone (Solu-Medrol): 500-1000 mg IV daily x3 days, then oral prednisone taper IV — For GCA with threatened vision. Do NOT delay for biopsy.

Prednisone (if IV unavailable): 1 mg/kg/day PO PO — Alternative to IV methylprednisolone. Start immediately if vision threatened.

Admit — emergent pathology | Aneurysm, stroke, cavernous sinus thrombosis, mass, MG crisis, GCA with threatened vision, elevated ICP, multiple CN palsies with unknown etiology

Discharge — monocular diplopia | Ocular cause, outpatient ophthalmology

Discharge — likely microvascular | Age >50, vascular risk factors, isolated palsy, negative imaging

Discharge — needs close follow-up | Stable but requires urgent outpatient workup (neurology within 1 week)

8 · One-glance algorithm

Resolves when EITHER eye is covered? — Binocular disappears with either eye covered = ocular misalignment, needs neuro workup. Monocular persists with one eye covered = ocular pathology.

Monocular → ocular cause, not neurologic — Refractive error, cataract, corneal irregularity, lens dislocation, macular pathology. Pinhole test. Outpatient ophthalmology. Exception: recent trauma + monocular diplopia may need urgent ophtho (lens dislocation).

Binocular: what direction is the diplopia? — Horizontal = CN III or VI; vertical = CN III or IV; oblique = CN III/IV/multiple — all three proceed to the full EOM exam (pupil, H-pattern EOM, eyelid, cover-uncover, associated findings, fundoscopy). Variable/fluctuating skips straight to MG workup without further localization exam.

Which pattern fits on exam? — CN III = down-and-out + ptosis. CN IV = head tilt + vertical diplopia. CN VI = cannot abduct. Multiple CN = more than one nerve. INO = adduction deficit + contralateral nystagmus. Fluctuating/fatigable = suspect MG.

CN III: pupil rule unreliable → emergent CTA regardless of pupil status — 20% of ischemic CN III involves the pupil; 36% of compressive lesions are initially pupil-sparing; pupil involvement may be delayed 5-7 days. CTA sensitivity >95% for aneurysm >3mm (99.4-99.8% for <5mm).

INO → MRI with DWI, MS vs stroke — Impaired adduction of ipsilateral eye + nystagmus of abducting contralateral eye. ~33% MS, ~33% stroke, INO present in ~23% of MS patients. MRI with DWI gold standard (CT/CTA only 10-41% sensitive for posterior stroke).

Multiple CN palsies → cavernous sinus / orbital apex workup + empiric antibiotics — Cavernous sinus, orbital apex, or superior orbital fissure syndrome. CTV/MRV, blood cultures, source ID. Empiric: vancomycin + ceftriaxone + metronidazole if septic. Mortality 20-30% even treated.

Fluctuating/fatigable → MG bedside tests, screen for crisis — Ice pack test sensitivity 80%, specificity 100%. AChR antibodies, single-fiber EMG. Myasthenic crisis: FVC <1L or bulbar symptoms → ICU.

CN VI: check papilledema — may be a false localizing sign of elevated ICP — Abduction only; esotropia; longest intracranial course, tethered at Dorello's canal. False localizing sign in ~33% of IIH patients. ALL children need emergent MRI.

CN IV: 3-step test → workup by age/trauma → dispo — Compensatory head tilt to opposite shoulder. Parks-Bielschowsky 3-step test ~75% sensitive. Trauma→CT; age>50+vascular RF+isolated→observe; else MRI with contrast. Discharge if isolated + reliable follow-up.

CTA / imaging result? — Aneurysm found; CTA negative + pupil-sparing + age>50 + vascular RF; CTA negative but concerning; or other pathology found.

Evaluation reveals? — Papilledema present; child with CN VI palsy; adult, no papilledema, vascular RF; pathology found on imaging.

PCommA aneurysm → emergent neurosurgery — ICU, aneurysm precautions, NPO. Coiling or clipping. Untreated rupture: 50% mortality.

Microvascular CN III → observe + follow-up — Age>50, vascular RF, pupil-sparing, negative CTA. Improvement 2-3 months. Ophtho f/u 1-2 weeks.

CTA negative but concerning → further imaging — MRI/MRA + thin cuts through cavernous sinus; DSA if still negative and high suspicion. Admit if high concern.

Other pathology on imaging — Mass/tumor, stroke/infarct, cavernous sinus pathology, inflammatory, infectious.

Elevated ICP → CT/MRV/LP — CT head, MRV if negative, LP with opening pressure after imaging. IIH: opening pressure >25 cm H2O.

Child with CN VI palsy → emergent MRI — Posterior fossa tumors. Gradenigo syndrome: otitis media + petrous apicitis + V1 pain + CN VI palsy. Admit.

Microvascular CN VI → observe + follow-up — Age>50, vascular RF, isolated, no papilledema. Improvement 2-3 months. Ophtho f/u 2-4 weeks.

Pathology found → admit for ID/onc/neuro workup — Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, pontine infarct, cerebral venous thrombosis, sarcoid/Tolosa-Hunt, Gradenigo/mastoiditis, meningitis, Lyme disease.

9 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT skip CTA in a pupil-sparing CN III palsy** — up to 36% of compressive lesions are initially pupil-sparing. All acute CN III palsies get emergent CTA regardless of pupil status.

**Do NOT let a normal ESR/CRP reassure you** in a patient >50 with new diplopia — both are normal in only 4% of GCA. Start steroids before biopsy if vision is threatened.

**Do NOT treat isolated CN VI palsy as benign without fundoscopy** — it can be a false localizing sign of elevated ICP in ~33% of IIH patients.

**Do NOT miss multiple cranial nerve palsies as isolated findings** — think cavernous sinus or orbital apex pathology, and start empiric antibiotics if cavernous sinus thrombosis is septic.

**Do NOT rely on CT/CTA to exclude a posterior circulation stroke** in suspected INO — sensitivity is only 10-41%; MRI with DWI is the gold standard.

**Do NOT give aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, beta-blockers, magnesium, or botulinum toxin** to a patient with myasthenia gravis — check any new medication before prescribing.
