Pediatric STEC / HUS: Recognition to Disposition

Children with suspected or confirmed Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) infection or hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), ED and inpatient. Peak population <5 years old. Excludes adults, and excludes atypical (complement-mediated) HUS management beyond initial recognition — atypical HUS requires nephrology-directed eculizumab per its own protocol.

⚠ Do NOT give antibiotics for suspected or confirmed STEC — meta-analyses show antibiotics (especially quinolones and TMP-SMX) may INCREASE HUS risk via bacterial lysis and increased Shiga toxin release. Hold antibiotics until stool Shiga toxin results are known.
⚠ Do NOT give antimotility agents (loperamide, diphenoxylate) in bloody diarrhea — CONTRAINDICATED, associated with increased HUS risk, prolonged illness, and toxic megacolon.
⚠ CNS involvement occurs in 20-30% of HUS and is the leading cause of mortality (3-5% with CNS involvement) — seizures, altered mental status, or stroke require urgent neuroimaging, aggressive BP control, and ICU admission.
⚠ Avoid platelet transfusion in typical HUS unless active life-threatening bleeding, a planned invasive procedure with platelets <50,000, or CNS hemorrhage — platelets may worsen microvascular thrombosis.
⚠ Eculizumab is FDA-approved for atypical HUS, NOT standard of care for typical STEC-HUS — reserve for severe CNS involvement, rapidly progressive multiorgan failure, or atypical/uncertain features, only after nephrology/hematology coordination and after sending complement studies + ADAMTS13.

1 · Recognition

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) is a leading cause of acute kidney injury in children. 5-15% of STEC cases progress to HUS, peaking at age <5 years. HUS triad: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) + thrombocytopenia (<150,000/µL) + acute kidney injury. Typical onset Days 5-10 post-diarrhea.

  • Age <5 years
  • Bloody diarrhea
  • Fever
  • Elevated WBC (>13,000)
  • Antibiotic or antimotility agent use
Clinical presentation (prodrome → HUS phase → severe features)Watery → bloody diarrhea, then pallor/petechiae/oliguria; poor-prognosis features

Prodromal phase (STEC colitis): watery diarrhea → bloody diarrhea (60-90%), severe abdominal cramping, nausea/vomiting, low-grade or no fever. Duration 3-8 days.

HUS phase: pallor and fatigue (anemia), petechiae/bruising (thrombocytopenia), decreased urine output (AKI), edema, hypertension, irritability, lethargy.

  • Anuria >24 hours
  • WBC >20,000
  • Hematocrit <23%
  • Need for RBC transfusion within 4 days
  • CNS involvement (seizures, altered mental status)
  • Bowel necrosis/perforation
Atypical presentation
HUS can occur without preceding diarrhea in ~10% of cases.

Incubation period: 2-10 days (median 3-4 days). STEC is reportable to public health; childcare exclusion until 2 negative stool cultures ≥24h apart.

▶ What to do now

Bloody diarrhea, no HUS features
Send stool Shiga toxin EIA (do not rely on culture alone — misses 50% of non-O157 strains) plus baseline CBC/BMP/LDH/retic/haptoglobin. Begin daily HUS-monitoring labs through the day 5-10 risk window. No antibiotics, no antimotility agents.
HUS triad present
MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI confirmed — assess severity (platelets/creatinine/urine output), begin aggressive euvolemic fluids if pre-anuric, avoid platelet transfusion, and escalate per severity band below.
Evolving / early HUS
Confirm the triad (Hgb <10 with schistocytes, platelets <150,000, rising creatinine). Send coagulation studies, complement (C3/C4/AH50/CH50), and ADAMTS13 to distinguish typical STEC-HUS from atypical HUS — atypical requires urgent nephrology and eculizumab.

4 · STEC colitis management

Goal urine output 1-2 mL/kg/hr; D5NS or D5LR at 1.5x maintenance. NPO initially if severe vomiting, advance to clear liquids → bland diet; avoid lactose acutely.

Analgesia caveats
Acetaminophen for cramping. Avoid NSAIDs (renal risk, bleeding risk) and avoid opioids if possible (ileus risk). Ondansetron PRN nausea; avoid promethazine (confuses mental-status assessment).
Antibiotics — controversial, generally avoided
2024 AAP/IDSA position: do NOT routinely give antibiotics for suspected STEC. Hold until Shiga toxin results known; if already started and STEC confirmed, consider stopping. May be considered only for septic-appearing or immunocompromised patients, or a confirmed non-STEC bacterial pathogen. Antimotility agents are contraindicated in bloody diarrhea.
  • IV fluids and close monitoring
  • Daily CBC, BMP, LDH, reticulocyte count
  • Strict I/O with urine output tracking, serial weights
  • Contact precautions; notify MD immediately if UOP <0.5 mL/kg/hr, new pallor, new petechiae/bruising, AMS, or seizure
  • Minimum 5-7 day length of stay (through HUS risk window)
  • Consults to consider: pediatric nephrology, pediatric GI, infectious disease (atypical cases)
  • Tolerating adequate oral fluids
  • Normal or stable hemoglobin, normal platelets, normal creatinine, normal urine output
  • Reliable family with transportation
  • Labs (CBC, BMP) in 24-48 hours, daily phone check-in
  • Return immediately for decreased urination, blood in urine, increased pallor, confusion, seizure, or worsening abdominal pain
  • Monitor through Day 10-14 post-diarrhea onset; report confirmed STEC to public health

2 · Management algorithm

STEC/HUS Overview — Peak age <5yr; 5-15% progress to HUS; onset days 5-10 post-diarrhea.

Current Presentation? — Bloody diarrhea only vs established HUS triad vs evolving/early HUS.

STEC Diagnostic Workup — Shiga toxin EIA is the most important test — order on all bloody diarrhea in children.

Evolving HUS Workup — Confirm triad; distinguish typical (STEC-HUS) vs atypical (complement-mediated) HUS.

HUS Monitoring Protocol — Daily CBC/BMP/LDH/retic days 5-10; admit if any lab abnormality or age <2.

STEC Colitis Management — Aggressive IV fluids, goal UOP 1-2 mL/kg/hr; acetaminophen for pain; avoid NSAIDs/opioids.

No Antibiotics / No Antimotility — Antibiotics may increase HUS risk via toxin release; antimotility agents contraindicated in bloody diarrhea.

STEC Disposition? — Admit if high-risk/lab abnormality/poor PO tolerance; discharge if well-appearing with normal labs and reliable follow-up.

Admit for STEC Monitoring — Daily CBC/BMP/LDH/retic, strict I/O, notify MD for UOP <0.5 mL/kg/hr, new pallor/petechiae, AMS, or seizure.

Discharge with Follow-up — Labs at 24-48h, daily check-in, return precautions, monitor through day 10-14.

HUS Severity Assessment — Mild: plt 100-150k, Cr <2x normal. Moderate: plt 50-100k. Severe: plt <50k, anuria, refractory HTN, CNS involvement.

HUS Fluid Management — Pre-anuric: aggressive volume expansion (20 mL/kg NS boluses). Anuric: strict fluid restriction to insensible losses.

Transfusion Guidelines — RBC transfuse Hgb <7, target 8-9. Avoid platelet transfusion — worsens microvascular thrombosis.

Hypertension Management — Amlodipine first-line oral; nicardipine IV for emergency; avoid ACEi/ARBs acutely (hyperK risk in AKI).

Dialysis Indications — Anuria >24h, K >6.5, pH <7.1, HCO3 <10, volume overload, uremic encephalopathy, BUN >80-100. PD often preferred.

CNS Complications — 20-30% incidence, leading mortality cause. Seizures most common. Urgent MRI, aggressive BP control, ICU.

HUS Level of Care? — PICU if dialysis needed, severe HTN, CNS involvement, or unstable. Floor if stable with some urine output.

PICU Admission — Continuous monitoring, arterial line if labile BP, central access if dialysis needed, nephrology co-management.

Floor Admission — Strict I/O q1h, daily weights, cardiac monitor, labs q12-24h, nephrology consult, low threshold for PICU escalation.
