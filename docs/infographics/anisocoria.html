Anisocoria: Sick Pupil First, Then Localize by Light vs Dark

Adults presenting to the ED with anisocoria (pupil-size asymmetry). Excludes diplopia/ptosis without anisocoria as the presenting complaint, and pediatric Horner syndrome oncology workup (needs a separate specialist protocol beyond this pathway).

⚠ Do not label anisocoria physiologic until dangerous causes are excluded. Diplopia, ptosis, abnormal EOM, severe headache, neuro deficit, or acute painful anisocoria must be screened for CN III palsy, Horner/dissection, acute angle closure, and trauma/open globe first.
⚠ Red painful eye + halos/vomiting/cloudy cornea + fixed or mid-dilated pupil = acute angle-closure glaucoma until proven otherwise — check IOP only if open globe is not suspected.
⚠ Trauma, irregular pupil, hyphema, or possible open globe: protect the globe first — avoid tonometry and pressure if open globe is possible.
⚠ Large abnormal pupil: do not miss CN III palsy or acute angle closure. New painful or pupil-involving CN III palsy with ptosis, diplopia, or incomplete third-nerve findings is an aneurysm rule-out emergency — image now, and do not reassure based on vascular risk factors alone.
⚠ Small abnormal pupil: acute painful Horner syndrome means carotid dissection until excluded — same-day CTA/MRA head and neck. Apraclonidine/cocaine confirmatory testing must not delay vascular imaging.
⚠ Horner syndrome is a localization diagnosis, not a reassurance. Acute, painful, traumatic, or neurologic Horner still requires same-day vascular imaging even after the triad is confirmed.
⚠ When classification is uncertain, disposition follows the worst remaining red flag, not the most benign possibility. Unreliable measurement, limited exam, no old photos, an altered patient, or uncertain drop exposure do not justify a benign label.

1 · Sick pupil screen — exclude before you localize

Start with visual acuity, pupil sizes in bright and dim light, EOMs, ptosis, IOP if no open-globe concern, fluorescein/slit lamp when appropriate, and a focused neuro exam.[1][2]

Emergency screen bundle
Visual acuity both eyes; pupils in bright and dim light including reactivity and shape; EOMs and ptosis; full neuro screen including gait if safe; IOP only if open globe is not suspected; CTA/MRA head and neck when CN III palsy or painful Horner is suspected; early ophthalmology plus neurology/neurosurgery depending on pattern.[3][5]
Red painful eye → acute angle-closure until proven otherwise
Pattern: painful red eye, halos, nausea/vomiting, headache, cloudy cornea, fixed or mid-dilated pupil, reduced vision, high IOP if measurable. Check IOP only if open globe is not suspected; start acute angle-closure treatment immediately if suspicion is high; emergent ophthalmology. Full medication sequence and disposition: Open AACG consult.[1][2]
Trauma or irregular pupil → protect the globe first
Pattern: blunt/penetrating trauma, teardrop or irregular pupil, hyphema, Seidel sign, peaked pupil, shallow anterior chamber, severe chemosis/proptosis, high IOP with vision loss, or restricted EOM. Shield the eye, avoid pressure, avoid tonometry if open globe is possible; CT orbits without contrast for globe/IOFB when indicated; emergent ophthalmology. Ocular Trauma consult / Open Globe consult.[1][2]

2 · Localize by light vs dark

Measure each pupil in bright room light, dim the room and wait several seconds, then measure again. Anisocoria that gets larger in bright light means the larger pupil is abnormal (usually parasympathetic); anisocoria that gets larger in the dark means the smaller pupil is abnormal (usually sympathetic). Same in bright/dark and <1 mm is commonly physiologic if chronic and the exam is otherwise normal.[1][2]

3 · Large pupil abnormal — parasympathetic pathway

The larger pupil failed to constrict. Important causes: CN III palsy, acute angle closure, traumatic/iris abnormality, pharmacologic mydriasis, and Adie tonic pupil. Look for EOM abnormality, ptosis, headache/eye pain, red eye, IOP elevation, trauma, and drug exposure.[1][3][6][7]

CN III palsy pattern → image for aneurysm now
Concerning pattern: larger poorly reactive pupil with ptosis, diplopia, impaired adduction/elevation/depression, eye down-and-out, headache, eye pain, or incomplete third-nerve findings. CTA head/neck or MRI/MRA per local pathway; if negative but suspicion remains high, catheter angiography may still be pursued; ophthalmology plus neurology/neurosurgery. Treat severe headache/SAH symptoms as the subarachnoid hemorrhage pathway. Do not reassure based only on vascular risk factors.[3][5]
Large irregular pupil after trauma or surgery
Likely causes: traumatic mydriasis, iris sphincter tear, hyphema, lens injury, open globe, prior ocular surgery, or pharmacologic dilation after an eye exam. Visual acuity, slit lamp if safe, fluorescein if no obvious open globe; avoid IOP measurement if open globe is possible; CT orbits if open globe/IOFB/fracture/retrobulbar hemorrhage is a concern; ophthalmology based on injury severity.[1][2]

Ask about exposure: diagnostic/cycloplegic eye drops, scopolamine patch hand-to-eye transfer, a leaking ipratropium neb or anticholinergic inhaler, glycopyrrolate, anticholinergic plants, sympathomimetic/decongestant sprays, cocaine/amphetamine exposure, botulinum toxin near the orbit, or an accidental wrong-eye/wrong-drop exposure. Bedside clue: an anticholinergic-blocked pupil usually will not constrict to 1% pilocarpine; adrenergic mydriasis may retain some light response; a nonpharmacologic dilated pupil often constricts. Do not let drop testing delay imaging when CN III signs, headache, ptosis, diplopia, or neuro deficit are present.[1][6][7]

Likely pharmacologic mydriasis
Pattern: isolated large pupil, poor light response, normal EOMs, no ptosis, no neuro deficit, no red painful eye, a compatible exposure, and no constriction with 1% pilocarpine if tested. Remove/stop the exposure if possible; explain expected gradual resolution; ophthalmology follow-up if persistent, painful, vision change, irregular pupil, or uncertain exposure. Discharge is reasonable only when exposure is clear and the danger screen is negative.[1][6]
Adie tonic pupil pattern
Pattern: young-to-middle-aged adult, larger pupil, poor light reaction, better near response, slow redilation, segmental iris sphincter palsy on slit lamp, usually unilateral and benign. Dilute pilocarpine may show denervation hypersensitivity, but false positives occur and this does not clear CN III palsy when EOM/ptosis/headache is present. Outpatient ophthalmology or neuro-ophthalmology if isolated and stable; escalate for any CN III features, headache, trauma, red eye, or neuro deficit.[1][7]

4 · Small pupil abnormal — sympathetic pathway

The smaller pupil failed to dilate. Important causes: Horner syndrome, physiologic anisocoria, uveitis/iritis, miotic medications, and rare brainstem/cavernous sinus lesions. Look for mild ptosis, dilation lag in the dark, face/neck/head pain, anhidrosis, trauma, neuro symptoms, and carotid dissection risk.[1][2][4]

Acute painful Horner → image head and neck same day
Concerning pattern: miosis + mild ptosis + dilation lag, especially with ipsilateral neck pain, face pain, headache, recent trauma/manipulation, TIA/stroke symptoms, or cluster-like pain. CTA head and neck or MRI/MRA per local pathway; differential includes carotid dissection, cavernous sinus lesion, brainstem stroke, and apical lung/neck pathology; neurology/stroke consultation when acute, painful, traumatic, or neurologic. Apraclonidine/cocaine testing must not delay vascular imaging in acute painful presentations.[1][4][8]
Horner syndrome: confirm and localize
Classic triad: miosis, mild ptosis, facial anhidrosis — often incomplete. Dilation lag in dim light supports Horner. Acute, painful, traumatic, or neurologic Horner needs same-day CTA/MRA head and neck; chronic painless Horner with old photos confirming stability may be appropriate for outpatient neuro-ophthalmology; pediatric Horner needs specialist evaluation for neuroblastoma/neck/chest pathology. Apraclonidine can reverse anisocoria after denervation hypersensitivity develops; cocaine testing is older and less available — neither should delay imaging when acute or painful.[1][4][8]
Small painful pupil → evaluate for anterior uveitis/iritis
Pattern: photophobia, ciliary flush, consensual photophobia, cells/flare, small or irregular pupil from posterior synechiae, pain out of proportion to simple conjunctivitis. Visual acuity, slit lamp, fluorescein, IOP if no open-globe concern; avoid starting topical steroid without ophthalmology direction; cycloplegic and pain control per local ophthalmology guidance; urgent ophthalmology follow-up, same day if severe vision loss, high IOP, hypopyon, immunocompromise, or HSV/VZV concern.[1][2]
Likely pharmacologic miosis
Pattern: smaller pupil abnormal, no painful red eye/photophobia, no ptosis or dilation lag suggesting Horner, no focal neurologic findings, and a compatible miotic/cholinergic exposure (pilocarpine/carbachol drops, older cholinesterase-inhibitor miotics, apraclonidine/brimonidine or other topical glaucoma drops, organophosphate/carbamate ocular splash, systemic opioid/clonidine). Remove/stop exposure, irrigate if chemical splash, contact poison center/toxicology for organophosphate or cholinergic syndrome, ophthalmology follow-up if persistent or uncertain.[1][2][9]

5 · Physiologic, uncertain, and disposition

Physiologic anisocoria likely
Reassuring pattern: difference usually <1 mm; similar difference in bright and dim light; normal visual acuity, EOMs, fields by confrontation, IOP if checked, and neuro exam; no ptosis, red eye, trauma, headache/neck pain, diplopia, or new neurologic symptom; old photos or driver license show the same asymmetry. Discharge with routine eye follow-up and strict return precautions; new anisocoria without an old-photo comparison should be handled more cautiously.[1][2]
Uncertain anisocoria: do not force a benign label
Use when measurement is unreliable, exam is limited, old photos are unavailable, the patient is intoxicated/altered, drop exposure is uncertain, or symptoms do not fit cleanly. Escalate if headache, neck/face pain, ptosis, diplopia, abnormal EOM, neuro deficit, red painful eye, trauma, irregular pupil, vision loss, or acute onset remain. If any neurologic red flag: CTA/MRA head and neck and neurology/ophthalmology; if red eye/vision loss: ophthalmology same day; if isolated but truly new and unexplained: urgent outpatient neuro-ophthalmology or short observation. Document pupil sizes in bright and dim light plus why dangerous causes were excluded or pursued.[1][2][3][4]

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT label anisocoria physiologic until dangerous causes are excluded** — CN III palsy, Horner/dissection, acute angle closure, and trauma/open globe must be screened first.

**Do NOT reassure a CN III palsy pattern based on vascular risk factors alone** — pupil involvement, pain, incomplete palsy, or atypical features need emergent imaging regardless of age or risk profile.

**Do NOT let pharmacologic drop testing delay imaging** when CN III signs, headache, ptosis, diplopia, or a neuro deficit are present.

**Do NOT measure IOP if an open globe is possible** — avoid tonometry and pressure on the globe in trauma or suspected rupture.

**Do NOT let apraclonidine/cocaine confirmatory testing delay vascular imaging** in acute painful Horner syndrome — image head and neck the same day.

**Do NOT force a benign label when classification is uncertain** — disposition follows the worst remaining red flag, not the most benign possibility.
