Acute Psychosis: First-Break Workup, Differentiation & Treatment

Adults presenting to the ED with new-onset (first-break) or exacerbated psychosis. Covers first-break workup, known-psychiatric-patient relapse, and acutely agitated presentations. Excludes very-young-onset psychosis (<13, consider developmental/organic causes) and active agitation management itself — hand off to the Acute Agitation consult first, then return here for workup.

⚠ New-onset psychosis in a patient age >=45 with no prior psychiatric history is a medical/organic cause until proven otherwise — do not treat as primary psychiatric disease without a targeted workup.
⚠ First-break psychosis needs a targeted medical assessment — red flags (age, acute onset, visual hallucinations, fluctuating mental status, abnormal vitals, new neuro deficits, prodrome) drive how far the workup expands; do not skip the organic workup because the presentation looks psychiatric.
⚠ Agitated patient: stabilize first — verbal de-escalation (BETA protocol) -> chemical restraint if de-escalation fails -> physical restraint only if immediately necessary — before starting the diagnostic workup. Check POC glucose and vital signs during sedation.
⚠ Delirium is a medical emergency, NOT a psychiatric admission. Do NOT use antipsychotics as first-line, do NOT use benzodiazepines (worsen delirium except in withdrawal), and do NOT use physical restraints (increase mortality) — find and treat the underlying cause.
⚠ Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is a treatable cause that is easily missed: young woman, viral-like prodrome, psychiatric symptoms progressing to movement disorder, seizures, and autonomic instability — screen with LP, EEG, and pelvic imaging (ovarian teratoma in 50% of cases).
⚠ Avoid antipsychotics in Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia except quetiapine or clozapine.
⚠ First-break psychosis without reliable support or rapid follow-up requires psychiatric admission even when symptoms are mild.

1 · Initial assessment

First-break or new-onset psychosis? Establish age at onset, prior psychiatric history, onset timing (hours vs weeks), and substance use before choosing how broad a workup to run. Red flags for an organic cause: Age >=45 with no prior psychiatric history, acute onset (hours-days), visual hallucinations predominant, fluctuating mental status, abnormal vital signs, new neurological deficits, recent illness/prodrome.

Onset patternTypical cause
Schizophrenia: 18-25 (men), 25-35 (women)Primary psychotic disorder
Bipolar with psychosis: 20-30Primary psychotic disorder
Brief psychotic disorder: 30-50Primary psychotic disorder
Age >=45, no prior historyOrganic/neurologic cause in the majority — dementia, delirium, stroke, Parkinson's, Lewy body, substance, brain tumor
Age <13Consider organic causes, developmental disorders

2 · What to do now

Agitated — safety first
Do not start the diagnostic workup until the patient is calm. Verbal de-escalation (BETA protocol) first; chemical restraint if de-escalation fails; physical restraint only if immediately necessary. Check POC glucose and vital signs during sedation, then return here for the workup. See the Acute Agitation consult.
Known psychiatric patient — find the trigger
Medication non-adherence is the #1 cause of relapse. Minimum workup: vital signs, POC glucose, urine drug screen, and medication levels if applicable (lithium, valproate). Still pursue organic causes if the presentation differs from baseline, vitals are abnormal, or there are new neurologic symptoms.
Structural cause found — treat it
An abnormal CT/MRI finding changes the diagnosis and the treatment. Treat the underlying cause (stroke, tumor, encephalitis, metabolic, thyroid, substance, or medication); use a low-dose antipsychotic for symptom control only (quetiapine often well-tolerated); avoid antipsychotics in Parkinson's/Lewy body disease except quetiapine or clozapine.
Delirium — medical emergency, not psych admission
Search for the cause: infection, metabolic derangement (glucose, sodium, calcium), drug effect/withdrawal (anticholinergics, benzos, opioids), hypoxia/hypercapnia, urinary retention/fecal impaction, or pain. Do NOT use antipsychotics as first-line, do NOT use benzodiazepines (worsen delirium except in withdrawal), and do NOT use physical restraints (increase mortality). See the Psych Medical Stability consult.
Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis — urgent workup
LP for lymphocytic pleocytosis and anti-NMDA receptor antibodies (CSF more sensitive than serum). EEG may show extreme delta brush. Screen for ovarian teratoma with pelvic ultrasound/CT — present in 50% of cases. First-line treatment: steroids, IVIG, plasmapheresis, and tumor removal if present; 75% have substantial recovery with early treatment.
Start an antipsychotic — start low, go slow
Second-generation antipsychotic, monotherapy, allow 2-4 weeks for full effect. Choose the agent based on metabolic risk, QTc, EPS sensitivity, and sedation needs below.

4 · Organic workup

  1. Full vitals, glucose, medication/substance history
  2. CBC, CMP, glucose
  3. TSH when thyroid symptoms, mood syndrome, or unclear cause
  4. Urinalysis if urinary symptoms, pregnancy concern, elderly, or delirium concern
  5. Urine drug screen when substance-induced symptoms are plausible
  6. ECG before QT-prolonging antipsychotics or if overdose/cardiac risk

Consider when risk factors or red flags: B12, folate, RPR/VDRL and HIV based on risk or atypical presentation, ammonia (hepatic encephalopathy), blood alcohol level, heavy metals (lead, mercury), ceruloplasmin (Wilson's disease if <40), ANA/ESR (autoimmune).

Age >=45 — extensive workup
High-yield investigations: CT head, MRI brain if CT unrevealing, LP if meningitis/encephalitis suspected, EEG for seizures/encephalopathy. Additional labs: ammonia, cortisol, parathyroid hormone, anti-NMDA receptor antibodies, paraneoplastic panel, HIV, RPR. Consider neurology consult and formal cognitive testing.
  1. Age >45 with first psychiatric symptoms
  2. Focal neurological deficits
  3. Headache, seizure, or decreased consciousness
  4. Recent head trauma
  5. Papilledema
  6. Anticoagulation or immunosuppression
  7. Atypical presentation or delirium features

MRI preferred if: concern for encephalitis, demyelinating disease, subacute neurologic symptoms, small lesions missed on CT, or stable first-episode psychosis needing non-emergent structural evaluation.

  3. Seizures

Diagnosis: LP (lymphocytic pleocytosis, anti-NMDA receptor antibodies — CSF more sensitive than serum); MRI often normal or nonspecific; EEG extreme delta brush (specific but not sensitive). Tumor screen: pelvic ultrasound/CT (ovarian teratoma in 50%), CT chest/abdomen. Treatment: first-line steroids, IVIG, plasmapheresis; tumor removal if present improves outcomes; second-line rituximab, cyclophosphamide. Prognosis: good with early treatment — 75% substantial recovery.

Organic cause identified; delirium; anti-NMDA encephalitis or other autoimmune concern; abnormal vital signs; neurologic findings, seizure, intoxication/withdrawal, or unstable medical issue.
