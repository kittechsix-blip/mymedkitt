Pediatric Submersion Injury: Resuscitation, Hypothermia, and Disposition

Pediatric patients (all ages) presenting to the ED after a submersion/drowning event. Based on ILCOR/AHA 2020 pediatric guidelines and pediatric drowning literature. Excludes scene/pre-hospital rescue operations beyond initial handoff and adult-specific drowning nuances.

⚠ Drowning cardiac arrest is a respiratory emergency: give 5 rescue breaths BEFORE starting compressions, then standard PALS CPR 15:2. Do NOT attempt a Heimlich maneuver — aspirated water cannot be expelled by abdominal thrusts.
⚠ Severe hypothermia (<28°C): limit defibrillation to 3 attempts and withhold IV/IO medications until core temperature is >30°C. Continue high-quality CPR during rewarming — "not dead until warm and dead."
⚠ Do NOT terminate resuscitation while core temperature is <30°C, regardless of poor prognostic factors — continue CPR until the patient is rewarmed or ECMO is available.
⚠ Consider non-accidental trauma in any submersion injury in a child <1 year of age, or when the history is inconsistent with the child's developmental stage.
⚠ There is no "dry drowning" or "secondary drowning" as a distinct clinical entity — delayed complications, when they occur, manifest within 4-8 hours of a symptomatic event, not days later.

1 · Overview & prognosis

Drowning is the leading cause of injury death in children ages 1-4 and second leading cause ages 5-14. Submersion duration is the single most important prognostic factor.

Other key factors: scene CPR and time to ROSC, water temperature (cold water may be protective), initial GCS and pupil reactivity.

There is no "dry drowning" as a distinct entity — delayed complications occur within 4-8 hours of a symptomatic presentation.

Submersion durationExpected outcome
<5 minGenerally good outcomes
5-10 minVariable outcomes
>10 minPoor prognosis (normothermic patients)
>25 min in warm waterFutility likely

Critical history: submersion duration, water type/temperature, witnessed status, time to rescue and first breath/pulse, scene CPR, pre-event trauma/seizure/underlying conditions.

Non-accidental trauma red flags: consider child abuse in submersion injuries <1 year of age, or an inconsistent history / delayed presentation / mechanism that does not fit the child's developmental stage.

2 · What to do now

Cardiac arrest — airway first
Clear the airway, give 5 rescue breaths before compressions, then standard PALS CPR 15:2. Do NOT attempt a Heimlich maneuver. Defibrillate VF/pVT if present (rare — usually asystole/PEA). Assess core temperature next.
Normothermic arrest — standard resuscitation
Standard PALS medications at standard intervals. Proceed to assess resuscitation outcome (ROSC / ongoing / termination).
Mild hypothermia (32-35°C)
Standard PALS medications and active rewarming (remove wet clothing, forced-air warming, warm IV fluids 38-42°C) — expected rate 1-2°C/hour. Do not withhold CPR or defibrillation. Watch for afterdrop and rewarming hyperkalemia.
Severe hypothermia (<28°C) — "not dead until warm and dead"
Limit defibrillation to 3 attempts and withhold IV medications until core temp >30°C. ECMO is gold standard (rewarms 9-10°C/hour); if unavailable use warm IV fluids, peritoneal/pleural lavage, or bladder irrigation. Continue CPR during transport.
ROSC achieved — post-arrest care
Target SpO2 94-98% (avoid hyperoxia and hyperventilation). Targeted temperature management 32-34°C for 24-72 hours in comatose patients, or maintain normothermia. Treat seizures aggressively; avoid hypo-/hyperglycemia.
Termination considerations
Consider when multiple poor prognostic factors are present: warm-water submersion >25 min, normothermic CPR >25 minutes without ROSC, asystole as initial rhythm (without hypothermia), or pH <6.75. Do NOT terminate if core temp <30°C or ECMO/transfer is possible. Involve family early.
Altered mental status — assess and rule out mimics
Assess airway protection, breathing, circulation. Intubate if GCS ≤8, unable to protect airway, or severe hypoxia. Check glucose. Do NOT attribute AMS solely to drowning — rule out hypoglycemia, seizure, head trauma, toxic ingestion.
AMS improving / stable, GCS >8
Serial neuro exams every 15-30 minutes, continuous monitoring, supportive care, admission for observation.
AMS static or worsening, GCS ≤8
Intubate for airway protection, consider head CT, ICU admission — proceed to post-arrest/critical care management.
Seizing — treat aggressively
First-line lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg), may repeat once. Second-line levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV (max 4500 mg) or fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg IV (max 1500 mg PE). Seizures worsen hypoxic-ischemic brain injury — treat aggressively and continue to post-arrest/ICU care.
Respiratory symptoms, normal mentation
Check SpO2 on room air, work of breathing, auscultation; consider CXR. Pathophysiology is surfactant washout and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema — as little as 1-3 mL/kg aspiration causes significant injury.
Mild respiratory symptoms (SpO2 >94% RA)
Supplemental O2 PRN, observe 4-6 hours, serial exams. Candidate for ED observation and discharge if criteria met.
Moderate respiratory symptoms (SpO2 90-94%)
Supplemental O2 to keep SpO2 >94%, consider CPAP/BiPAP, admit for observation.
Severe respiratory symptoms (SpO2 <90%)
High-flow O2 or NIPPV, consider intubation if failing, ARDS-protocol ventilation if intubated — proceed to post-arrest/critical care management.
Asymptomatic
Good prognostic signs: GCS 15, normal respiratory exam, SpO2 ≥95% RA, brief submersion (<5 min). No evidence of delayed deterioration in truly asymptomatic patients — proceed to ED observation.
