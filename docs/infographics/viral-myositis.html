Pediatric (2-14y) and adult (>14y) patients presenting to the ED with viral myositis, including Benign Acute Childhood Myositis (BACM), following a viral prodrome (most commonly influenza). Age-based triage into a pediatric BACM pathway, an adult rhabdomyolysis-risk pathway, or a toxic/ill-appearing pathway requiring expanded workup for red flags and alternative diagnoses.

Initial assessment

Viral myositis is an acute inflammatory condition of skeletal muscle following viral infection. In children it typically presents as Benign Acute Childhood Myositis (BACM) with excellent prognosis; in adults it may progress to rhabdomyolysis with potential AKI.

  • Influenza A and B (most common)
  • Parainfluenza, RSV
  • Enteroviruses, Coxsackie
  • EBV, CMV, Adenovirus
  • COVID-19

Toxic / ill-appearing pathway

  1. IV access, cardiac monitor
  2. Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (NS 10-20 mL/kg bolus)
  3. Labs: CBC, CMP, CK, UA, myoglobin, lactate
  4. ECG (hyperkalemia screening)
Red flags for alternative diagnoses
Focal neurologic deficits (consider GBS, transverse myelitis); ascending paralysis (GBS); ptosis/bulbar symptoms (myasthenia gravis); diffuse rash (dermatomyositis, toxic shock); proximal > distal weakness (inflammatory myopathy).

Pediatric (BACM) clinical presentation

Benign Acute Childhood Myositis (BACM): peak age 5-9 years (range 2-14), male predominance (3:1), symptom onset 1-7 days after a viral prodrome (often influenza). Classic symptoms: sudden-onset bilateral calf pain (90%), refusal to walk or "duck waddle" gait, calf tenderness. Usually afebrile at presentation, normal neurologic exam, symmetric involvement, self-limited (resolves in 3-5 days).

Exclude serious conditions before diagnosing BACM

Red flags requiring emergent workup
Asymmetric leg pain/swelling (DVT, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis); focal bone tenderness (fracture, osteomyelitis); joint effusion/erythema (septic arthritis); abdominal pain + leg pain (psoas abscess, appendicitis); ascending weakness (Guillain-Barre syndrome); petechiae/purpura (meningococcemia, HSP); tea-colored urine (myoglobinuria); recent trauma or extreme exertion.

Expanded workup for red flags

  • Labs: CBC, CMP, CK, UA, ESR, CRP, blood culture if febrile, procalcitonin if sepsis concern
  • Imaging as indicated: X-ray (fracture/osteomyelitis), ultrasound (DVT/abscess/effusion), MRI (osteomyelitis/pyomyositis concern)
  • Additional testing: LP (GBS/meningitis concern), nerve conduction studies (GBS)

Disposition depends on findings: if an alternative diagnosis is confirmed, treat accordingly; if BACM is confirmed after workup, manage per risk stratification.

BACM laboratory workup

Minimal workup for classic BACM: CK (typically 1,000-10,000 U/L), UA for myoglobin (blood-positive dipstick, no RBCs on microscopy), and optional BMP if clinical concern for renal involvement. Expected findings: normal creatinine, mild transaminitis (AST > ALT, from muscle), possible mild leukopenia/thrombocytopenia (viral). CK peaks at 2-3 days and normalizes by 7-14 days.

CK does not predict severity
CK level does NOT correlate with severity in BACM — even massive CK elevation rarely causes AKI in children.

Adult viral myositis clinical presentation

Adults have higher risk of rhabdomyolysis and AKI than children. Common presentation: diffuse myalgias (not limited to calves), proximal weakness (thighs, shoulders), muscle tenderness, possible ongoing fever/constitutional symptoms. Dark urine (myoglobinuria) is a concerning sign. Risk factors for a severe course: older age, pre-existing renal disease, dehydration, concurrent statins/fibrates, immunocompromised state.

Adult myositis severity assessment

Concerning features for rhabdomyolysis
Tea-colored or dark urine; decreased urine output; severe muscle pain/weakness; inability to ambulate; fever persisting >5 days; concurrent statin/fibrate use.

Mild adult myositis workup

Basic labs: CK, BMP (creatinine, potassium), UA (check for myoglobin). If normal renal function and CK <5,000: supportive care at home, PO hydration (2-3 L/day), Acetaminophen or Ibuprofen for pain, avoid strenuous activity. Consider viral testing (influenza, COVID-19) if suggestive history.

Full adult lab panel (moderate-severe symptoms)

Standard labs: CBC with differential, CMP (renal function, electrolytes, LFTs), CK, UA with microscopy, serum myoglobin if available, lactate if ill-appearing. Consider: ECG if K+ concerns, influenza/COVID testing, TSH (hypothyroid myopathy), ANA/aldolase if subacute course. CK thresholds: CK >5,000 U/L + dehydration/acidosis = high AKI risk; CK >10,000 U/L = significant rhabdomyolysis; CK >100,000 U/L = severe, high AKI risk regardless of hydration.

Rhabdomyolysis — pathophysiology and complications

CK >10,000 U/L, evidence of AKI, or myoglobinuria requires aggressive management. Muscle cell necrosis releases myoglobin, which precipitates in renal tubules causing direct tubular toxicity and renal vasoconstriction — AKI can occur rapidly.

Complications
AKI (21% incidence in rhabdomyolysis); hyperkalemia (life-threatening); hypocalcemia (early); hypercalcemia (recovery phase); DIC (severe cases); compartment syndrome (rare in viral myositis).

Rhabdomyolysis treatment protocol

Normal Saline
1-2 L bolus (adults) or 20 mL/kg (peds)
IV
Then 200-300 mL/hr (or 3-5 mL/kg/hr peds), until CK <5,000 and downtrending. May require 10-12 L in first 24 hours. Foley catheter required. Goal UOP: 200-300 mL/hr (adults) or 2-3 mL/kg/hr (peds).
  • Hyperkalemia: treat if K+ >6.0 or ECG changes — calcium gluconate for cardiac membrane stabilization, insulin + dextrose, albuterol. Avoid calcium chloride if possible (can worsen rhabdo).
  • Hypocalcemia: do NOT treat unless symptomatic — will correct with recovery.
  • Bicarbonate: controversial, no proven benefit; consider if pH <7.1 or severe acidosis.
  • Diuretics: generally avoid (can worsen hypovolemia); mannitol only with nephrology guidance.
  • Dialysis indications: refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, fluid overload, uremia symptoms.
Disposition
Admit to ICU or step-down. Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation targeting UOP 200-300 mL/hr. Serial CK, BMP, and UOP monitoring. Early nephrology consult if AKI progressing.

Reference — influenza-associated myositis (not tied to a flow step)

Antiviral therapy: Oseltamivir if within 48 hours of symptom onset; may still provide benefit after 48h in severe cases. Adult dosing: 75 mg PO BID x 5 days. Pediatric: weight-based dosing (<15 kg: 30 mg BID; 15-23 kg: 45 mg BID; 23-40 kg: 60 mg BID; >40 kg: 75 mg BID).

2024-2025 season data: BACM incidence was 32.7% in hospitalized influenza cases. Influenza B is more commonly associated with BACM than influenza A. Median CK 2,637 U/L (range 189-129,390). All had preserved renal function; no ICU admissions or deaths.

Prevention: Annual influenza vaccination may reduce BACM incidence; quadrivalent vaccine preferred.

Mild workup result? — Interpret CK, creatinine, myoglobinuria to risk-stratify.

Full panel result? — High risk for AKI: CK >10,000, myoglobinuria, elevated creatinine, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis.

Low risk: discharge, hydration, analgesia — CK <5,000, normal renal function. Outpatient management with oral hydration and analgesia; oseltamivir if influenza confirmed <48h.

Moderate risk: ED observation with IV fluids — CK 5,000-10,000 or mild creatinine elevation. Discharge if improving after 4-6h; admit if concerning trends.

Rhabdomyolysis: CK >10,000, AKI, or myoglobinuria — Myoglobin causes direct tubular toxicity and renal vasoconstriction — 21% AKI incidence in rhabdomyolysis.

Admit ICU/step-down: aggressive IV fluids — Goal UOP 200-300 mL/hr (adults). Avoid diuretics and NSAIDs. Serial CK, BMP, UOP monitoring; early nephrology consult.

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for the toxic/ill-appearing pathway detail, expanded red-flag workup, and complete BACM/adult risk stratification.

Reference — influenza-associated myositis (not tied to a flow step)

Antiviral therapy: Oseltamivir if within 48 hours of symptom onset; may still provide benefit after 48h in severe cases. Adult dosing: 75 mg PO BID x 5 days. Pediatric: weight-based dosing (<15 kg: 30 mg BID; 15-23 kg: 45 mg BID; 23-40 kg: 60 mg BID; >40 kg: 75 mg BID).

2024-2025 season data: BACM incidence was 32.7% in hospitalized influenza cases. Influenza B is more commonly associated with BACM than influenza A. Median CK 2,637 U/L (range 189-129,390). All had preserved renal function; no ICU admissions or deaths.

Prevention: Annual influenza vaccination may reduce BACM incidence; quadrivalent vaccine preferred.

Oseltamivir (pediatric, approximate): 3 mg/kg PO PO — Approximate per-kg equivalent of the weight-banded label dosing above, x 5 days, within 48h of symptom onset.

Reference — differential diagnosis / mimics (not tied to a flow step)

Category | Mimics | Distinguishing feature

Infectious | Pyomyositis, necrotizing fasciitis, trichinosis, Lyme disease | Pyomyositis focal/unilateral; nec fasc rapid progression + systemic toxicity; trichinosis eosinophilia + periorbital edema; Lyme may have rash/arthritis

Inflammatory | Dermatomyositis, polymyositis, vasculitis | Rash + proximal weakness (dermatomyositis); insidious proximal weakness (polymyositis)

Neurologic | Guillain-Barre syndrome, transverse myelitis, myasthenia gravis | Ascending weakness + areflexia (GBS); sensory level (transverse myelitis); fatigable weakness + ptosis (myasthenia gravis)

Other | Compartment syndrome, DVT, drug-induced myopathy | Tense compartment + pain with passive stretch; unilateral swelling + Homan sign (DVT); statins/fibrates/colchicine history
