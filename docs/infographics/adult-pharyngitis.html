Adult Pharyngitis (Strep Throat) — ED Management

Adults ≥18 in the ED or outpatient setting with acute sore throat. Excludes pediatrics (back-up culture and weight-based dosing rules differ). Covers airway-threat triage, Centor/McIsaac-guided GAS testing/treatment, and screening for dangerous mimics (peritonsillar/retropharyngeal abscess, Lemierre syndrome, epiglottitis, Ludwig angina, mono, acute HIV, gonococcal pharyngitis).

⚠ Screen every sore throat for airway threat FIRST: drooling, tripoding, stridor, severe trismus, muffled/hot-potato voice, neck swelling, toxicity, or immunocompromise — manage the airway before Centor scoring or a detailed exam.
⚠ Never discharge a sore throat with airway compromise — call ENT/anesthesia early, have a surgical airway tray ready, avoid sedation/paralytics until the airway plan is set.
⚠ Do NOT test or treat with antibiotics at Centor/McIsaac score ≤1 — no test, no antibiotics, symptomatic care only.
⚠ Rapid GAS antigen only if Centor/McIsaac ≥2. IDSA does NOT recommend routine back-up culture after a negative rapid antigen in ADULTS (children DO need back-up culture).
⚠ Penicillin V 500 mg PO BID or TID is first-line for confirmed GAS — the full 10-day course is required to prevent acute rheumatic fever (ARF); treatment must start within 9 days of symptom onset.
⚠ AVOID amoxicillin/ampicillin if infectious mononucleosis is in the differential — a diffuse maculopapular rash develops in >80% of EBV cases on aminopenicillins.
⚠ Trismus + uvular deviation + hot-potato voice = peritonsillar abscess — needs drainage (needle aspiration or I&D), not antibiotics alone.
⚠ Persistent fever + new unilateral neck pain after a sore throat = rule out Lemierre syndrome (CT neck with IV contrast) — easy to miss because the sore throat itself is often reported as improving.
⚠ Retropharyngeal abscess, adult epiglottitis, and Ludwig angina are airway emergencies — CT neck with IV contrast, IV antibiotics per diagnosis, ENT/anesthesia at bedside, ICU admission. Do not force a detailed oropharyngeal exam before the airway plan is set.
⚠ Suspect gonococcal or acute HIV pharyngitis with high-risk sexual exposure — standard HIV antibody testing can be falsely NEGATIVE in acute HIV; HIV-1 RNA (viral load) is the test of choice.

Overview

Pharyngitis is one of the top 20 reasons for adult ED visits. Most cases are viral (~75-80%) and self-limited. Group A Strep (GAS) causes ~5-15% of adult sore throats — antibiotics shorten symptoms by ~16 hours and prevent acute rheumatic fever (ARF) and suppurative complications. The high-stakes work is identifying airway-threatening or life-threatening mimics: peritonsillar/retropharyngeal abscess, Lemierre syndrome, epiglottitis, Ludwig angina, acute HIV.

  1. Triage every sore throat for airway threat and red flags FIRST
  2. Apply Centor/McIsaac to risk-stratify for GAS
  3. Test (rapid antigen) only those with Centor/McIsaac ≥2
  4. Treat confirmed GAS, or empirically at high scores per local protocol
  5. Actively scan for dangerous mimics on every visit

  2. Sepsis screen — vitals, mental status, perfusion

A high score does NOT confirm GAS — viral pharyngitis (especially adenovirus, EBV) can mimic all four Centor criteria. A low score does NOT rule out the rare dangerous mimic — always screen for red flags separately. The score is not a substitute for examining the airway, neck, and skin.

Rapid GAS Testing

Sensitivity 70-90%, specificity 95-99%, result in 5-10 minutes. A positive RADT with compatible clinical findings is diagnostic — treat. A negative RADT in an adult with Centor/McIsaac ≥2 does NOT require back-up culture (IDSA 2012) — high specificity, low pretest probability in adults. Pediatric patients DO need back-up culture for a negative RADT. Molecular (NAAT/PCR) tests (sensitivity/specificity 95-99%) are accepted alone without back-up culture in adults AND children. Do NOT test asymptomatic contacts (chronic carriage 5-20% of the population is biologically unimportant).

Complications & Mimics

Screen for mimics on every visit
Actively scan for these — not just in airway-threatened patients. Trismus + uvular deviation + hot-potato voice = peritonsillar abscess. Persistent fever + unilateral neck pain after sore throat = Lemierre.

The most common deep neck space infection in adults. Unilateral severe sore throat, trismus, uvular deviation away from the abscess, hot-potato/muffled voice, drooling, dysphagia, tender unilateral cervical LAD, bulging peritonsillar tissue. Bedside intraoral/transcervical ultrasound helps confirm abscess vs cellulitis; CT neck with IV contrast if exam non-diagnostic or can't open mouth.

Amoxicillin-clavulanate
875/125 mg PO BID (if discharging)
PO
Oral step-down after drainage if tolerating PO and reliable follow-up. Course 10-14 days.

More common in children but occurs in adults, often after trauma, FB ingestion, or dental infection. Neck stiffness or extension preference ("won't flex"), torticollis, posterior pharyngeal wall swelling, drooling, dysphagia, dyspnea. Adult red flags: IVDU, recent dental work, immunocompromise. CT neck with IV contrast is the test of choice. Most RPAs require operative drainage by ENT/oral maxillofacial surgery; admit, usually ICU with an airway-trained team.

"The forgotten disease" — septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, classically Fusobacterium necrophorum. Recent sore throat (often 4-12 days prior, sometimes improved transiently), persistent/recurrent fever, unilateral neck pain/swelling along the SCM, toxic appearance, septic pulmonary emboli (pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis). CT neck with IV contrast is the test of choice; CT chest with contrast for septic emboli; blood cultures. Anticoagulation is controversial — shared decision with IR/hematology/ID. Easy to miss in early visits because the sore throat is "improving" while fever and neck pain are new.

Epiglottitis and Ludwig angina use DIFFERENT antibiotic regimens
These two diagnoses share this node because both are airway emergencies, but their antibiotic regimens are NOT interchangeable: epiglottitis = ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h + vancomycin. Ludwig angina = ampicillin-sulbactam or piperacillin-tazobactam (± clindamycin if PCN-allergic). Do not substitute one regimen for the other.

Adult epiglottitis: severe sore throat out of proportion to the oropharyngeal exam, drooling, tripoding, muffled voice, stridor (late). Definitive diagnosis: fiberoptic laryngoscopy by ENT/anesthesia. Airway plan (OR intubation by ENT/anesthesia; surgical airway ready). ICU admission.

Teens and young adults; exudative pharyngitis often indistinguishable from GAS. Posterior cervical LAD (vs anterior in GAS), generalized LAD, fatigue. Splenomegaly in up to 50% — palpate the LUQ. Atypical lymphocytosis; monospot false negatives common in first week, EBV VCA IgM more sensitive early. Return precautions for abdominal pain (Kehr sign of splenic rupture).

Scarlet fever: GAS + erythrogenic exotoxin. Sandpaper-textured rash (sparing palms/soles, blanches), strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, Pastia lines. Treat as GAS pharyngitis. Notify public health if outbreak suspected.

Acute HIV: non-exudative pharyngitis + fever + generalized LAD + maculopapular rash + myalgias + mucocutaneous ulcers, mononucleosis-like illness 2-4 weeks after exposure. Standard HIV antibody tests can be NEGATIVE in the acute phase — HIV-1 RNA (viral load) is the test of choice, turnaround 24-48h, sensitivity >99%. Gonococcal pharyngitis: often asymptomatic; GC NAAT on throat swab (do NOT rely on rapid GAS antigen). Sexual partner notification + STI panel. Consider Doxy-PEP (doxycycline 200 mg PO within 72h of unprotected sex) for MSM/transgender women with a bacterial STI in the prior 12 months.

Peritonsillar abscess (Quinsy) — Trismus + uvular deviation + hot-potato voice. Drain (needle or I&D) + IV antibiotics + single-dose dexamethasone. Bedside US helpful; CT if non-diagnostic.

Rapid GAS antigen result? — Sens 70-90%, spec 95-99%. Positive = treat. Negative + score 2-3 = symptomatic. Negative + score ≥4 = empiric decision per local protocol.

Retropharyngeal abscess — Neck stiffness/extension preference, posterior pharyngeal swelling. CT neck w/ contrast. Airway first, IV amp-sulbactam or pip-tazo, ENT drainage, admit ICU.

First-line antibiotics for confirmed GAS — Penicillin V 500mg PO BID-TID x10d (first-line); amoxicillin 1000mg PO daily x10d; benzathine PCN G 1.2 MU IM x1. 10-day course required for ARF prevention. Avoid amoxicillin if mono.

Symptomatic / supportive care — NSAID + APAP combination; single-dose dexamethasone 10mg PO/IM; salt gargle, lozenges; no empiric antibiotics; strong return precautions.

Lemierre syndrome — Septic IJ thrombophlebitis, Fusobacterium. CT neck/chest w/ contrast, blood cultures. Amp-sulbactam/pip-tazo/meropenem 1g IV q8h ± metronidazole 500mg IV q8h. 4-6 weeks abx. ICU + ID.

Epiglottitis & Ludwig angina — Both airway emergencies — different regimens. Epiglottitis: ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h + vancomycin. Ludwig: bilateral submandibular swelling + tongue elevation; amp-sulbactam/pip-tazo ± clindamycin, surgical drainage. ICU.

Mononucleosis & scarlet fever — Posterior LAD, splenomegaly up to 50%. AVOID amoxicillin (rash >80%). Splenic precautions 4-6 weeks. Scarlet fever = sandpaper rash + strawberry tongue, treat as GAS.

Acute HIV & gonococcal pharyngitis — Acute HIV: HIV-1 RNA is the test (antibody can be falsely negative). GC: NAAT; ceftriaxone 500mg IM x1 (1g if ≥150kg) + empiric doxycycline for chlamydia; test-of-cure 7-14 days.

Disposition — Discharge if stable airway, tolerating PO, reliable follow-up. Admit for abscess, Lemierre, Ludwig, epiglottitis, immunocompromise, or airway compromise.

Home — uncomplicated pharyngitis — Stable airway, tolerating PO, adequate pain control, reliable follow-up. Antibiotics if indicated, NSAID+APAP, single-dose dexamethasone option, strong return precautions, PCP follow-up 24-72h.

Admit — complicated pharyngitis — Abscess, Lemierre, epiglottitis, Ludwig, septic/toxic shock, immunocompromise, airway compromise, failed outpatient management. IV antibiotics per diagnosis, airway monitoring, ENT/ID/ICU as indicated.

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for complete disposition criteria and every citation.

Complications & Mimics

Actively scan for these — not just in airway-threatened patients. Trismus + uvular deviation + hot-potato voice = peritonsillar abscess. Persistent fever + unilateral neck pain after sore throat = Lemierre.

The most common deep neck space infection in adults. Unilateral severe sore throat, trismus, uvular deviation away from the abscess, hot-potato/muffled voice, drooling, dysphagia, tender unilateral cervical LAD, bulging peritonsillar tissue. Bedside intraoral/transcervical ultrasound helps confirm abscess vs cellulitis; CT neck with IV contrast if exam non-diagnostic or can't open mouth.

Amoxicillin-clavulanate: 875/125 mg PO BID (if discharging) PO — Oral step-down after drainage if tolerating PO and reliable follow-up. Course 10-14 days.

More common in children but occurs in adults, often after trauma, FB ingestion, or dental infection. Neck stiffness or extension preference ("won't flex"), torticollis, posterior pharyngeal wall swelling, drooling, dysphagia, dyspnea. Adult red flags: IVDU, recent dental work, immunocompromise. CT neck with IV contrast is the test of choice. Most RPAs require operative drainage by ENT/oral maxillofacial surgery; admit, usually ICU with an airway-trained team.

"The forgotten disease" — septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, classically Fusobacterium necrophorum. Recent sore throat (often 4-12 days prior, sometimes improved transiently), persistent/recurrent fever, unilateral neck pain/swelling along the SCM, toxic appearance, septic pulmonary emboli (pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis). CT neck with IV contrast is the test of choice; CT chest with contrast for septic emboli; blood cultures. Anticoagulation is controversial — shared decision with IR/hematology/ID. Easy to miss in early visits because the sore throat is "improving" while fever and neck pain are new.

Meropenem: 1 g IV q8h IV — For severe sepsis or beta-lactamase concerns in Lemierre syndrome. Total duration 4-6 weeks.

Metronidazole: 500 mg IV q8h IV — Add if not using a beta-lactam/beta-lactamase-inhibitor combination (amp-sulbactam or pip-tazo already cover anaerobes).

These two diagnoses share this node because both are airway emergencies, but their antibiotic regimens are NOT interchangeable: epiglottitis = ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h + vancomycin. Ludwig angina = ampicillin-sulbactam or piperacillin-tazobactam (± clindamycin if PCN-allergic). Do not substitute one regimen for the other.

Adult epiglottitis: severe sore throat out of proportion to the oropharyngeal exam, drooling, tripoding, muffled voice, stridor (late). Definitive diagnosis: fiberoptic laryngoscopy by ENT/anesthesia. Airway plan (OR intubation by ENT/anesthesia; surgical airway ready). ICU admission.

Ceftriaxone (epiglottitis-specific): 2 g IV q24h IV — Covers S. pneumo, GAS, H. flu. Combine with vancomycin below. NOT the Ludwig regimen.

Ludwig angina: bilateral, rapidly progressive cellulitis of the submandibular spaces (often odontogenic). Bilateral submandibular swelling + tongue elevation ("woody" floor of mouth) + trismus + drool. Early awake fiberoptic intubation or surgical airway preferred. Surgical drainage if abscess on imaging (ENT/oral maxillofacial surgery). ICU admission with airway-capable team.

Vancomycin (low end): 15 mg/kg IV IV — Add for MRSA risk or hospital-acquired infection. Full range 15-20 mg/kg IV; this is the low end.

Vancomycin (high end): 20 mg/kg IV IV — High end of MRSA-coverage range.

Ampicillin-sulbactam: 3 g IV q6h IV — Covers oral anaerobes, strep, most gram-negatives. Used across airway, PTA, RPA, Ludwig, and admission regimens.

Piperacillin-tazobactam: 4.5 g IV q6-8h IV — Broader alternative to ampicillin-sulbactam for septic/Lemierre/Ludwig presentations.

Clindamycin (IV, PCN-allergic): 600-900 mg IV q8h IV — PCN-allergic alternative for airway-threat and Ludwig regimens ± levofloxacin.

Teens and young adults; exudative pharyngitis often indistinguishable from GAS. Posterior cervical LAD (vs anterior in GAS), generalized LAD, fatigue. Splenomegaly in up to 50% — palpate the LUQ. Atypical lymphocytosis; monospot false negatives common in first week, EBV VCA IgM more sensitive early. Return precautions for abdominal pain (Kehr sign of splenic rupture).

Scarlet fever: GAS + erythrogenic exotoxin. Sandpaper-textured rash (sparing palms/soles, blanches), strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, Pastia lines. Treat as GAS pharyngitis. Notify public health if outbreak suspected.

Acute HIV: non-exudative pharyngitis + fever + generalized LAD + maculopapular rash + myalgias + mucocutaneous ulcers, mononucleosis-like illness 2-4 weeks after exposure. Standard HIV antibody tests can be NEGATIVE in the acute phase — HIV-1 RNA (viral load) is the test of choice, turnaround 24-48h, sensitivity >99%. Gonococcal pharyngitis: often asymptomatic; GC NAAT on throat swab (do NOT rely on rapid GAS antigen). Sexual partner notification + STI panel. Consider Doxy-PEP (doxycycline 200 mg PO within 72h of unprotected sex) for MSM/transgender women with a bacterial STI in the prior 12 months.

Ceftriaxone: 500 mg IM × 1 (1 g if ≥150 kg) IM — Per CDC 2021 STI guidelines.

Doxycycline: 100 mg PO BID × 7 days PO — Empiric chlamydia co-treatment unless ruled out. Test-of-cure required 7-14 days after treatment for pharyngeal GC.

Disposition

  • Stable airway, no drool/stridor/tripod
  • Tolerating PO fluids and analgesia
  • Reliable follow-up + transportation + phone
  • No red flags on exam; splenic precautions if mono
  • Prescribe antibiotics if indicated + NSAID/APAP + single-dose dexamethasone option
  • Follow-up: PCP in 24-72h, sooner if not improving
  • Abscess requiring observation/failed drainage; retropharyngeal abscess (essentially always)
  • Lemierre syndrome (ICU + ID + 4-6 weeks IV antibiotics)
  • Adult epiglottitis or Ludwig angina (ICU + airway team)
  • Septic/toxic shock from GAS; immunocompromised host
  • Inability to tolerate PO, or suspected airway compromise
  • IV antibiotics per diagnosis; source control (drainage) for abscess; ID consult for Lemierre/septic shock
