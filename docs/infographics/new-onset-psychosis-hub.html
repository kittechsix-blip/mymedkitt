New-Onset Psychosis: Sick Check First, Then Time-Critical Exclusions

Adults with new-onset or acutely changed psychotic symptoms evaluated in the emergency department. Requires safety, vitals/glucose, delirium, toxicologic, and neurologic screening before any presentation is treated as primary psychiatric illness.

⚠ New psychosis is medical until vitals, glucose, delirium, tox, and neurologic red flags are addressed. The 5 do-not-miss: delirium from a medical cause, CNS infection (encephalitis, incl. autoimmune/anti-NMDA), toxidrome/withdrawal, hypoglycemia/hypoxia/metabolic disease, and intracranial catastrophe/NMS.
⚠ Pick the first dangerous lane before assuming primary psychosis. Screen every new-psychosis patient for unsafe agitation, medical/delirium/tox red flags, neuro/CNS red flags, catatonia, and mood/postpartum risk.
⚠ Stabilize safety first, but do not let sedation replace the medical evaluation — glucose, vitals, temperature, and delirium/tox/hypoxia/hypoglycemia/head-injury/sepsis/withdrawal screening still apply during agitation control.
⚠ If attention, vitals, tox, glucose, or medical stability is abnormal, treat as medical/delirium until proven otherwise — new-onset or red-flag psychosis is not a low-risk psych-only presentation.
⚠ Psychosis with seizure, decreased consciousness, dyskinesia, autonomic instability, fever, meningismus, focal deficit, or papilledema needs a neuro/CNS-infection/structural workup, not routine psych triage.
⚠ Autoimmune encephalitis red flags should block routine psychiatric disposition — rapid progression under 3 months, seizures, movement disorder, mutism, decreased consciousness, autonomic instability, catatonia, or hyponatremia: get neurology early, MRI, EEG, LP; do not rely on serum antibody alone when suspicion is high.
⚠ Do NOT delay empiric antibiotics/acyclovir for CT/LP logistics when CNS infection is plausible — psychosis can be an early encephalitis presentation.
⚠ Neuro signs or late/atypical first psychosis deserve brain imaging, not routine psych clearance.
⚠ Catatonia is a medical-psychiatric emergency, not simple agitation. Avoid reflex antipsychotic escalation when catatonia is possible — a lorazepam challenge and medical evaluation are often the safer first diagnostic move.
⚠ Postpartum psychosis is high-risk and generally requires admission-level safety planning — rule out preeclampsia/eclampsia, thyroid disease, infection, and substance/medication exposure as medical mimics.
⚠ Default rescue bundle: vitals/temperature/glucose, full medication/substance/collateral timeline, focused neuro/trauma/toxidrome/withdrawal/delirium screen, pregnancy test when applicable, ECG before QT-prolonging antipsychotics/overdose concern/older age/cardiac or electrolyte risk, and SI/HI/weapons/grave-disability risk assessment.
⚠ Medical red flags should not be routed to psychiatric placement as the primary disposition.
⚠ Psychiatric disposition still needs documented medical stability and risk rationale — danger to self/others, grave disability, severe disorganization, or unreliable support before admission/hold.

1 · Sick check first — 5 do-not-miss

First 60 seconds: safety (weapons, elopement risk, agitation, SI/HI, command hallucinations), medical stability (vitals incl. temperature, glucose, oxygenation, trauma, toxidrome, withdrawal, pregnancy/postpartum), mental status (attention, orientation, fluctuation, level of consciousness), neuro (headache, seizure, focal deficits, movement disorder, catatonia, autonomic instability), and substance/meds (stimulants, cannabis/synthetic cannabinoids, hallucinogens, steroids, anticholinergics, dopaminergics, withdrawal). New psychosis is a symptom, not a destination.[1][2][3]

Do-not-missClue
Delirium from a medical causeFluctuating attention, abnormal vitals — psychosis is rarely "primary" in the ED
CNS infection (encephalitis, incl. autoimmune/anti-NMDA)Fever, seizure, headache, movement disorder
Toxidrome / withdrawal (stimulant, anticholinergic, serotonin, alcohol)Vitals, pupils, skin
Hypoglycemia / hypoxia / metabolic (hypo-Na, hepatic, thyroid)Glucose and labs
Intracranial catastrophe / NMSFocal deficit/headache, or rigidity + hyperthermia + neuroleptics

Unsafe agitation
Open Acute Agitation and Combative Patient. Immediate priorities: verbal de-escalation, reduce stimulation, staff safety, trauma-informed approach, least restrictive intervention, and medication chosen by likely etiology when medication is required. Even during agitation control, do glucose/vitals/temperature and look for delirium, tox, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, head injury, sepsis, withdrawal, anticholinergic toxicity, and stimulant toxicity. Stabilize safety first, but do not let sedation replace the medical evaluation.[1][2]
Medical / delirium / tox red flags
Open Psych Medical Stability, Delirium, Altered Mental Status Hub, or Organic vs Psych. Red flags: abnormal vitals, fever, hypoxia, hypoglycemia/hyperglycemia, fluctuating attention, disorientation, visual hallucinations, intoxication/withdrawal, anticholinergic signs, severe insomnia with autonomic findings, trauma, immunocompromise, age >45 without prior psychosis. ACEP policy supports targeted testing guided by history/exam rather than automatic blanket labs, but new onset or red-flag psychosis is not a low-risk psych-only presentation. If attention, vitals, tox, glucose, or medical stability is abnormal, treat as medical/delirium until proven otherwise.[1][2][6]
Catatonia / malignant catatonia
Open Catatonia. Clues: stupor, mutism, negativism, posturing, waxy flexibility, echolalia/echopraxia, rigidity, autonomic instability, fever, dehydration, rhabdo risk. Catatonia can coexist with mood disorder, psychosis, medical illness, autoimmune encephalitis, or NMS. Pitfall: avoid reflex antipsychotic escalation when catatonia is possible. Lorazepam challenge and medical evaluation are often the safer first diagnostic move. Catatonia is a medical-psychiatric emergency, not simple agitation.[4][8]
Mood psychosis / postpartum risk
High-risk phenotypes: postpartum psychosis, mania with psychosis, severe depression with psychotic guilt/nihilism, command hallucinations, insomnia with escalating disorganization, suicidal/homicidal risk, inability to care for infant/self. Pregnancy/postpartum psychosis requires urgent psychiatry and OB coordination, safety planning for parent and infant, and medical mimics considered: preeclampsia/eclampsia, thyroid disease, infection, substance/medication exposure. Open Psych Triage, Suicidal Patient, or Eclampsia as appropriate. Postpartum psychosis is high-risk and generally requires admission-level safety planning.[3][9]
First-episode psychosis pathway
Open Acute Psychosis / First Break and MSE Dictation Guide. Core ED work: safety assessment, vitals/glucose, targeted labs by history/exam, substance/medication review, pregnancy test when applicable, ECG before QT-prolonging medication or overdose concern, collateral, first-episode psychosis referral/admission planning. NICE and VA/DoD emphasize early-intervention services, full physical health assessment, and coordinated specialty care after first episode. When no medical red flags exist, first-episode psychosis still needs safety, collateral, and early-intervention follow-up or admission.[1][3][9]
Known psychosis but changed presentation
A known psychiatric diagnosis does not clear a changed presentation. Search for: medication nonadherence, substance use, withdrawal, lithium/valproate toxicity, anticholinergic burden, infection, pain, sleep deprivation, steroids, dopaminergic drugs, pregnancy/postpartum, trauma, delirium, and neurologic red flags. Open Psych Medical Stability, Acute Agitation, or Delirium as appropriate. Changed baseline gets a trigger search before psychiatric placement.[1][2]

Autoimmune encephalitis concern
Open Acute Psychosis / First Break. Red flags: rapid progression under 3 months, psychiatric symptoms plus seizures, movement disorder/dyskinesias, speech dysfunction/mutism, decreased consciousness, autonomic instability, central hypoventilation, catatonia, hyponatremia, headache, recent viral encephalitis, young woman with ovarian teratoma risk. Next steps: neurology early, MRI brain, EEG, LP with CSF studies, infectious encephalitis coverage when clinically appropriate, and autoimmune testing guided by neurology. Do not rely on serum antibody alone when suspicion is high.[4][5]
CNS infection / encephalitis
Open Meningitis / Encephalitis. Next 5 minutes: cultures if they do not delay treatment, empiric antibiotics/acyclovir when CNS infection is plausible, CT before LP only when indicated, LP when safe. Psychosis can be an early encephalitis presentation. Pitfall: do not delay empiric therapy for CT/LP logistics when encephalitis/meningitis is plausible.[7]
Structural / stroke / bleed concern
Open Acute Ischemic Stroke, ICH, or SAH when the phenotype fits. Image now when: focal neuro deficit, papilledema, seizure, head trauma, anticoagulation, severe headache, decreased consciousness, immunocompromise, or atypical late-onset first psychosis. Use CT for emergent bleed/trauma questions; MRI when CT is unrevealing and encephalitis, tumor, demyelination, or limbic process remains possible. Neuro signs or late/atypical first psychosis deserve brain imaging, not routine psych clearance.[1][6]

Labs: targeted by presentation — glucose, CBC, CMP, TSH, pregnancy test when applicable, tox testing when plausible, infectious/autoimmune tests when risk or red flags exist. ACEP opposes automatic perfunctory testing for all psychiatric patients, but red-flag new psychosis is not routine clearance.[1][2][4][5][6]

First-episode psychosis pathway — Normal vitals, intact attention, no tox/neuro red flags. Safety, vitals/glucose, targeted labs, ECG before QT-prolonging medication, collateral, early-intervention referral.

Known psychosis, changed presentation — Search for nonadherence, substance/withdrawal, lithium/valproate toxicity, anticholinergic burden, infection, steroids, pregnancy/postpartum, delirium.

Autoimmune encephalitis concern — Rapid progression under 3 months, seizures, movement disorder, mutism, decreased consciousness, autonomic instability, hyponatremia. Neurology, MRI, EEG, LP early — do not rely on serum antibody alone.

CNS infection / encephalitis — Cultures if they don't delay treatment, empiric antibiotics/acyclovir when plausible, CT before LP only when indicated. Do not delay empiric therapy for CT/LP logistics.

Structural / stroke / bleed concern — Focal deficit, papilledema, head trauma, anticoagulation, severe headache, decreased consciousness, atypical late-onset first psychosis. CT for emergent bleed/trauma; MRI if CT unrevealing.

Rescue bundle: vitals/glucose, collateral, targeted workup, ECG, risk assessment — Default bundle when no single killer declares itself. ECG before QT-prolonging antipsychotics/overdose concern/older age/cardiac or electrolyte risk. Reassess after sleep, sobriety, fluids, glucose correction, fever control, agitation control, collateral.

Reassess after initial workup — Persistent red flags return to exclusions. Stable first episode needs a structured plan. Known relapse, stable, may go straight to disposition.

Imaging / labs / EEG / LP strategy — Targeted, not automatic: brain imaging for focal/seizure/trauma/papilledema/anticoagulation/immunocompromise/late-onset; LP/EEG/MRI for fever/meningismus/AMS/autoimmune/catatonia+autonomic.

Disposition — Depends on medical stability, safety, function, supports, and whether this is first episode.

Medical admission / transfer — Delirium, abnormal vitals, intoxication/withdrawal monitoring, seizure/NCSE, CNS infection, autoimmune encephalitis, catatonia, severe metabolic/endocrine disease, eclampsia concerns.

Psychiatric admission / hold — Medically stable but dangerous to self/others, gravely disabled, severely disorganized, command hallucinations create risk, unreliable support, or medication monitoring needs inpatient care.

Rare discharge — Requires stable vitals/attention, no red flags, no SI/HI/grave disability, reliable support, rapid follow-up, medication plan, and clear return precautions.

6 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT let sedation replace the medical evaluation during agitation control — still check glucose/vitals/temperature and screen for delirium, tox, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, head injury, sepsis, and withdrawal.

Do NOT treat abnormal vitals, tox, glucose, or fluctuating attention as psychiatric — treat as medical/delirium until proven otherwise.

Do NOT rely on serum antibody alone when autoimmune encephalitis suspicion is high — pursue neurology, MRI, EEG, and LP early instead.

Do NOT delay empiric antibiotics/acyclovir for CT/LP logistics when CNS infection is plausible — psychosis can be an early encephalitis presentation.

Do NOT reflexively escalate antipsychotics when catatonia is possible — a lorazepam challenge and medical evaluation are often the safer first move.

Do NOT order every test for every patient, and do NOT under-test red flags — ACEP opposes automatic perfunctory testing, but red-flag new psychosis is not routine clearance.

Do NOT route medical red flags to psychiatric placement as the primary disposition — and do NOT assume a known psychiatric diagnosis clears a changed presentation without a trigger search.
