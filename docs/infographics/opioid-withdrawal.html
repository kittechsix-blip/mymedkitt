Opioid Withdrawal — ED Recognition, Agonist Therapy & Disposition

Adults ≥18 with opioid withdrawal (natural cessation or antagonist-precipitated) in the ED. Excludes neonatal abstinence syndrome and outpatient OTP maintenance-dose initiation. Assumes a COWS score has been obtained and ethanol/sedative-hypnotic withdrawal has been actively excluded before treating as opioid withdrawal.

⚠ Do NOT initiate buprenorphine before objective withdrawal is present (COWS ≥8; consider ≥13 with fentanyl exposure) — dosing too early can precipitate severe withdrawal via partial-agonist receptor displacement.
⚠ Ethanol/sedative-hypnotic withdrawal is life-threatening (seizures, delirium tremens, hyperthermia) and is NEVER caused by pure opioid withdrawal — piloerection, yawning, and lacrimation are specific to opioid withdrawal. If uncertain, treat the more dangerous condition first.
⚠ Precipitated withdrawal (naloxone or naltrexone) can cause a sudden catecholamine surge and hemodynamic instability, unlike natural opioid withdrawal — monitor vitals closely, especially with naltrexone depot.
⚠ Methadone: do NOT give a full maintenance dose to an unfamiliar patient — full mu agonist, no ceiling on respiratory depression. Give 20 mg PO or 10 mg IM only, unless the outpatient clinic confirms the patient's actual dose.
⚠ Fentanyl exposure: do not dose buprenorphine by clock time alone — use objective withdrawal signs, or choose low-dose overlap/microdosing when heavy fentanyl exposure or prior precipitated withdrawal is present.
⚠ QT risk when combining methadone + ondansetron + loperamide, worsened by hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia from vomiting or diarrhea — check ECG and electrolytes before combining.

1 · Recognition & COWS

Opioid withdrawal is rarely life-threatening on its own — but it is profoundly uncomfortable, drives patients back to opioid use, and the resulting loss of tolerance makes subsequent overdose frequently fatal. 5.5% of patients surviving an opioid overdose die within 1 year. The ED may be the only healthcare touchpoint for patients with opioid use disorder — ED-initiated MOUD significantly improves treatment engagement and reduces mortality.

  • History: craving, dysphoria/restlessness, rhinorrhea/lacrimation, myalgias/arthralgias, nausea/vomiting/diarrhea
  • Onset 4–12 hours after last dose of short-acting opioids, 24–48 hours after methadone cessation
  • Exam: mydriasis, yawning, diaphoresis, piloerection ("gooseflesh"), increased bowel sounds, rhinorrhea
  • Vital signs often normal — tachycardia reflects agitation/hypovolemia, not the withdrawal itself; temperature is normal

4 · Fentanyl-specific nuance

Why fentanyl changes the first-dose decision
Fentanyl is highly lipophilic and redistributes into peripheral tissue with repeated exposure — time since last use alone is not a reliable marker of receptor-level readiness for buprenorphine. Mean urine fentanyl clearance is 7.3 days (norfentanyl ~13.3 days), with substantial interpatient variability. Buprenorphine's high mu-receptor affinity can displace residual full-agonist effect and cause a sudden net reduction in opioid agonism — precipitated withdrawal.
  1. Do not dose by clock time alone
  2. Do not start standard induction if sedated, intoxicated, or only subjective craving/anxiety without objective withdrawal
  3. Use clear objective withdrawal signs (mydriasis, piloerection, yawning, rhinorrhea, diarrhea, tachycardia, restlessness) when choosing standard induction — SAMHSA advises adequate withdrawal, generally COWS>12, and notes fentanyl may need a higher COWS with lower initial dosing
  4. If borderline, 2–4 mg SL initially and reassess in 30–60 minutes
  5. If prior precipitated withdrawal, heavy daily fentanyl exposure, inability to tolerate abstinence, or repeated COWS 5–12, use low-dose overlap/microdosing
  6. If precipitated withdrawal occurs, give MORE buprenorphine (commonly 8–16 mg increments up to 24–32 mg total) — this is receptor displacement, not treatment failure

8 · Precipitated withdrawal

Withdrawal precipitated by an opioid antagonist can produce sudden catecholamine surges and hemodynamic instability that may be life-threatening — unlike naturally occurring opioid withdrawal. Management depends on the half-life of the precipitating agent: naloxone (30–90 min) vs naltrexone PO (5–10 days) or IM depot (up to 30 days).

Buprenorphine — naloxone-precipitated withdrawal
16–32 mg SL, matched to the naloxone dose that precipitated withdrawal (e.g. naloxone 2–4 mg → buprenorphine 16 mg, escalate to 32 mg)
SL
Functions as a rapid induction protocol. Monitor for re-narcotization (rebound opioid intoxication) as naloxone clears.
Naltrexone-precipitated — aggressive non-opioid adjuncts
Buprenorphine may be less effective — naltrexone blocks the mu receptor. Primary treatment: clonidine 0.1–0.3 mg PO q1h (critical for hemodynamic control) + diazepam 5–10 mg IV q5–10min PRN for severe agitation/instability + aggressive IV fluids. Consider ICU admission for IM depot naltrexone-precipitated withdrawal.

9 · Disposition

Discharge — MOUD referral
Bridge prescription of buprenorphine/naloxone: 3–7 day supply minimum. Follow-up with addiction medicine/OTP within 72 hours. No X-waiver required since December 2022. Provide a naloxone kit and overdose-risk counseling — tolerance drops rapidly during abstinence, so using the same dose after withdrawal can be fatal. Return precautions: intractable vomiting, inability to tolerate PO, chest pain, seizures, fever.
Admission criteria
Hemodynamic instability (especially naltrexone-precipitated), intractable vomiting/diarrhea, significant dehydration with AKI, naltrexone depot-precipitated withdrawal, concurrent medical illness, pregnancy (obstetric consult), or psychiatric emergency. Inpatient: continue agonist therapy + adjuncts, IV fluids, electrolyte monitoring/replacement, cardiac monitoring if on QT-prolonging medications.

9 · Disposition

Bridge prescription of buprenorphine/naloxone: 3–7 day supply minimum. Follow-up with addiction medicine/OTP within 72 hours. No X-waiver required since December 2022. Return precautions: intractable vomiting, inability to tolerate PO, chest pain, seizures, fever.

Hemodynamic instability (especially naltrexone-precipitated), intractable vomiting/diarrhea, significant dehydration with AKI, naltrexone depot-precipitated withdrawal, concurrent medical illness, pregnancy (obstetric consult), or psychiatric emergency. Inpatient: continue agonist therapy + adjuncts, IV fluids, electrolyte monitoring/replacement, cardiac monitoring if on QT-prolonging medications.

Do NOT initiate buprenorphine before objective withdrawal signs (COWS ≥8; ≥13 with fentanyl exposure) — precipitates withdrawal via receptor displacement.

Do NOT dose buprenorphine by clock time alone in fentanyl-exposed patients — use objective withdrawal signs or choose microdosing.

Do NOT give methadone a full maintenance dose to an unfamiliar patient — give 20 mg PO/10 mg IM only unless the clinic confirms the actual dose.

Do NOT mistake ethanol/sedative withdrawal for opioid withdrawal — seizures and hyperthermia are never seen in pure opioid withdrawal.

Do NOT withhold additional buprenorphine after a precipitated withdrawal event — give MORE (up to 16–32 mg total), not less.

Do NOT combine methadone + ondansetron + loperamide without checking ECG/electrolytes — additive QT prolongation, worsened by hypoK/hypoMg.
