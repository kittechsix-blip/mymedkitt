ED physicians managing extensor tendon injuries of the hand, wrist, and forearm. Zones I, II, IV, V (clean lacerations only), and VI may be repaired in the ED with appropriate training and equipment; Zones III, VII, VIII, and any fight bite require hand surgery referral.

Overview & Physical Examination

Extensor tendon injuries are common in the ED. Unlike flexor tendons, many extensor injuries can be definitively managed by emergency physicians with appropriate training.

Key principles:
- Extensor tendons lack true sheaths, making them prone to adhesions
- Flat, thin distal tendons are more challenging than thick proximal tendons
- Zone classification guides repair approach and referral decisions
- Up to 90% tendon laceration can preserve gravity-dependent extension — examine carefully

ED-Repairable Zones: I, II, IV, V, VI (with conditions)
Refer to Hand Surgery: III, VII, VIII (complex anatomy)

  1. Prepare: adequate lighting and analgesia (digital or regional block); hemostasis via Penrose wrap or BP cuff at 260 mmHg
  2. Digital cascade: compare resting posture to the contralateral hand — loss of normal cascade suggests tendon injury
  3. Active extension testing: test each joint (DIP, PIP, MCP) in isolation against resistance, not just gravity
  4. Elson test (Zone III): flex PIP 90° over table edge, ask patient to extend against resistance — a rigid DIP or inability to extend DIP is positive for central slip injury
  5. Drop finger test: inability to maintain active extension is pathognomonic for complete tendon disruption
  6. Wound exploration: extend the wound if needed, examine with the finger positioned as it was at the time of injury, look for partial lacerations (>50% requires repair)
Examination pitfalls
Up to 90% tendon laceration can still allow gravity-dependent extension — always test against resistance. Examine with the joint in the position it was in at the time of injury. Any Zone V laceration over the MCP joint = treat as a human bite ("fight bite") until proven otherwise. Imaging: X-ray to rule out fracture/foreign body; high-resolution ultrasound is more accurate than exam or MRI for detecting tendon injury; MRI rarely needed acutely.

Zone-Specific Management

Zone I — Mallet Finger. Terminal extensor tendon inserts on the distal phalanx. Closed mechanism: forced flexion of an extended DIP; presents as a flexion deformity/"dropped" fingertip.

Classification: Type I closed (most common); Type II open laceration; Type III open with skin loss; Type IV with fracture (A: pediatric, B: <50% articular, C: >50% articular).

Closed (Type I): stack splint, DIP in 0-10° hyperextension, PIP FREE, continuous extension 100% of the time — any flexion restarts the clock; effective even with delayed splinting.

Open (Type II-III): dermatotenodesis or figure-of-8 suture, then stack splint.

With fracture (Type IV): <50% articular without subluxation → splint; >50% articular or volar subluxation → hand surgery referral.

Zone II — Middle Phalanx. Lateral bands merge with a central slip contribution at this level. Tendons are thin here and may not hold core sutures well.

Partial laceration (<50%): wound care, splint in extension x 3-4 weeks, hand surgery follow-up.

Complete or >50% laceration: may repair in ED with figure-of-8 sutures (4-0 or 5-0 non-absorbable braided) — use horizontal mattress or figure-of-8 rather than Kessler; avoid shortening (can cause extension lag). Splint DIP in slight extension x 6 weeks post-repair.

Zone III — Boutonniere (Central Slip): REFER to Hand Surgery
Central slip inserts at the base of the middle phalanx; lateral bands pass on either side. Complex interaction of central slip, lateral bands, and lumbrical insertion is beyond ED scope. Missed injuries lead to boutonniere deformity (PIP flexion + DIP hyperextension) as lateral bands migrate volar to the PIP axis over days-weeks.

Closed injuries: splint PIP in full extension x 6 weeks (DIP left free for active flexion exercises), then prolonged night splinting x 6 more weeks.

Open injuries: irrigate, debride, loosely close skin, splint in extension, hand surgery follow-up within 3-5 days for operative repair.

Zone IV — Proximal Phalanx. Broad extensor expansion with intrinsic muscle contributions — relatively straightforward anatomy, may repair in ED.

Partial (<50%): splint x 3-4 weeks, hand therapy follow-up.

Complete or >50%: modified Kessler or Bunnell stitch (4-0 or 5-0 non-absorbable); 2 side-by-side figure-of-8 sutures for broad tendons. Volar splint: wrist 30-45° extension, MCP neutral to slight flexion, IP joints free. Hand surgery follow-up within 7 days, hand therapy for early mobilization protocols.

Zone V — MCP Joint. Sagittal bands stabilize the extensor tendon over the MCP; EDC, EIP, EDM present.

CRITICAL: treat any laceration over the MCP from a punch as a human bite until proven otherwise.

  1. X-ray for tooth fragment, fracture, or air in the joint
  2. Copious irrigation — at least 500 mL saline
  3. Wound exploration — look for joint capsule violation
  4. Do NOT close primarily — leave the wound open
  5. Antibiotics: amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg BID x 5-7 days
  6. Tetanus update if needed
  7. Hand surgery follow-up within 24-48 hours

Clean, sharp laceration (NOT a bite): may repair in ED with a modified Kessler or Bunnell stitch (4-0 non-absorbable). Repair the sagittal bands if lacerated (figure-of-8 sutures) — this prevents tendon subluxation with MCP flexion. Splint: wrist 45° extension, MCP 20° flexion, IP joints free.

Zone VI — Dorsum of Hand. Often technically easy to repair in the ED. Juncturae tendinae connect adjacent tendons and minimize retraction after laceration; multiple tendons may be involved.

Repair: modified Bunnell stitch (4-0 non-absorbable) — excellent prognosis with primary repair. Splint: wrist 30° extension, MCP neutral.

Zones VII-VIII — Wrist/Forearm: REFER to Hand Surgery
Zone VII (wrist/retinaculum): the extensor retinaculum covers tendons in 6 compartments — high adhesion risk, retinaculum release/repair requires OR expertise, risk of tendon bowstringing if mismanaged.

Zone VIII (distal forearm): musculotendinous junction, multiple tendons likely involved, muscle bellies require figure-of-8 mattress sutures — complex repair requiring OR.

ED management (both): irrigate, debride, loosely close skin, volar splint in extension, urgent hand surgery referral. Static immobilization: wrist 45° extension x 4-5 weeks.

Repair Techniques Reference

General principles: 3-0 suture for tendons at hand level (Zone VI); 4-0 to 5-0 for finger tendons (Zones I-V); braided non-absorbable (Ethibond, Ticron) with a tapered needle preferred. Handle tendons delicately, avoid crushing with forceps, and minimize excess punctures (adhesion risk). Proximal tendons are round and thick (hold core sutures well); distal tendons are flat and thin (may only tolerate mattress/figure-of-8).

Timing: optimal repair within 5 days (97.8% ROM vs 89.5% if >5 days); delayed repair feasible up to 7 days with proper wound care. If delayed: irrigate, debride, loose skin closure, splint, hand surgery follow-up.

Modified Kessler Stitch — indications: Zones IV, V, VI (thicker tendons that hold core sutures). Locking-loop core suture: enters tendon ~1/3 diameter deep about 1 cm from the cut end, passes through the lateral margin, wraps and re-enters perpendicular 1-2 mm from the cut end, threading to exit at the cut surface; repeat on the opposing half; knot tied buried within the repair. 4-0 braided non-absorbable (e.g. Ethibond). Verify no gap at the repair site before splinting.

Modified Bunnell Stitch — indications: Zones V, VI (alternative core suture). Creates a crisscrossing pattern within the tendon: enters ~1/3 diameter deep, passes diagonally exiting ulnar, re-enters dorsally to exit radial, crosses the initial stitch to exit ulnar (X pattern), mirrored on the opposite tendon end, then tied. Good purchase in flat tendons; slightly more tendon trauma than Kessler. Zone VI: 4-0 suture adequate; splint wrist 30° extension, MCP neutral.

Figure-of-8 Suture — indications: all zones, especially thin distal tendons (I, II, III). Needle enters one tendon end ~3-4 mm from the cut edge, passes across to the opposite end at the same distance, crosses back to create the figure-of-8, tied dorsally or buried; 2-3 sutures may be used for broader tendons. Simple, less tendon trauma than core sutures. Zone I-II: 5-0 suture, 2 buried figure-of-8 sutures often sufficient. Zone VIII (muscle belly): multiple figure-of-8 sutures in mattress configuration through muscle.

Dermatotenodesis — indications: Zone I (DIP) open mallet injuries. A single 4-0 or 5-0 non-absorbable suture enters the skin edge ~3 mm from the wound, passes through the dorsal aspect of one tendon stump, continues through the opposite stump, and exits the opposite skin edge — one suture repairs the tendon AND closes the wound. Useful when the tendon is too thin for a traditional repair. Post-procedure: stack splint x 8 weeks, DIP in slight hyperextension, PIP free. Alternative: standard figure-of-8 tendon repair plus separate skin closure.

Stack splint (Zone I): aluminum finger splint, PIP joint to nail tip, DIP in 0-10° hyperextension, PIP must be FREE.

Finger splint (Zone II-III): dorsal or volar aluminum splint, custom molded — Zone II: DIP extension; Zone III: PIP extension, DIP free.

Volar wrist/hand splint (Zone IV-VIII): plaster or fiberglass, extends from forearm to MCPs or fingertips per zone.

Patient instructions: keep the splint dry; do not remove (Zone I) or only as directed; any flexion at the repair site restarts the healing clock; follow up within 7 days.

Static immobilization (traditional): 64% good/excellent results, higher adhesion rates; appropriate when hand therapy is not available.

Early passive motion (dynamic splinting): outrigger splint holds fingers in extension while allowing active flexion against resistance; better outcomes but requires dedicated hand therapy.

Early active motion (EAM): most cost-effective for Zones III-VI; relative motion splint keeps the injured finger 15-20° less extended than adjacent fingers; ~90% recovery in compliant patients.

ED disposition: static splinting is appropriate for ED discharge — early motion protocols are initiated at hand therapy follow-up.

Disposition & Follow-up

All extensor tendon injuries require specialist follow-up, even uncomplicated ED repairs: hand surgery follow-up in 7 days, reassessment of the repair, skin suture removal (if applicable), and hand therapy referral.

ED Repair Track
Zones appropriate for ED repair: I (closed or open mallet), II (>50% laceration), IV (clean sharp laceration), V (clean sharp laceration, NOT fight bite), VI (any clean laceration).

Prerequisites: adequate lighting/analgesia/tourniquet, appropriate suture material, physician comfortable with the technique, ability to splint appropriately, reliable follow-up confirmed.

Relative contraindications: delayed presentation >7 days (may still attempt if wound clean), significant contamination, extensive tissue loss, associated fracture requiring fixation, tendon retraction beyond the wound.

Post-repair: tetanus update if needed, keep splint dry and in place, elevate hand above heart level, return for worsening pain/swelling/drainage, hand surgery follow-up in 7 days.
Refer to Hand Surgery
Mandatory referral: Zone III (PIP — complex anatomy, boutonniere risk), Zone VII (wrist — retinaculum management, adhesion risk), Zone VIII (forearm — multiple structures, muscle repair), fight bite (Zone V — contamination, infection risk), open fracture (concurrent fixation needed), neurovascular injury, extensive skin loss (may need grafting), thumb injuries, elite athletes, immunocompromised patients.

ED management for referral cases: irrigate thoroughly (500-1000 mL saline), debride devitalized tissue, loosely approximate skin (do NOT close tightly), volar resting splint in extension, antibiotics if contaminated, tetanus update, urgent hand surgery follow-up (24-72 hours). Fight bite wounds are left OPEN.

Complications by zone:

Zone I-II: swan neck deformity, mallet recurrence, skin ulceration from splint.

Zone III: boutonniere deformity — most common long-term complication if missed/undertreated.

Zone IV-VI: adhesion formation (most common complication overall), extensor lag, loss of flexion (impacts grip more than extension loss), tendon rupture (0.2-1.7%).

Zone VII-VIII: adhesions to retinaculum, tendon bowstringing, reduced ROM.

Reoperation: tenolysis for adhesions in 0-17% of cases, higher with static immobilization than early motion protocols.

Prognostic factors: repair within 5 days gives 97.8% ROM vs 89.5% if >5 days; early mobilization improves outcomes; patient compliance is critical; children heal better than adults despite prolonged immobilization.

Volar wrist/hand splint (Zone IV-VIII): plaster or fiberglass, extends from forearm to MCPs or fingertips per zone.

Patient instructions: keep the splint dry; do not remove (Zone I) or only as directed; any flexion at the repair site restarts the healing clock; follow up within 7 days.

Static immobilization (traditional): 64% good/excellent results, higher adhesion rates; appropriate when hand therapy is not available.

Early passive motion (dynamic splinting): outrigger splint holds fingers in extension while allowing active flexion against resistance; better outcomes but requires dedicated hand therapy.

Early active motion (EAM): most cost-effective for Zones III-VI; relative motion splint keeps the injured finger 15-20° less extended than adjacent fingers; ~90% recovery in compliant patients.

ED disposition: static splinting is appropriate for ED discharge — early motion protocols are initiated at hand therapy follow-up.

Disposition & Follow-up

All extensor tendon injuries require specialist follow-up, even uncomplicated ED repairs: hand surgery follow-up in 7 days, reassessment of the repair, skin suture removal (if applicable), and hand therapy referral.

Zones appropriate for ED repair: I (closed or open mallet), II (>50% laceration), IV (clean sharp laceration), V (clean sharp laceration, NOT fight bite), VI (any clean laceration).

Prerequisites: adequate lighting/analgesia/tourniquet, appropriate suture material, physician comfortable with the technique, ability to splint appropriately, reliable follow-up confirmed.

Relative contraindications: delayed presentation >7 days (may still attempt if wound clean), significant contamination, extensive tissue loss, associated fracture requiring fixation, tendon retraction beyond the wound.

Post-repair: tetanus update if needed, keep splint dry and in place, elevate hand above heart level, return for worsening pain/swelling/drainage, hand surgery follow-up in 7 days.

Mandatory referral: Zone III (PIP — complex anatomy, boutonniere risk), Zone VII (wrist — retinaculum management, adhesion risk), Zone VIII (forearm — multiple structures, muscle repair), fight bite (Zone V — contamination, infection risk), open fracture (concurrent fixation needed), neurovascular injury, extensive skin loss (may need grafting), thumb injuries, elite athletes, immunocompromised patients.

ED management for referral cases: irrigate thoroughly (500-1000 mL saline), debride devitalized tissue, loosely approximate skin (do NOT close tightly), volar resting splint in extension, antibiotics if contaminated, tetanus update, urgent hand surgery follow-up (24-72 hours). Fight bite wounds are left OPEN.

Complications by zone:

Zone I-II: swan neck deformity, mallet recurrence, skin ulceration from splint.

Zone III: boutonniere deformity — most common long-term complication if missed/undertreated.

Zone IV-VI: adhesion formation (most common complication overall), extensor lag, loss of flexion (impacts grip more than extension loss), tendon rupture (0.2-1.7%).

Zone VII-VIII: adhesions to retinaculum, tendon bowstringing, reduced ROM.

Reoperation: tenolysis for adhesions in 0-17% of cases, higher with static immobilization than early motion protocols.

Prognostic factors: repair within 5 days gives 97.8% ROM vs 89.5% if >5 days; early mobilization improves outcomes; patient compliance is critical; children heal better than adults despite prolonged immobilization.

degrees extension lag: Excellent — no extension lag 0-0; Good — <10° lag 0-10; Fair — 11-45° lag 10-45; Poor — >45° lag 45-60

Suture selection: hand (Zone VI) 3-0 to 4-0 braided non-absorbable; fingers (Zones I-V) 4-0 to 5-0 braided non-absorbable.

Universal follow-up: ALL injuries need hand surgery follow-up within 7 days, plus hand therapy referral for rehabilitation.

Timing: repair within 5 days is optimal; delayed repair up to 7 days is acceptable with good wound care.

Management Algorithm

Extensor Tendon Injury — ED-repairable zones: I, II, IV, V, VI. Refer zones III, VII, VIII to hand surgery.

Physical exam: extension vs. resistance, Elson test, wound exploration — Test extension against resistance at each joint — up to 90% laceration can still allow gravity-dependent extension. Zone V over the MCP = fight bite until proven otherwise.

Determine zone (I-VIII) — Odd zones overlie joints, even zones between joints — classification guides repair and referral. The <50%/≥50% laceration-extent question only changes management within Zone II/IV; other zones are referred or repaired based on zone/mechanism regardless of extent.

Zone I: Mallet finger — stack splint / dermatotenodesis — Mallet finger: stack splint DIP in hyperextension x 8 weeks continuous — PIP must be free.

Zone II: figure-of-8 if >50% laceration — Zone II: <50% laceration — splint in extension 3-4 weeks, hand surgery follow-up, no repair needed. ≥50% or complete — figure-of-8 sutures, splint DIP in extension x 6 weeks.

Zone III: Boutonniere — REFER — Zone III (boutonniere): REFER to hand surgery regardless of laceration extent — missed injuries cause PIP flexion + DIP hyperextension.

Zone IV: Kessler / Bunnell repair — Zone IV: <50% laceration — splint 3-4 weeks, hand therapy follow-up, no repair needed. ≥50% or complete — modified Kessler or Bunnell stitch, wrist 30-45° ext, MCP neutral splint x 6 weeks.

Zone V: punch mechanism? — CRITICAL: treat as human bite until proven otherwise — any MCP laceration from a punch is contaminated until proven otherwise, regardless of laceration extent.

Zone VI: dorsum hand — Bunnell repair — Zone VI: ED-repairable — modified Bunnell, wrist 30° ext, MCP neutral.

Zones VII-VIII: REFER — Zones VII-VIII: REFER regardless of laceration extent — retinaculum/muscle-tendon junction complexity.

Fight bite: irrigate, leave OPEN, antibiotics, refer — Fight bite: ANY laceration from a punch = fight bite — irrigate, do NOT close, antibiotics, hand surgery within 24-48h.

Clean laceration: Kessler/Bunnell + sagittal band repair — Clean, sharp laceration (not bite): repair Kessler/Bunnell, repair sagittal bands, splint wrist 45° ext / MCP 20° flexion.

ED Repair Track — ED repair zones I, II, IV, V (not bite), VI — confirm adequate equipment and reliable follow-up.

Refer to Hand Surgery — Refer zones III, VII, VIII, fight bites, open fractures — irrigate, splint, urgent hand surgery 24-72h.

Disposition: hand surgery F/U + hand therapy — ALL extensor tendon injuries need hand surgery follow-up within 7 days.

Repair techniques overview — Use 4-0/5-0 braided non-absorbable suture — repair within 5 days for best outcomes (97.8% ROM).

Modified Kessler — Modified Kessler: locking loop core suture for zones IV-VI, knot buried between tendon ends.

Modified Bunnell — Modified Bunnell: crisscross pattern, good purchase in flat tendons — zones V-VI.

Figure-of-8 — Figure-of-8: simplest technique, works for all zones especially thin distal tendons.

Dermatotenodesis — Dermatotenodesis: combined skin + tendon repair for Zone I open mallet injuries.

Splinting protocols by zone — Continuous extension critical — any flexion at the repair site disrupts healing and restarts the clock.

Splint types & application — Stack splint for Zone I, finger splint for II-III, volar wrist/hand splint for IV-VIII.

Early motion protocols — Static splinting for ED discharge — early motion protocols initiated at hand therapy follow-up.
