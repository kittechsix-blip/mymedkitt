Culture Positive Results in the ED — Callback Management

Adults ≥18, ED clinician managing a positive culture callback on a patient already discharged from (or being reassessed in) the ED. Covers blood, urine (clean catch), Foley/indwelling catheter, wound, sputum, stool/C. difficile, CSF, and throat sources. Excludes pediatric dosing (weight-based calculators shown reflect the adult indications the tree cites) and excludes purely inpatient/ICU-only decisions beyond the initial recall/admit disposition.

⚠ S. aureus bacteremia (MSSA or MRSA) is NEVER a contaminant — recall to ED now, admit, TTE within 5-7 days, ID consult (mortality 20-30% untreated).
⚠ Candidemia — recall, admit, echinocandin empiric, ophthalmology consult (endophthalmitis screen within 1 week), ID consult, remove central lines. Mortality 30-40%.
⚠ STEC / E. coli O157:H7 — antibiotics and antimotility agents INCREASE HUS risk. NO antibiotics, NO loperamide. Supportive care + CBC/BMP at 5-7 days.
⚠ Asymptomatic bacteriuria — DO NOT treat, except pregnancy or a planned urologic procedure with mucosal trauma. Treatment does not improve outcomes and drives C. difficile + resistance.
⚠ Asymptomatic catheter bacteriuria — DO NOT treat. Cloudy or foul-smelling urine and pyuria alone are NOT symptoms of CAUTI.
⚠ ESBL bacteremia — use a carbapenem. Avoid piperacillin-tazobactam even when labeled susceptible on the antibiogram (MERINO trial: inferior outcomes).
⚠ CSF culture positive — always admit, no exceptions. Empiric vancomycin + ceftriaxone; add ampicillin if age ≥50, pregnant, or immunocompromised (Listeria is NOT covered by cephalosporins).
⚠ GAS pharyngitis with a positive culture — treat even if the rapid antigen test was negative, to prevent rheumatic fever (highest risk <25 years).
⚠ C. difficile initial episode — fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin are first-line. Metronidazole is second-line / not recommended for adults.
⚠ CAUTI — replace (or remove) the catheter before starting antibiotics if it has been in place >2 weeks; biofilm on the old catheter causes treatment failure.

Source triage — universal first questions

Lab called with a positive culture on a patient previously seen in the ED. Ask, for every source: What is the source? Is the organism a likely pathogen or a contaminant? Is the patient symptomatic now? Are they on adequate antibiotic coverage? Any special host factors (pregnant, immunocompromised, indwelling device)?

Blood cultures

Almost always a true pathogen (>90%): S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, GAS, E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Candida, Listeria, N. meningitidis, Salmonella.

Usually a contaminant (<10% unless context): CoNS, Corynebacterium, Bacillus, Cutibacterium (P. acnes), Micrococcus, single-bottle viridans strep.

S. aureus bacteremia is a never-miss diagnosis — mortality 20-30% untreated. Recall to ED immediately (do NOT defer to PCP). Admission is mandatory for all SAB.

Workup: repeat blood cultures q48h until clear (defines duration of bacteremia); TTE within 5-7 days (TEE if persistent bacteremia, prosthetic valve, IVDU, or suspicious lesions); ID consult is standard of care (~50% mortality reduction); source hunt (line removal if catheter-related, drain abscess, image spine if back pain).

Streptococcal bacteremia (pneumo / GAS / GBS / viridans)

Source-directed treatment. S. pneumoniae bacteremia: usually from CAP or meningitis source; ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h covers most strains; add vancomycin if meningitis cannot be excluded; recall + admit unless rapidly improving on PO amoxicillin for known CAP.

Group A strep: high virulence — recall + admit; penicillin G IV + clindamycin (toxin suppression); hunt for necrotizing infection, emergency surgical consult if any concern.

Group B strep: always a pathogen in adults; source UTI/SSTI/endocarditis; ampicillin or ceftriaxone.

Viridans group: single bottle + well + no endocarditis risk = possible contaminant; multiple bottles, dental work, or valve disease = endocarditis workup + TTE.

  • Recall + admit (except clearly improving CAP with pneumococcal sensitivity)
  • Ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h covers most
  • GAS: penicillin G + clindamycin, screen for necrotizing infection
  • Viridans + multiple bottles or risk factors: TTE
  • ID consult for endocarditis suspicion
Likely contaminant (CoNS, single bottle, stable, no device)

Cues for contamination: single bottle out of 2-4 drawn, time to positivity >14h, patient now well/no fevers, no indwelling lines or prosthesis, not immunocompromised, organism is CoNS (NOT S. lugdunensis), Corynebacterium, Bacillus, P. acnes, or Micrococcus.

Cues for TRUE bloodstream infection (treat as pathogen): multiple bottles positive with same organism, S. lugdunensis identified (treat like S. aureus), indwelling line/prosthesis/immunocompromised/≥3 SIRS criteria, time to positivity <14h.

  • Phone contact, advise return if new symptoms
  • Notify PCP
  • Do NOT start antibiotics
  • Repeat cultures only if symptomatic
Other blood pathogens — Pseudomonas, Listeria, anaerobes, N. meningitidis
Listeria — cephalosporins do NOT cover it
Pregnant, >50, and immunocompromised patients are at highest risk. Recall + admit. Ampicillin 2g IV q4h (or TMP-SMX if PCN-allergic) — cephalosporins have no activity against L. monocytogenes.

Pseudomonas: recall + admit; empiric pip-tazo 4.5g IV q6h or cefepime 2g IV q8h; hunt source (healthcare exposure, neutropenia, structural lung disease).

Anaerobes (Bacteroides, Clostridium): indicates intra-abdominal or deep tissue source; recall + admit + imaging + surgery consult; pip-tazo or metronidazole-containing regimen.

N. meningitidis: emergency — recall, admit, isolate, droplet precautions; ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h; public health notification; prophylaxis for close contacts.

Urine cultures (clean catch)

The single most important question for urine culture interpretation is symptomatic status. Symptomatic → treat by syndrome. Asymptomatic → do NOT treat, with two exceptions: pregnancy and a planned urologic procedure with mucosal trauma.

Treat ALL asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy
Untreated ASB in pregnancy → ~25% progress to pyelonephritis, plus low birth weight and preterm delivery risk. Test of cure 1-2 weeks post-treatment is recommended.

Cystitis: dysuria, frequency, suprapubic discomfort, urgency — no flank pain, no fever. Pyelonephritis: flank pain, fever, rigors, nausea/vomiting, costovertebral angle tenderness.

Pre-urologic procedure ASB — treat
Treat ASB before any urologic procedure with mucosal trauma (TURP, ureteroscopy, stone manipulation): short course (3-5 days) timed to complete before the procedure, per susceptibilities. Do NOT treat for non-mucosal procedures.

Foley / indwelling catheter urine

CAUTI diagnosis requires SYMPTOMS plus ≥10³ CFU/mL. Symptoms include fever, suprapubic pain, costovertebral tenderness, acute hematuria, new altered mental status (in cognitively intact patients). Symptoms do NOT include cloudy/foul-smelling urine alone or pyuria alone.

Do NOT treat asymptomatic catheter bacteriuria
Bacteriuria develops at 3-10% per catheter-day; by 30 days, 100% of catheterized patients are colonized. Treating does not reduce symptomatic CAUTI and drives C. difficile + resistance. Exceptions (rare): pre-urologic procedure with mucosal trauma, pregnancy, confirmed CAUTI symptoms develop. Action: no antibiotics; consider catheter removal if no longer indicated (most important intervention).

Step 1 — REPLACE the catheter before treatment if it has been in place >2 weeks (biofilm reservoir causes treatment failure); send culture from the NEW catheter, not the old one. Consider catheter discontinuation altogether if possible — the most effective intervention.

Wound cultures

Surface wound swabs often show colonizers, not pathogens
If the patient is improving on empiric therapy and the culture shows an unexpected organism → continue current therapy. Recall and change ONLY if: not improving, a clearly resistant organism (e.g., MRSA on a penicillin), or an unusual pathogen.

Sputum cultures

IDSA/ATS 2019 CAP guideline: routine sputum cultures are NOT recommended in outpatients. Useful in: severe CAP, treatment failure, HCAP, structural lung disease (CF, bronchiectasis), suspected resistant organism.

Interpretation: heavy growth of a single organism + >25 PMN + <10 epithelial cells/LPF = likely pathogen. Mixed flora, few PMN, many epithelial cells = oral contamination. Candida in sputum is almost never a pathogen — do NOT treat.

Improving on empiric therapy

Continue current therapy. Most outpatient CAP improves on amoxicillin 1g TID or doxycycline regardless of sputum result — culture rarely changes management when the patient is improving. Document phone contact + return precautions.

NOT improving

Recall and reassess: repeat exam + vitals + chest imaging, reassess severity (CURB-65, PSI), consider admission, check culture susceptibilities.

  • Atypical pathogen not covered (mycoplasma, chlamydia, Legionella) — add macrolide or doxycycline
  • Resistant pneumococcus — switch to levofloxacin
  • Wrong diagnosis (PE, heart failure, malignancy)
  • Empyema — image, drain if present
Resistant organism (Pseudomonas, MRSA)

Pseudomonas: chronic structural lung disease (CF, severe COPD, bronchiectasis), HCAP, or immunocompromised — treat if symptomatic + clinical fit with ciprofloxacin 750mg PO BID or levofloxacin 750mg PO qd; inhaled tobramycin for chronic colonization; admit if sepsis.

MRSA: improving on standard CAP Rx + low colonization burden in COPD = likely colonizer, weigh context. Severe CAP + clinical fit (necrotizing, cavitary, post-influenza) = add linezolid or vancomycin. S. aureus + influenza history = high-risk, admit.

Stool cultures & C. difficile

Bacterial pathogen identification drives treatment decisions. Salmonella/Campylobacter → treat only severe cases. Shigella → treat all. STEC → NEVER treat. CDI → severity-based.

CRITICAL: antibiotics INCREASE HUS risk
NO antibiotics. NO loperamide or antimotility agents (also increase HUS risk). Supportive care: hydration, monitor electrolytes. Monitor for HUS with CBC + BMP at 5-7 days post-infection. HUS signs: hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury — admit. Bloody diarrhea + child <10 = high suspicion, especially during outbreaks.
  1. NO antibiotics
  2. NO antimotility agents
  3. Supportive care (hydration)
  4. CBC/BMP at 5-7 days
  5. Admit if HUS signs (decreased urine output, pallor, bleeding, lethargy)
Salmonella (non-typhi) — usually do NOT treat

Most immunocompetent adults do NOT need antibiotics — antibiotics PROLONG fecal shedding without shortening illness. Treat only if: age <3 months or >65 years, immunocompromised, severe disease (sepsis, hospitalized), bacteremia confirmed, sickle cell disease, or vascular grafts/prosthetic joints. When indicated: ciprofloxacin 500mg PO BID x5-7d (first-line, check susceptibility), ceftriaxone 1-2g IV qd if resistant/severe, or azithromycin 500mg PO qd x7d for FQ-resistant strains.

Shigella — treat all

Treat all Shigella to reduce transmission and shorten illness. First-line: azithromycin 500mg PO qd x3d (preferred — rising FQ resistance); ciprofloxacin 500mg PO BID x3d if susceptible; ceftriaxone 2g IV qd x5d if severe. XDR Shigella increasing in MSM and travelers — check susceptibility.

Campylobacter — treat severe only

Most Campylobacter is self-limited. Treat only: severe illness (>8 stools/day, fever, blood, dehydration), immunocompromised, pregnancy, or symptoms >1 week. Azithromycin 500mg PO qd x3d first-line (FQ resistance ~25% in the US); ciprofloxacin 500mg PO BID x3d if susceptible. Counsel on Guillain-Barré association — small risk, return for new weakness.

Vibrio (non-cholera) — treat severe + high-risk
V. vulnificus mortality up to 50% in cirrhotics
Most common in US Gulf Coast, raw oyster ingestion, salt-water wound exposure. Treat severe disease and high-risk hosts: sepsis/bacteremia, cirrhosis/chronic liver disease, hemochromatosis, immunocompromised, or necrotizing soft tissue from salt/brackish water exposure.

Do NOT

🛑 Critical pitfalls to avoid
  • Do NOT call S. aureus or Candida a contaminant, regardless of context — both are always true pathogens.
  • Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria outside pregnancy or a planned mucosal-trauma urologic procedure.
  • Do NOT treat asymptomatic catheter bacteriuria based on cloudy or foul-smelling urine or pyuria alone.
  • Do NOT give antibiotics or antimotility agents for suspected STEC/E. coli O157:H7 — both increase HUS risk.
  • Do NOT use piperacillin-tazobactam for ESBL bacteremia even if the antibiogram reports it as susceptible (MERINO trial).
  • Do NOT rely on a cephalosporin alone to cover Listeria in a meningitis patient ≥50, pregnant, or immunocompromised — add ampicillin.
  • Do NOT use metronidazole as first-line therapy for adult C. difficile — fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin are preferred.
  • Do NOT withhold treatment for a positive GAS throat culture just because the rapid antigen test was negative.
  • Do NOT keep an indwelling catheter in place >2 weeks and expect a CAUTI to clear — replace it before treating.
  • Do NOT discharge or defer a positive CSF culture — always admit, no exceptions.

Almost always a true pathogen (>90%): S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, GAS, E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Candida, Listeria, N. meningitidis, Salmonella.

Usually a contaminant (<10% unless context): CoNS, Corynebacterium, Bacillus, Cutibacterium (P. acnes), Micrococcus, single-bottle viridans strep.

S. aureus bacteremia is a never-miss diagnosis — mortality 20-30% untreated. Recall to ED immediately (do NOT defer to PCP). Admission is mandatory for all SAB.

Workup: repeat blood cultures q48h until clear (defines duration of bacteremia); TTE within 5-7 days (TEE if persistent bacteremia, prosthetic valve, IVDU, or suspicious lesions); ID consult is standard of care (~50% mortality reduction); source hunt (line removal if catheter-related, drain abscess, image spine if back pain).

Cefazolin (MSSA confirmed): 2g IV q8h IV — Alternative: nafcillin or oxacillin 2g IV q4h.

Vancomycin load — low end (15 mg/kg): 15 mg/kg IV IV — For MRSA or pending speciation. Target trough 15-20; then weight-based maintenance q8h.

Vancomycin load — high end (20 mg/kg): 20 mg/kg IV IV — Upper bound of the load range for higher-acuity dosing.

Daptomycin — standard (if persistent bacteremia on vancomycin): 6 mg/kg IV q24h IV — Standard bacteremia/sepsis dose.

Daptomycin — complicated/endocarditis (upper bound): 10 mg/kg IV q24h IV — Use 8-10 mg/kg for complicated bacteremia or endocarditis.

  • Call patient — return to ED now
  • ED admission orders + ID consult on arrival
  • Repeat blood cultures drawn immediately
  • Vancomycin load if pending speciation, cefazolin if MSSA
  • TTE within 5-7 days
  • Search for source (lines, skin, spine, joints)
  • Duration: 14 days uncomplicated; 4-6 weeks if complicated (endocarditis, metastatic foci, prosthesis)

Always recall, always admit. Mandatory adjuncts: ophthalmology consult (endophthalmitis screen within 1 week), ID consult every case, remove central lines if present, hunt for source (TPN, abdominal sepsis, prior abdominal surgery).

Micafungin (first-line echinocandin): 100 mg IV once daily IV — Alternatives: anidulafungin 200 mg IV load then 100 mg qd, or caspofungin 70 mg load then 50 mg qd. Fluconazole 800 mg load only if non-critical + no azole exposure + susceptible species likely. Duration: 14 days from FIRST NEGATIVE blood culture, not from diagnosis.

  • Call patient — return to ED now
  • Admit, ID + ophthalmology consults
  • Empiric echinocandin (micafungin 100 mg IV qd)
  • Remove central line if present
  • Repeat cultures daily until negative

Gram-negative bacteremia workup: most common sources are UTI/pyelo (60%), intra-abdominal, biliary, line, pneumonia. Key question: is the patient already on adequate empiric coverage AND clinically improving?

Confirm susceptibility covers the organism and the patient is clinically improving (call them). Complete 7 days total (Yahav 2019 — non-inferior to 14d). Return precautions: worsening symptoms, fever. This is a DIFFERENT pathway from the recall/admit route — do not treat it as equivalent to an untreated GNB bacteremia.

Stable, well-appearing, source-controlled → switch PO to a susceptible agent + close follow-up. Sepsis criteria, fever, ill-appearing → admit, IV broad-spectrum per susceptibility. Uncertain → recall to ED for re-evaluation, repeat cultures, decision.

Ceftriaxone (community-acquired, stable): 2g IV q24h IV — Sepsis or healthcare exposure: pip-tazo 4.5g IV q6h. ESBL risk or prior ESBL history: meropenem 1g IV q8h. Severe PCN allergy: aztreonam + vancomycin (if gram-positive concern). Local antibiogram drives empiric choice. Duration: 7 days uncomplicated (Yahav 2019); source-directed beyond that.

  • Recall patient to ED
  • Admit
  • Empiric ceftriaxone (CAB) or pip-tazo (HCAB/sepsis)
  • Repeat blood culture
  • Hunt source: UA, imaging if abdominal/biliary

Recall and admit. Avoid pip-tazo even if labeled susceptible — MERINO trial showed inferior outcomes. ID consult. Source hunt: ESBL more common in UTI/pyelo from healthcare exposure, prior antibiotic use, foreign travel.

Meropenem: 1g IV q8h IV — Standard for ESBL bacteremia. Ertapenem 1g IV qd acceptable for non-critical patients with no Pseudomonas concern.

  • Recall + admit
  • Meropenem 1g IV q8h
  • ID consult
  • Document foreign travel, prior antibiotics

Streptococcal bacteremia (pneumo / GAS / GBS / viridans)

Source-directed treatment. S. pneumoniae bacteremia: usually from CAP or meningitis source; ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h covers most strains; add vancomycin if meningitis cannot be excluded; recall + admit unless rapidly improving on PO amoxicillin for known CAP.

Group A strep: high virulence — recall + admit; penicillin G IV + clindamycin (toxin suppression); hunt for necrotizing infection, emergency surgical consult if any concern.

Group B strep: always a pathogen in adults; source UTI/SSTI/endocarditis; ampicillin or ceftriaxone.

Viridans group: single bottle + well + no endocarditis risk = possible contaminant; multiple bottles, dental work, or valve disease = endocarditis workup + TTE.

  • Recall + admit (except clearly improving CAP with pneumococcal sensitivity)
  • Ceftriaxone 2g IV q24h covers most
  • GAS: penicillin G + clindamycin, screen for necrotizing infection
  • Viridans + multiple bottles or risk factors: TTE
  • ID consult for endocarditis suspicion

Likely contaminant (CoNS, single bottle, stable, no device)

Cues for contamination: single bottle out of 2-4 drawn, time to positivity >14h, patient now well/no fevers, no indwelling lines or prosthesis, not immunocompromised, organism is CoNS (NOT S. lugdunensis), Corynebacterium, Bacillus, P. acnes, or Micrococcus.

Cues for TRUE bloodstream infection (treat as pathogen): multiple bottles positive with same organism, S. lugdunensis identified (treat like S. aureus), indwelling line/prosthesis/immunocompromised/≥3 SIRS criteria, time to positivity <14h.

  • Phone contact, advise return if new symptoms
  • Notify PCP
  • Do NOT start antibiotics
  • Repeat cultures only if symptomatic

Other blood pathogens — Pseudomonas, Listeria, anaerobes, N. meningitidis

Pregnant, >50, and immunocompromised patients are at highest risk. Recall + admit. Ampicillin 2g IV q4h (or TMP-SMX if PCN-allergic) — cephalosporins have no activity against L. monocytogenes.

Pseudomonas: recall + admit; empiric pip-tazo 4.5g IV q6h or cefepime 2g IV q8h; hunt source (healthcare exposure, neutropenia, structural lung disease).

Anaerobes (Bacteroides, Clostridium): indicates intra-abdominal or deep tissue source; recall + admit + imaging + surgery consult; pip-tazo or metronidazole-containing regimen.

N. meningitidis: emergency — recall, admit, isolate, droplet precautions; ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h; public health notification; prophylaxis for close contacts.

Urine cultures (clean catch)

The single most important question for urine culture interpretation is symptomatic status. Symptomatic → treat by syndrome. Asymptomatic → do NOT treat, with two exceptions: pregnancy and a planned urologic procedure with mucosal trauma.

Untreated ASB in pregnancy → ~25% progress to pyelonephritis, plus low birth weight and preterm delivery risk. Test of cure 1-2 weeks post-treatment is recommended.

Cephalexin (safest all trimesters): 500 mg PO QID x 7 days PO — Alternatives: fosfomycin 3g PO x1 (safe all trimesters); nitrofurantoin 100mg PO BID x5-7d (acceptable 1st/2nd trimester, AVOID at term ≥38wk — neonatal hemolysis); amoxicillin/Augmentin if susceptible. Avoid TMP-SMX in 1st/3rd trimester, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines.

Applies to: elderly, diabetics, post-menopausal women, indwelling catheter patients, spinal cord injury, neutropenic patients, ICU patients. Treatment does not improve outcomes, drives C. difficile, drives resistance, and causes adverse drug events. Action: document phone contact, no antibiotics, notify PCP, advise return if new urinary symptoms.

Symptomatic UTI — cystitis vs. pyelonephritis

Cystitis: dysuria, frequency, suprapubic discomfort, urgency — no flank pain, no fever. Pyelonephritis: flank pain, fever, rigors, nausea/vomiting, costovertebral angle tenderness.

Cystitis — nitrofurantoin (first-line): 100 mg PO BID x 5 days PO — Or TMP-SMX DS PO BID x 3 days (only if local resistance <20%, none in prior 3 months), or fosfomycin 3g PO x1. Avoid fluoroquinolones for cystitis (FDA 2016 black box — reserve for pyelo). ESBL cystitis: nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin still work.

Pyelonephritis, outpatient (stable + PO tolerant + reliable follow-up): Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO BID x 7d, or levofloxacin 750 mg PO qd x 5d PO — Only if local FQ resistance <10%. If ≥10%: ceftriaxone 1g IV bridge in ED, then PO cefpodoxime 200mg BID x10d. TMP-SMX only if confirmed susceptible, 14 days. Avoid nitrofurantoin and single-dose fosfomycin (inadequate tissue penetration). ESBL pyelo outpatient: ertapenem 1g IV qd or admit.

Pyelonephritis, admit — stable: 1g IV q24h IV — Sepsis: pip-tazo 4.5g IV q6h or cefepime 2g IV q8h. ESBL: meropenem 1g IV q8h. Admit if sepsis criteria, pregnancy, intractable vomiting, obstruction, immunocompromised, or failed outpatient therapy.

The tree states this admit-pathway dose as "2g IV q24h" at one node vs "1g IV" at the outpatient-bridge node. Drug-store's dedicated Pyelonephritis (adult, empiric IV) indication confirms 1g IV q24h — shown here. See sourceConflictsFound.

Treat ASB before any urologic procedure with mucosal trauma (TURP, ureteroscopy, stone manipulation): short course (3-5 days) timed to complete before the procedure, per susceptibilities. Do NOT treat for non-mucosal procedures.

Foley / indwelling catheter urine

CAUTI diagnosis requires SYMPTOMS plus ≥10³ CFU/mL. Symptoms include fever, suprapubic pain, costovertebral tenderness, acute hematuria, new altered mental status (in cognitively intact patients). Symptoms do NOT include cloudy/foul-smelling urine alone or pyuria alone.

Bacteriuria develops at 3-10% per catheter-day; by 30 days, 100% of catheterized patients are colonized. Treating does not reduce symptomatic CAUTI and drives C. difficile + resistance. Exceptions (rare): pre-urologic procedure with mucosal trauma, pregnancy, confirmed CAUTI symptoms develop. Action: no antibiotics; consider catheter removal if no longer indicated (most important intervention).

Step 1 — REPLACE the catheter before treatment if it has been in place >2 weeks (biofilm reservoir causes treatment failure); send culture from the NEW catheter, not the old one. Consider catheter discontinuation altogether if possible — the most effective intervention.

Acute community CAUTI: Ceftriaxone 1-2g IV qd, or cefepime if Pseudomonas concern IV — Chronic catheter (more resistant flora): pip-tazo or cefepime. ESBL or prior carbapenem use: meropenem. Duration: 7 days if rapid resolution; 10-14 days if delayed response or complicated. Tailor to susceptibilities when culture finalizes.

  • Replace catheter (or remove if possible) before treatment
  • Empiric per host: ceftriaxone (community) or pip-tazo (chronic)
  • Tailor to susceptibilities
  • 7-14 days total
  • Consider admission if sepsis, immunocompromised, or frail

Candiduria — usually do NOT treat

Candida in catheter urine = colonization in most patients. Do NOT treat an asymptomatic catheterized OR non-catheterized patient with positive Candida urine. TREAT only if: symptomatic (fever, dysuria, CVA tenderness), neutropenic, renal/urologic transplant recipient, or pre-urologic procedure with mucosal trauma.

Fluconazole (when treatment indicated): 200-400 mg PO qd x 14 days PO — For susceptible strains. Echinocandins do NOT achieve urinary concentrations — avoid for isolated funguria. Amphotericin bladder irrigation for resistant species when symptomatic. If catheter present + asymptomatic: REMOVE catheter, do not treat.

Wound cultures

If the patient is improving on empiric therapy and the culture shows an unexpected organism → continue current therapy. Recall and change ONLY if: not improving, a clearly resistant organism (e.g., MRSA on a penicillin), or an unusual pathogen.

Cellulitis (non-purulent SSTI)

Non-purulent cellulitis is usually strep, not staph.

Cephalexin: 500 mg PO QID x 5 days PO — Extend if not improved. Add MRSA coverage (TMP-SMX, doxycycline, or clindamycin) only if: penetrating trauma, IVDU, purulent, prior MRSA, or nasal MRSA colonization. Culture growing MRSA + improving on cephalexin/dicloxacillin → likely colonizer/co-infection, consider continue + follow closely. If not improving + MRSA confirmed → recall, switch to TMP-SMX DS BID or doxycycline 100mg BID.

Abscess / purulent SSTI

Add antibiotics to I&D if: systemic signs (fever, SIRS), large abscess (>2cm), surrounding cellulitis, immunocompromised, face/hand/genital location, or failure of I&D alone.

TMP-SMX DS (when antibiotics indicated): 1 DS tablet PO BID x 5-7 days PO — Alternatives: doxycycline 100mg BID x5-7d, or clindamycin 300mg QID (check inducible resistance). Culture MRSA on TMP-SMX/doxy already → continue if improving; if on cephalexin → switch. Culture growing unexpected organism (Pseudomonas, fungus) → consider water exposure/IVDU/immunocompromise, recall for reassessment. Decolonization (chlorhexidine 4% body wash + mupirocin 2% nares BID x5d) for recurrent MRSA SSTI.

Diabetic foot infection (IWGDF/IDSA 2023 staging)

Stage | Criteria | Treatment

Mild | Cellulitis only, ≤2cm beyond wound, no systemic signs | PO cephalexin or augmentin x1-2 weeks, outpatient + close follow-up

Moderate | Deeper involvement (joint, abscess, gangrene, bone), no SIRS | PO augmentin OR levofloxacin+metronidazole OR moxifloxacin; or admit for IV ampicillin-sulbactam/ertapenem; consider MRI for osteomyelitis

Severe | SIRS or sepsis | Admit, IV broad spectrum (pip-tazo+vancomycin OR carbapenem+vancomycin); vascular + surgery consults

4-6 weeks total duration. ID + surgery involvement. Bone biopsy when possible. Pseudomonas in culture: add pip-tazo or cefepime, avoid amoxicillin/cephalexin monotherapy. Do NOT culture clinically uninfected wounds — yields colonizers only.

Bite wound (animal or human)

Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin) — standard: 875/125 mg PO BID x 5 days PO — PCN-allergic adults (non-anaphylactic pathway not applicable here — use for anaphylaxis too per source): doxycycline + metronidazole OR moxifloxacin. PCN-allergic kids: clindamycin + TMP-SMX. Cat bites: prophylaxis recommended (deep puncture, aggressive Pasteurella). Human bites: always treat; hand involvement → hand surgery consult. Dog bites: prophylaxis if deep, hand/face, immunocompromised, or signs of infection.

Culture result interpretation: Pasteurella multocida — augmentin works, doxycycline alternative. Eikenella (human bites) — augmentin works; metronidazole alone does NOT cover it. MRSA — uncommon in bites but switch if present and not improving. Capnocytophaga (dog bites, immunocompromised/splenectomy) — admit, IV broad spectrum.

Sputum cultures

IDSA/ATS 2019 CAP guideline: routine sputum cultures are NOT recommended in outpatients. Useful in: severe CAP, treatment failure, HCAP, structural lung disease (CF, bronchiectasis), suspected resistant organism.

Interpretation: heavy growth of a single organism + >25 PMN + <10 epithelial cells/LPF = likely pathogen. Mixed flora, few PMN, many epithelial cells = oral contamination. Candida in sputum is almost never a pathogen — do NOT treat.

Improving on empiric therapy

Continue current therapy. Most outpatient CAP improves on amoxicillin 1g TID or doxycycline regardless of sputum result — culture rarely changes management when the patient is improving. Document phone contact + return precautions.

NOT improving

Recall and reassess: repeat exam + vitals + chest imaging, reassess severity (CURB-65, PSI), consider admission, check culture susceptibilities.

  • Atypical pathogen not covered (mycoplasma, chlamydia, Legionella) — add macrolide or doxycycline
  • Resistant pneumococcus — switch to levofloxacin
  • Wrong diagnosis (PE, heart failure, malignancy)
  • Empyema — image, drain if present

Resistant organism (Pseudomonas, MRSA)

Pseudomonas: chronic structural lung disease (CF, severe COPD, bronchiectasis), HCAP, or immunocompromised — treat if symptomatic + clinical fit with ciprofloxacin 750mg PO BID or levofloxacin 750mg PO qd; inhaled tobramycin for chronic colonization; admit if sepsis.

MRSA: improving on standard CAP Rx + low colonization burden in COPD = likely colonizer, weigh context. Severe CAP + clinical fit (necrotizing, cavitary, post-influenza) = add linezolid or vancomycin. S. aureus + influenza history = high-risk, admit.

Stool cultures & C. difficile

Bacterial pathogen identification drives treatment decisions. Salmonella/Campylobacter → treat only severe cases. Shigella → treat all. STEC → NEVER treat. CDI → severity-based.

NO antibiotics. NO loperamide or antimotility agents (also increase HUS risk). Supportive care: hydration, monitor electrolytes. Monitor for HUS with CBC + BMP at 5-7 days post-infection. HUS signs: hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury — admit. Bloody diarrhea + child <10 = high suspicion, especially during outbreaks.

  • NO antibiotics
  • NO antimotility agents
  • Supportive care (hydration)
  • CBC/BMP at 5-7 days
  • Admit if HUS signs (decreased urine output, pallor, bleeding, lethargy)

Salmonella (non-typhi) — usually do NOT treat

Most immunocompetent adults do NOT need antibiotics — antibiotics PROLONG fecal shedding without shortening illness. Treat only if: age <3 months or >65 years, immunocompromised, severe disease (sepsis, hospitalized), bacteremia confirmed, sickle cell disease, or vascular grafts/prosthetic joints. When indicated: ciprofloxacin 500mg PO BID x5-7d (first-line, check susceptibility), ceftriaxone 1-2g IV qd if resistant/severe, or azithromycin 500mg PO qd x7d for FQ-resistant strains.

Shigella — treat all

Treat all Shigella to reduce transmission and shorten illness. First-line: azithromycin 500mg PO qd x3d (preferred — rising FQ resistance); ciprofloxacin 500mg PO BID x3d if susceptible; ceftriaxone 2g IV qd x5d if severe. XDR Shigella increasing in MSM and travelers — check susceptibility.

Campylobacter — treat severe only

Most Campylobacter is self-limited. Treat only: severe illness (>8 stools/day, fever, blood, dehydration), immunocompromised, pregnancy, or symptoms >1 week. Azithromycin 500mg PO qd x3d first-line (FQ resistance ~25% in the US); ciprofloxacin 500mg PO BID x3d if susceptible. Counsel on Guillain-Barré association — small risk, return for new weakness.

Vibrio (non-cholera) — treat severe + high-risk

Most common in US Gulf Coast, raw oyster ingestion, salt-water wound exposure. Treat severe disease and high-risk hosts: sepsis/bacteremia, cirrhosis/chronic liver disease, hemochromatosis, immunocompromised, or necrotizing soft tissue from salt/brackish water exposure.

Doxycycline + ceftriaxone: Doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV BID + ceftriaxone 2g IV qd PO/IV — Surgical consult if necrotizing soft tissue. Admit.

C. difficile — diagnosis, treatment, recurrence

Diagnostic interpretation: NAAT alone is insufficient — can detect colonizers. NAAT + toxin EIA positive = true CDI. Symptomatic + both positive = treat. NAAT positive/toxin negative + asymptomatic = likely colonizer, do NOT treat. No diarrhea (≥3 unformed stools/24h) — do NOT test or treat.

Severity | Criteria | Treatment

Non-severe | WBC <15k AND Cr <1.5 | Fidaxomicin 200mg PO BID x10d (preferred) OR vancomycin 125mg PO QID x10d

Severe | WBC ≥15k OR Cr ≥1.5 | Same as non-severe — fidaxomicin or vancomycin PO x10d

Fulminant | Hypotension, ileus, megacolon | Vancomycin 500mg PO/NG QID + metronidazole 500mg IV q8h + surgical consult + ICU

IDSA/SHEA 2021: metronidazole is no longer recommended as primary therapy for adult CDI — use fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin. Stop concurrent offending antibiotics if possible.

Recurrence: first recurrence — if vancomycin used initially: switch to fidaxomicin 200mg PO BID x10d; if fidaxomicin used initially: vancomycin pulse-taper (125mg QID x14d, then BID x7d, then qd x7d, then q2-3d x2-8 weeks). Multiple recurrences (≥2): bezlotoxumab 10 mg/kg IV as adjunct to standard Rx, consider FMT consult, ID + GI consult. SER-109 (oral microbiome therapeutic) FDA-approved 2023 for recurrence prevention.

Treating colonizers drives resistance and provides no benefit. Document phone contact + return precautions for new diarrhea; hand hygiene with soap and water (alcohol gel does not kill C. diff spores).

CSF & throat cultures

This pathway covers the rare case of an LP done in the ED for low-suspicion meningitis (headache/fever workup) with culture turning positive after discharge. Call the patient — return immediately. Admit. Neurology + ID consults. Neurosurgery if post-op or shunt.

Ceftriaxone (adult community meningitis): 2g IV q12h IV — Higher dose required for consistent CNS penetration. Combine with vancomycin.

Vancomycin — low end (15 mg/kg): 15 mg/kg IV IV — Meningitic dosing: 15-20 mg/kg IV q8h, AUC/trough-guided.

Vancomycin — high end (20 mg/kg): 20 mg/kg IV IV — Upper bound of meningitic load range.

Ampicillin (add if age ≥50, pregnant, or immunocompromised): 2g IV q4h IV — Covers Listeria monocytogenes, which cephalosporins do NOT cover.

Dexamethasone (if pneumococcal suspicion — gram-positive cocci): 0.15 mg/kg IV q6h IV — x4 days. Start WITH or BEFORE the first antibiotic dose.

Acyclovir (if HSV suspected): 10 mg/kg IV q8h IV — Empiric for suspected HSV meningoencephalitis pending PCR.

  • Recall patient — return immediately
  • Admit
  • Neurology + ID consults (neurosurgery if post-op/shunt)
  • Empiric vancomycin + ceftriaxone
  • Add ampicillin if ≥50/pregnant/immunocompromised
  • Add dexamethasone if pneumococcal suspicion
  • Add acyclovir if HSV suspected
  • Post-neurosurgical/shunt: vancomycin + cefepime or meropenem instead

Primary goal: prevent rheumatic fever (especially <25 years) + reduce transmission + shorten illness. Asymptomatic GAS carriage: do NOT treat (except outbreak, household contact of rheumatic fever, or recurrent symptomatic GAS).

Penicillin V (first-line): 500 mg PO BID or TID x 10 days PO — Or amoxicillin 1g PO qd x10d (or 500mg PO BID x10d), or single-dose benzathine penicillin G IM 1.2 million units for adherence concerns. PCN allergy (non-anaphylactic): cephalexin 500mg PO BID x10d or cefadroxil 1g PO qd x10d. PCN allergy (anaphylactic): azithromycin 500mg PO x1 then 250mg qd x4d (5-15% macrolide resistance, rising) or clindamycin 300mg PO QID x10d.

  • Treat all positive culture (even if rapid antigen was negative)
  • Penicillin V or amoxicillin x10d
  • Cephalexin if non-anaphylactic PCN allergy
  • Azithromycin or clindamycin if anaphylactic PCN allergy

Do NOT

🛑 Critical pitfalls to avoid

  • Do NOT call S. aureus or Candida a contaminant, regardless of context — both are always true pathogens.
  • Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria outside pregnancy or a planned mucosal-trauma urologic procedure.
  • Do NOT treat asymptomatic catheter bacteriuria based on cloudy or foul-smelling urine or pyuria alone.
  • Do NOT give antibiotics or antimotility agents for suspected STEC/E. coli O157:H7 — both increase HUS risk.
  • Do NOT use piperacillin-tazobactam for ESBL bacteremia even if the antibiogram reports it as susceptible (MERINO trial).
  • Do NOT rely on a cephalosporin alone to cover Listeria in a meningitis patient ≥50, pregnant, or immunocompromised — add ampicillin.
  • Do NOT use metronidazole as first-line therapy for adult C. difficile — fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin are preferred.
  • Do NOT withhold treatment for a positive GAS throat culture just because the rapid antigen test was negative.
  • Do NOT keep an indwelling catheter in place >2 weeks and expect a CAUTI to clear — replace it before treating.
  • Do NOT discharge or defer a positive CSF culture — always admit, no exceptions.
