Awake Intubation: Look Before You Leap

Adults ≥18 in the ED who need a definitive airway but for whom RSI/paralysis is too risky (structural obstruction, distorted anatomy, or physiology that cannot tolerate apnea). Excludes pediatrics and elective OR fiberoptic intubation — each needs a modified protocol.

⚠ Angioedema: RSI contraindicated, double-setup with surgical airway ready — 81% first-attempt success, cric in <5%
⚠ Epiglottitis: maintain spontaneous ventilation, do NOT lay flat or insert tongue depressor — heliox, dex, abx
⚠ Awake VL: confirm cords (Grade 1-2) → tube under vision → full induction ONLY after tube through cords; DO NOT paralyze if Grade 3-4
⚠ Desat: STOP → OXYGENATE → RECOVER → REASSESS; if cannot recover SpO2, convert to RSI with surgical backup ready
⚠ Laryngospasm: remove stimulus → bag-mask → succinylcholine 20-50mg if persists >30s; have sux immediately available
⚠ Cannot visualize: DO NOT PARALYZE — troubleshoot anesthesia/secretions/anatomy; after 3 fails consider surgical airway

1 · Is awake the right call?

The core principle
"Look before you leap." Awake intubation preserves spontaneous ventilation and protective reflexes — you confirm the vocal cords with your own eyes before any paralytic. Overall first-attempt success is 85%; techniques need 15-30 minutes of preparation.[1]
Choose awake when…Why RSI is dangerous
AngioedemaParalysis risks a can't-intubate-can't-oxygenate (CICO) emergency
Epiglottitis / supraglottitisManipulation can provoke complete obstruction
Neck trauma / mass / distorted anatomySurgical airway landmarks may also be lost
Predicted difficult airwayConfirm the view before losing the breathing
Severe metabolic acidosisEven brief apnea causes a rapid, dangerous pH drop

Do this now: angioedema / structural obstruction
RSI is contraindicated. Prepare a double-setup — surgical airway open and ready before any attempt. ED physicians achieve 81% first-attempt success with awake techniques here; cricothyrotomy is needed in <5% of cases. Treat with epinephrine/antihistamines while you prepare.[5]
Do this now: uncooperative patient
The patient cannot cooperate for a true awake technique — run Delayed Sequence Intubation (DSI) first: dissociate with ketamine, preoxygenate 3-5 min while they keep breathing, topicalize if time allows, then intubate.[7]
Do this now: airway bleeding / aspiration risk
Blood and secretions defeat a fiberoptic scope. Use awake video laryngoscopy instead — you can suction and visualize at once, and it is less dependent on perfect topical anesthesia. Position head-up 30-45°, suction immediately available.[2]
Standard awake pathway
No overriding modifier set. Run the standard cooperative-patient sequence: dry the airway → topicalize → light sedation → look before you leap.

2 · Set the patient up

Meticulous prep is the whole game. Open the surgical airway kit now, draw up a paralytic for *after* visualization, and have suction and a second operator ready before you touch the airway.[1]

Dry the mouth first — it is not optional
Wet mucosa dilutes topical lidocaine and gives you a poor block. Give the antisialagogue 15 minutes before topicalization and wipe the mucosa dry with gauze.[9]
Glycopyrrolate (preferred antisialagogue)
0.2-0.3 mg IV
IV, 15 min before topicalization
Does not cross the blood-brain barrier — no CNS effects. Alternative: atropine 0.5 mg IV (crosses BBB, may confuse the elderly). Add ondansetron 4 mg IV to blunt gag.

If you are running DSI for an uncooperative patient, dissociate first with weight-based ketamine (1-2 mg/kg), then preoxygenate while they keep breathing. Enter weight above:

3 · Topicalize the airway

Pick the route by anatomy and by what defeats it. Nasal fiberoptic is the most comfortable and highest-yield in clean airways; awake VL wins when there is blood or secretions.

RouteFirst-attempt successBest for
Nasal fiberoptic92%Most patients; comfort, scope stability (max 7.0 mm tube)
Oral fiberoptic57%Need a larger tube (8.0-8.5 mm); bite block required
Awake video laryngoscopy89%Bleeding / secretions; suction + visualize together
Track the total lidocaine dose
Nebulizer + viscous gargle + MAD spray all add up. The hard ceiling is 9 mg/kg across every route — write it on the board and keep a running total. Systemic toxicity (perioral numbness, seizures, arrhythmia) is the failure mode.[9]
—Maximum total topical lidocaine for this patient
  1. Nebulized 4% lidocaine 4-5 mL over 5-10 min — anesthetizes the lower airway and trachea.
  2. Viscous 4% lidocaine gargle — the patient self-anesthetizes the pharynx.
  3. Nasal route only: oxymetazoline 0.05% FIRST (vasoconstrict), wait 2-3 min, then nasal lidocaine and serial NPA dilation.
  4. MAD atomizer 4% lidocaine to the epiglottis and cords — expect a cough; the cough means you reached the target.

4 · Light sedation — never deep

The goal is light
Patient drowsy but still following the command "take a deep breath," still breathing spontaneously. Never paralyze until you see the cords.

Ketamine is the ED workhorse — it preserves airway reflexes and spontaneous breathing. Titrate in 20 mg IV aliquots every 2 minutes (typical total 40-60 mg). Watch for apnea or laryngospasm at higher doses.[7]

Dexmedetomidine gives "cooperative sedation" with no respiratory depression and a bonus antisialagogue effect — best comfort and fewest desaturations, but slower (needs an infusion). Load over 10 min, then 0.6-0.7 mcg/kg/hr; watch for bradycardia and hypotension.[8]

—Dexmedetomidine load — low end (0.5 mcg/kg, elderly)
—Dexmedetomidine load — high end (1 mcg/kg, anxious)

5 · Look, then leap

The decision point
Get a clear view of the cords and grade it (Cormack-Lehane). Grade 1-2: advance the tube under direct vision, and only after the tube is through the cords give full induction. Grade 3-4 — DO NOT paralyze: troubleshoot anesthesia, secretions, and position instead.[2]

Tube confirmed in the trachea (capnography + auscultation) → complete induction: ketamine 1-2 mg/kg, propofol 1-2 mg/kg, or etomidate 0.3 mg/kg, then rocuronium if paralysis is needed. Enter weight above for the rocuronium dose:

7 · When it goes wrong

Desaturation — the most common complication (12%)
STOP (remove scope) → OXYGENATE (bag-mask / NRB) → RECOVER (let SpO₂ climb) → REASSESS. Apneic oxygenation with high-flow nasal O₂ cuts desaturation to 0-1.5%. If you cannot recover the sat, this is CICO — convert to RSI with the surgical airway ready.[2]
Laryngospasm — act fast
Remove the stimulus → positive-pressure bag-mask with 100% O₂ → if it persists >30 s, give succinylcholine 20-50 mg IV (sub-intubating dose) to relax the cords. Keep sux drawn up and immediately available whenever you sedate.[1]
Cannot visualize the cordsdo NOT paralyze
  1. Inadequate anesthesia? Add topical lidocaine (within the 9 mg/kg ceiling) and give it 2-3 min.
  2. Secretions or blood? Suction carefully; confirm the antisialagogue was given; switch to VL.
  3. Anatomy? Try a different blade, jaw thrust by an assistant, or reposition.
  4. After 3 failed attempts: continue awake with more topicalization, or move to a surgical airway — do NOT paralyze blindly.

8 · Do NOT

The four unforgivable errors4 items
Do NOT paralyze before you see the cords. The entire technique exists to confirm the view first.
Do NOT lay an epiglottitis patient flat or probe with a tongue depressor — either can trigger complete obstruction.
Do NOT use RSI in angioedema. Paralysis converts a hard airway into a CICO emergency.
Do NOT exceed 9 mg/kg total lidocaine. Count every nebulizer, gargle, and spray toward the ceiling.
