IV Potassium Infusion: Indication, Access/Rate, Monitoring, Disposition

Adults requiring IV potassium replacement in the ED/ICU, including DKA/HHS. Excludes patients for whom oral replacement is adequate and pediatric dosing (not addressed in this protocol). Renal failure/anuria and hyperkalemia are contraindications to routine infusion, not a dosing variant.

⚠ IV potassium is a high-alert medication — never give IV push; confirm indication, renal function/urine output, infusion concentration, access, pump programming, and continuous monitoring before starting any infusion.
⚠ Do not give routine IV potassium with hyperkalemia or rapidly rising K+, anuria/severe renal failure without specialist-directed replacement, unknown current K+ in an unstable renal patient, potassium-containing IV fluid already running without a clear indication, or suspected pseudohypokalemia/hemolyzed lab.
⚠ Severe/symptomatic hypokalemia (K+ ≤2.5, arrhythmia, ECG changes, paralysis, respiratory weakness) requires continuous ECG monitoring, concurrent magnesium repletion if low/unknown with arrhythmia risk, and central access preferred for rates >10 mEq/hr.
⚠ DKA/HHS: hold insulin when K+ <3.3 mEq/L and replete potassium first — starting insulin with K+ <3.3 risks fatal arrhythmia as insulin shifts potassium further intracellular.
⚠ Central-line monitored potassium requires continuous ECG, verified pump channel/bag concentration/line tracing, and treats arrhythmia or paralysis as a resuscitation medication with continuous reassessment; avoid routine concentrated potassium through peripheral lines.
⚠ Stop before infusing if there is no infusion pump, the dose/rate/concentration cannot be verified, current K+/renal function is unknown in a high-risk patient, hyperkalemia/anuria/worsening renal failure is present, or the line cannot be traced to the patient. Potassium must never be given IV push.

1 · High-alert medication — start here

Use IV potassium when hypokalemia is severe, symptomatic, associated with ECG changes/arrhythmia, ongoing losses are significant, or the patient cannot take oral replacement. IV potassium is a high-alert medication — confirm indication, renal function/urine output, infusion concentration, access, pump programming, and monitoring before administration.

Core safety rule
Never give IV potassium push. Confirm indication, renal function/urine output, infusion concentration, access, pump programming, and monitoring before every infusion.

3 · What to do now

Severe/symptomatic — monitored IV replacement now
Continuous ECG monitoring. Replace magnesium concurrently if low/unknown with arrhythmia risk. Prefer central access for rates >10 mEq/hr. Typical severe replacement: Potassium chloride IV 20 mEq/hr via central line with continuous monitoring. Recheck K every 2-4 hr.
DKA/HHS — check K before insulin
Potassium determines whether insulin can safely start. Enter the current K+ below to see the band and rule.
Peripheral IV — routine replacement
Max 10 mEq/hr per peripheral line, 10 mEq in 100 mL, infusion pump required, never IV push. Oral replacement preferred when gut function and urgency allow.
Central line + telemetry — higher rate allowed
Common severe replacement 20 mEq/hr with continuous ECG monitoring. Higher rates require institutional protocol, pharmacist/senior involvement, and frequent K checks. Avoid routine concentrated potassium through peripheral lines.
No pump/monitoring — unsafe, stop
Do not start IV potassium until indication, rate, access, pump, and monitoring are verified. Potassium must never be given IV push.
Contraindication — hold routine infusion
Hold routine potassium infusion and reassess indication, renal function, urine output, and current K. If life-threatening hypokalemia coexists with renal failure, use senior/pharmacy/nephrology guidance, continuous ECG monitoring, and very small reassessed doses.

DKA / HHS potassium rules

  1. K+ <3.3 mEq/L: hold insulin; give potassium chloride IV 20-30 mEq/hr until K+ ≥3.3
  2. K+ 3.3-5.2 mEq/L: give 20-30 mEq potassium per liter of IV fluid while insulin runs
  3. K+ >5.2 mEq/L: do not give potassium initially; recheck q2h and start replacement when K falls into range

Peripheral infusion

  • Typical maximum: 10 mEq/hr per peripheral line
  • Common concentration: 10 mEq in 100 mL, infused over 1 hr
  • Use infusion pump; never IV push
  • Burning/phlebitis is common; slow rate, dilute, or use a second line when needed
  • If larger hourly replacement is required, use multiple peripheral lines or central access per local policy

For moderate hypokalemia, oral replacement is preferred when gut function and urgency allow.

Magnesium co-repletion

Hypomagnesemia causes renal potassium wasting and makes hypokalemia difficult to correct.

  • Check magnesium early
  • Replete magnesium when low, unknown with arrhythmia, torsades risk, alcoholism/malnutrition, diuretic use, or refractory hypokalemia
  • Treat torsades or marked QT prolongation with IV magnesium while correcting potassium

Failure to correct magnesium is a common reason potassium replacement appears ineffective.

Monitoring / endpoints

  • Continuous ECG for severe hypokalemia, rates >10 mEq/hr, arrhythmia, digoxin use, or high-risk cardiac disease
  • Recheck K every 2-4 hr during aggressive IV replacement
  • Check Mg, phosphate, creatinine, glucose/acid-base when relevant
  • Track total potassium administered from all sources
  • Resolve symptoms/ECG changes
  • K generally >3.0 for immediate safety, then target 3.5-4.5 based on clinical context
  • Avoid overshoot in renal impairment or when acidosis/insulin shifts reverse
