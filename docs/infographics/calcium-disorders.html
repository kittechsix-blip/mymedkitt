Calcium Disorders: Symptoms Drive the Move, Not the Number

Adults ≥18 in the ED with a high or low calcium. Excludes pediatrics, pregnancy, and primary management of the underlying malignancy or parathyroid disease — those need specialty pathways.

⚠ Give IV calcium now for seizure, tetany, laryngospasm, hypotension, arrhythmia, or a markedly prolonged QT with low calcium — do not delay for a confirmatory number.
⚠ Do NOT give repeated IV calcium for asymptomatic hypocalcemia driven by severe hyperphosphatemia (TLS, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure) — it raises calcium-phosphate precipitation risk. Reserve IV calcium for symptomatic or electrically unstable patients.
⚠ Central line required for calcium chloride — peripheral infusion causes severe tissue necrosis. Use calcium gluconate when only peripheral access is available.
⚠ Correct hypomagnesemia before calling hypocalcemia refractory — low magnesium impairs both PTH release and PTH action.
⚠ Do NOT rely on calcitonin alone for severe hypercalcemia — tachyphylaxis develops within 48-72 hr. Bridge to a durable antiresorptive (denosumab or zoledronic acid).
⚠ Do NOT force hydration in severe hypercalcemia with pulmonary edema or anuria — call nephrology early for dialysis planning.

1 · Which calcium problem?

Anchor on the ionized calcium
Use ionized calcium when available. If you only have a total calcium, correct for albumin and interpret against pH — but do not lean on a corrected number when an ionized level or an abnormal pH is in front of you.[2]
  • Low calcium → grade the symptoms and ECG, then treat or work up.
  • High calcium → grade severity, volume status, and renal function, then lower it.
  • Mixed Ca–phosphate problem → start the workup (Mg, phosphate, PTH, vitamin D, renal function) before committing.

Severity is set by symptoms, ECG findings, and rate of change — not by the total calcium alone.

Severe symptomatic hypocalcemia → IV calcium NOW
Give IV calcium with cardiac monitoring. Peripheral: Calcium Gluconate 1-2 g IV over 10-20 min, repeat until severe symptoms improve. Central or peri-arrest: Calcium Chloride 500 mg-1 g IV over 10 min. Recheck ionized Ca, Mg, phosphate, K, creatinine, and ECG.[1]
Low calcium WITH high phosphate / renal failure
Give IV calcium only when the patient is symptomatic or electrically unstable — do not chase an asymptomatic low total calcium, because IV calcium raises Ca–phosphate precipitation risk in TLS, rhabdomyolysis, and severe hyperphosphatemia. Fix the phosphate and the driver.[2]
Stable hypocalcemia → workup + oral repletion
Patient tolerating PO: start oral calcium plus vitamin D and arrange endocrine/nephrology follow-up by etiology. Send Mg, phosphate, PTH, 25-OH vitamin D. Correct low Mg before labeling it refractory.[1]
Severe hypercalcemia → lower the calcium
Corrected Ca >14 mg/dL or symptomatic: stop calcium/vitamin D/thiazides/lithium, give isotonic crystalloid if tolerated, bridge with Calcitonin 4 IU/kg SC/IM q12h, and start durable antiresorptive therapy (Denosumab or Zoledronic Acid). Renal failure or volume overload that blocks safe hydration → call nephrology for dialysis.[3][5]
Stable hypercalcemia → let PTH split it
Repeat Ca/ionized Ca, albumin, creatinine, phosphate, Mg, ECG, PTH, and review meds. PTH high/inappropriately normal → hyperparathyroidism, lithium, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia. PTH suppressed → malignancy/PTHrP, calcitriol-mediated or granulomatous disease, thyrotoxicosis, milk-alkali.[4]
High calcium WITH renal failure / volume overload
Hydration may be unsafe. Severe hypercalcemia with renal failure, anuria, volume overload, or neurologic/cardiac toxicity may require urgent hemodialysis with nephrology co-management; otherwise treat per severity (fluids as tolerated, calcitonin bridge, durable antiresorptive).[2][6]

4 · Severe hypercalcemia — the sequence

Severe = corrected Ca >14 mg/dL, altered mental status, AKI/dehydration, arrhythmia, or inability to keep up oral hydration. Run these in order.

  1. Stop the drivers — calcium, vitamin D, thiazides, lithium, and immobilization contributors where possible.
  2. Isotonic crystalloid for volume depletion; reassess lungs, urine output, and cardiac function.
  3. Calcitonin bridge — 4 IU/kg SC/IM q12h; limit to 48-72 hr before tachyphylaxis.
  4. Durable antiresorptive — Denosumab or Zoledronic Acid, guided by renal function and malignancy context.
  5. Etiology-directed care — glucocorticoids help calcitriol-mediated disease; nephrology for dialysis when hydration is unsafe.
