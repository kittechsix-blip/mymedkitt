Typhoid Fever: Travel History, Resistance-Guided Therapy, and Week 2-3 Complications

Patients (adult and pediatric) evaluated in the ED for suspected typhoid (enteric) fever — travelers returning from an endemic region within 60 days, or unexplained prolonged fever with epidemiologic risk (chronic-carrier contact, contaminated food/lab exposure). Excludes long-term chronic-carriage eradication protocols and paratyphoid-specific nuances beyond the source tree's note that paratyphoid is managed the same as typhoid.

⚠ Pakistan/Iraq travel = XDR typhoid risk. Ceftriaxone will FAIL — the only reliably effective agents are azithromycin (uncomplicated) or meropenem (complicated/severe). Do NOT use ceftriaxone empirically for these travelers.
⚠ Avoid fluoroquinolones empirically (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) — >70% resistance in US typhoid cases. Ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and TMP-SMX are also unreliable due to global multidrug resistance.
⚠ Blood cultures BEFORE antibiotics — the gold standard (60-80% sensitivity in week 1). Do NOT delay treatment for, or rely on, the Widal test: sensitivity 63-82% and specificity 18-73%, unreliable in endemic-area baseline-antibody populations, and takes 7-14 days to rise.
⚠ Complications cluster in week 2-3 of untreated illness: intestinal perforation (5-20% mortality), GI hemorrhage, and typhoid encephalopathy (25% of hospitalized cases). Maintain suspicion for new severe abdominal pain, GI bleeding, or altered mental status in any typhoid patient beyond day 10.
⚠ Suspected intestinal perforation is a surgical emergency — NPO, IV fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics with anaerobic coverage, and a STAT surgical consult. Do not wait on imaging if the patient is unstable.
⚠ GI hemorrhage requires two large-bore IVs, type and screen, activation of the transfusion protocol for massive bleeding, NPO, and GI/surgical consultation — most are self-limited but can be fatal.
⚠ Dexamethasone for encephalopathy is CONTRAINDICATED if intestinal perforation is suspected — steroids mask peritoneal signs and can delay recognition of a surgical abdomen.

1 · Recognition & travel history

What it is
Typhoid fever is a systemic infection caused by *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhi, endemic in South Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the Caribbean. 85% of US cases are travelers returning from an endemic area — travel history is the single most important piece of data you will collect.[1][2]

The classic triad (stepladder fever, rose spots, relative bradycardia) is now rare — present in under 12% of contemporary cases. Do not wait for it. Common ED presentation: fever (95%), headache (48%), abdominal pain (43%), diarrhea or constipation (29%), averaging 7.9 days of symptoms before the ED visit. Labs typically show leukopenia (not leukocytosis, 33%), mild transaminitis, and thrombocytopenia.[1][2][5]

Pakistan or Iraq travel — XDR alert
Extensively Drug-Resistant (XDR) typhoid has been endemic in Pakistan and Iraq since a 2016 outbreak — resistant to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolones, AND third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone). Only Azithromycin (uncomplicated) or Meropenem (complicated/severe) reliably work. Do NOT use ceftriaxone empirically. A typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV) was 97% effective against XDR in the Pakistan outbreak.[3][4]
Not Pakistan/Iraq — default state covers two different scenarios
The toggle above only distinguishes Pakistan/Iraq (XDR) from everything else — it does NOT mean "no travel." This default state covers two very different patients: (1) genuine travel to another endemic region (India, Bangladesh, other South Asia, Africa, Latin America, Caribbean) — real typhoid risk, MDR but non-XDR resistance, see the regional resistance table and treatment section below; or (2) true no-travel/unknown — typhoid is very rare in the US without endemic travel; rare exceptions include contact with a chronic carrier, contaminated food from an endemic region, or lab exposure. Re-confirm the exact travel history — do not read this as "travel is unlikely." If suspicion remains high, obtain blood cultures; otherwise evaluate malaria, dengue, rickettsial infection, or bacterial sepsis.[1][2]

2 · Diagnostic workup

Blood culture is the gold standard
Draw 2-3 sets of blood cultures BEFORE antibiotics. Sensitivity is 60-80% in week 1 of untreated illness, dropping to 20-30% after week 1. Bone marrow culture (~80% sensitive even in week 2+) is rarely obtained in the ED but valuable if blood cultures are negative with high suspicion. Stool culture has lower sensitivity but helps identify chronic carriers. Get CBC, BMP, LFTs, and CT abdomen if perforation is suspected.[1][2][6]
Widal test — do NOT use
The Widal test should NOT be used for acute diagnosis: sensitivity 63-82%, specificity 18-73%, cross-reacts with non-typhoidal Salmonella/malaria/dengue, false positives from prior vaccination, and takes 7-14 days for antibodies to rise. It remains widely used in endemic countries but should NOT drive ED management. PCR (82.7% sensitivity, 100% specificity) is more accurate but not widely available. Bottom line: clinical suspicion + blood cultures — do not wait for or rely on Widal.[6][7]

Suspected perforation overrides everything below
NPO, NG decompression, IV fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics with anaerobic coverage (meropenem already covers anaerobes; add metronidazole 500mg IV q8h to ceftriaxone if not on meropenem), and a STAT surgical consult. Uncomplicated, Pakistan/Iraq (XDR) — Azithromycin

4 · Complications (week 2-3)

Complications occur in 10-15% of hospitalized patients, typically week 2-3 of untreated illness. Perforation and hemorrhage arise from necrosis of Peyer's patches in the terminal ileum; encephalopathy from blood-brain-barrier disruption by typhoid toxin.[1][2][11]

ComplicationKey findingRate / mortality
Intestinal perforationSudden severe abdominal pain, peritoneal signs, ~day 10-14Mortality 5-20%
GI hemorrhageMelena or BRBPR, falling hemoglobin~12% of severe cases
EncephalopathyMuttering delirium, disorientation, rarely coma25% of hospitalized cases
Perforation — surgical emergency
NPO with NG decompression, IV fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics with anaerobic coverage (meropenem already covers anaerobes, or add metronidazole 500mg IV q8h to ceftriaxone), STAT surgical consult, CT abdomen only if stable. Multiple perforations in 30% of cases. Mortality 5-20%, higher in resource-limited settings.[11][12]
GI hemorrhage — resuscitate and transfuse
Two large-bore IVs, type and screen, activate the massive transfusion protocol if bleeding is massive, NPO, GI consult (endoscopy if bleeding persists), continue antibiotics for source control, surgical consult if uncontrolled or concurrent perforation. Most hemorrhages are self-limited with supportive care.[11][12]
Encephalopathy — dexamethasone if no perforation
Risk factors: age >50, delayed treatment, dehydration, high Widal titers. Dexamethasone 3 mg/kg IV loading dose, then 1 mg/kg IV q6h × 48 hours (total 4 days of treatment) reduces mortality in severe cases with altered mental status — but is contraindicated if intestinal perforation is suspected (masks peritoneal signs). ICU admission recommended with frequent neuro checks.[10][11]

5 · Disposition

Most uncomplicated typhoid can be managed as an outpatient on oral azithromycin if hemodynamically stable, tolerating oral medications, no complications, and reliable follow-up is available.[1][2][8]

Discharge — outpatient management
Azithromycin 1g PO day 1 (in ED), then 500mg PO daily × 6 more days. Complete the full 7-day course — do not stop early. Follow-up in 2-3 days; return for high fever unresponsive to antipyretics, severe abdominal pain, bloody stool, or confusion. Typhoid is a reportable disease — health department notification required; food handlers need documented clearance (stool cultures ×3).[1][2][8]
Admit — inpatient management
Indications: altered mental status, hemodynamic instability, sepsis/septic shock, GI bleeding/suspected perforation, unable to tolerate PO, severe dehydration, immunocompromised, age >50 or very young, or unreliable follow-up. IV antibiotics per region, IV fluids, enteric precautions, daily labs, ID consult recommended. Duration: 7 days uncomplicated, 10-14 days complicated. Step down to oral azithromycin once afebrile 24-48h and tolerating PO.[1][2][8]
