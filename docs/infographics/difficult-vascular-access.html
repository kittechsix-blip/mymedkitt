Difficult Vascular Access: US-Guided PIV + IO

Adults and children in the ED with delayed peripheral access, failing repeated blind attempts, or an immediate resuscitation-access need. Excludes central line placement technique, pediatric-specific IO landmark technique detail, and non-ED/outpatient access planning.

⚠ Use IO immediately when access cannot wait — a failed US-PIV attempt or an unstable patient should not delay IO placement.
⚠ Do not place an IO through fractured bone, an infected or burned insertion site, the same bone as a prior IO attempt, a prosthetic limb/joint at the target site, or when landmarks cannot be identified.
⚠ Stop infusion through a suspected infiltrated IV or IO immediately. Leave the catheter/needle in place briefly if an antidote or aspiration is needed, assess distal pulses, motor/sensory status, and compartment firmness, elevate the limb when appropriate, call surgery/orthopedics for compartment concern, and re-site access elsewhere — extravasation and compartment syndrome are limb-threatening under high-pressure resuscitation infusion.
⚠ When US-PIV and IO both fail or access is inadequate, escalate to a central venous catheter, external jugular access, the surgical/trauma access pathway, a vascular access team, or an alternate medication route — do not let repeated access attempts delay airway, hemorrhage control, seizure control, or shock treatment.
⚠ IO and US-PIV are bridges, not durable access — replace with durable peripheral or central access once resuscitation stabilizes.

3 · US-PIV: vessel selection + technique

Scan before puncture. Target: compressible vein, straight segment, not directly adjacent to artery/nerve, adequate depth within catheter length reserve, and adequate diameter for catheter and flow need. Avoid tiny/deep/tortuous veins where most of the catheter will not sit inside the vessel.

  1. Sterile gel/cover per local practice
  2. Optimize probe, depth, gain, and needle visualization
  3. Use long-axis or short-axis with dynamic needle tip tracking
  4. Enter shallow enough to keep the catheter in vessel
  5. Confirm wire/catheter tip in lumen before flushing
  6. Secure well — deep US-PIVs fail from dislodgement

Avoid
Fractured bone, infected/burned insertion site, prior IO attempt in same bone, prosthetic limb/joint at target site, or inability to identify landmarks.
  1. Identify landmarks and stabilize limb
  2. Prep skin; use device per manufacturer/local protocol
  3. Confirm loss of resistance and stable needle hub
  4. Aspirate marrow if possible, but absence does not exclude correct placement
  5. Flush forcefully to open marrow space
  6. Use pressure bag/pump for flow
  7. For awake patients, consider lidocaine before flush/infusion per local protocol

6 · Management algorithm

Difficult Vascular Access — Peripheral access delayed, repeated blind attempts failing, or immediate resuscitation access needed.

How Urgent Is Access? — Access choice depends on whether the patient can tolerate more attempts.

Stable Difficult Access — Fewer attempts, better setup: US, warmed extremity, lower probe pressure, longer catheter, experienced operator, vascular access team. Oral/IM/SC route if nonurgent.

US-PIV: Choose The Vessel — Compressible, straight, not adjacent to artery/nerve, adequate depth/diameter. Avoid tiny/deep/tortuous veins.

IO Access: Site Selection — Proximal tibia, distal tibia, proximal humerus, distal femur (peds). Avoid fractured/infected/burned/prior-IO/prosthetic sites or unidentifiable landmarks.

US-PIV: Technique — Dynamic needle tip tracking, keep catheter in vessel, confirm tip in lumen before flushing, secure well.

IO Insertion / First Use — Landmarks, loss of resistance, aspirate if possible, flush forcefully, pressure bag/pump. Lidocaine before flush in awake patients per protocol.

Did US-PIV Work? — Confirm before relying on the line for resuscitation.

Confirm / Use The Line — Flushes without extravasation, no rapidly increasing swelling/pain, stable catheter/needle, expected clinical response. Bridges only — replace with durable access.

Any Access Complication? — Access complications become limb-threatening when missed during high-pressure infusion.

Access Achieved — Secure, label, reassess frequently, transition to durable access. Document attempts, US use, IO site, confirmation, complications, response.

Stop / Complication — Disconnect infusion, leave catheter briefly if antidote/aspiration needed, assess pulses/motor-sensory/compartment, elevate limb, call surg/ortho, re-site elsewhere.

Escalate Access — CVC, EJ access, surgical/trauma pathway, vascular access team, or alternate route. Do not delay airway/hemorrhage/seizure/shock treatment.

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT delay IO when access cannot wait — arrest, peri-arrest, shock, status epilepticus, severe trauma, or a time-critical medication is not a setting for repeated blind IV attempts.

Do NOT rely on US-PIV success without checking vein depth, catheter length, and needle tip tracking — deep US-PIVs fail from dislodgement even when the initial stick looks good.

Do NOT place an IO through fracture, infection/burn, or a same-bone prior IO attempt — pick an alternate site or bone.

Do NOT rely on a weak or absent aspirate to exclude IO placement — flush forcefully and use pressure infusion; absent marrow aspirate does not exclude correct placement.

Do NOT continue infusion through suspected extravasation or a compartment concern — stop immediately, reassess the limb, and involve surgery/orthopedics.
