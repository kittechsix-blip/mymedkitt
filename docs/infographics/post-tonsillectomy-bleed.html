Overview

Definition: Bleeding from the tonsillar fossa following tonsillectomy.

Incidence:
• Overall: 0.1–7.5% (average ~4.2%)
• Primary (<24h): 0.1–1%
• Secondary (days 5-10): 1.5–4.3% — more common
• Lethal hemorrhage: 7 per 100,000

Primary risk is ASPIRATION, not exsanguination
Most deaths are from airway compromise, not blood loss. Children compensate until ~40% blood loss, then decompensate rapidly.
  1. Resuscitation — IV access, fluids, blood products if needed
  2. Temporizing measures — stop further bleeding
  3. ENT consultation — early involvement is critical

Initial stabilization

  • Sit patient upright (reduces aspiration risk)
  • Suction at bedside
  • Avoid manipulation, coughing, straining (destabilizes clots)
  • Large-bore IV access
  • Labs: CBC, type & screen, consider PT/PTT
  • Estimate blood loss

Timing of hemorrhage

Primary hemorrhage is often surgical in nature. Usually from inadequate intraoperative hemostasis; often requires surgical re-exploration; higher-volume bleeds more common. Same initial stabilization, but a lower threshold for OR return and immediate ENT consultation. These patients are still recovering from anesthesia — watch the airway closely. Most primary hemorrhages go to OR.

Most common type — often manageable conservatively. Fibrin eschar sloughs as healing progresses, exposing underlying vessels; peak at day 6-7 post-op. May present with minor blood-tinged sputum or active bleeding — ~50% can be managed without surgery.

Favorable for conservative management: blood-tinged sputum only, no fresh clot, stable vitals, older cooperative child.
Unfavorable: active hemorrhage, fresh clot visible, hemodynamic instability, young/poorly cooperative child.

Late hemorrhage (>14 days) — unusual, consider other etiologies
Possible causes: delayed eschar separation (rare), infection at the surgical site, foreign body, undiagnosed coagulopathy, or (very rarely) malignancy. Workup: careful fossa exam, coagulation studies if not already done, consider CT if concerning features. Manage per secondary-hemorrhage principles with ENT consultation and a higher suspicion for underlying pathology.

Reference — Hemorrhage timing patterns & risk factors (background, not a flow step)

Primary hemorrhage (<24h): usually surgical-technique related; more likely to require OR return; associated with coblation technique and surgeon inexperience.

Secondary hemorrhage (days 5-10): most common type (66% of all bleeds), caused by sloughing of the fibrin eschar, peak incidence day 6-7, may respond to conservative management.

Risk factors for any bleeding: age >6 years (11+ years = 2× risk), recurrent tonsillitis history (4.5× risk), ADHD diagnosis, chronic tonsillitis/tonsilloliths.

Coagulopathy (special population): hemophilia/vWD have a similar primary rate but higher volume bleeds; secondary hemorrhage occurs in 15% (vs 4.3% normal).

Recurrent hemorrhage: 7.1% of initial bleeds will rebleed; average rebleed timing is 9.3 days post-op.

Grade 1 — Minor bleeding

Blood-tinged sputum, no active bleeding visible. Rebleed risk 10.2% — most can be observed.

Initial management: keep NPO, IV access and labs, notify ENT; ice chips may be soothing.

Observation acceptable if: no active bleeding on exam, hemodynamically stable, older cooperative child who can follow instructions (no straining, coughing).

Consider discharge if: observed 4-6 hours, no recurrent bleeding, reliable caregivers, close follow-up available.

Grade 2 — Moderate bleeding

Active oozing, hemodynamically stable. Initial management: NPO, IV access, labs (CBC, type & screen), ENT notification (urgent, not emergent), begin hemostatic measures.

Observation required: minimum 12-24 hours, watch for recurrence — ~50% can avoid OR with conservative measures.

  • Ice/cold fluids
  • Suction gently (avoid dislodging clot)
  • Consider silver nitrate if appropriate
  • TXA (emerging evidence)

Grade 3 — Severe hemorrhage

Active brisk bleeding — prepare for OR
Signs of shock may be LATE in children: tachycardia, delayed capillary refill, pallor, altered mental status, hypotension (late sign). This patient needs OR.
  1. Maintain upright position if conscious; suction ready
  2. Large-bore IV × 2
  3. Type & crossmatch immediately; consider uncrossmatched if unstable
  4. ENT to bedside STAT
  5. Transfuse if Hgb <7 g/dL (stable) or <10 g/dL (active hemorrhage with shock)
  6. Have difficult airway equipment ready; senior provider for intubation

Grade 1 — Conservative hemostatic measures

For blood-tinged sputum without active bleeding:

Cold therapy: ice chips, cold fluids (if alert and able to swallow), ice pack to neck (external).

Positioning: upright or side-lying; avoid supine (aspiration risk).

Avoid: coughing, straining, hot foods/drinks, NSAIDs.

Observation: minimum 4-6 hours; if no recurrence, may discharge with close follow-up.

Hydrogen peroxide gargle — NOT proven effective
3% H2O2 diluted 1:6 with water, 20 mL gargle (do NOT swallow) is common practice but NOT evidence-supported — a 10-year retrospective showed no difference in outcomes.

Hemostatic intervention choice

1. Silver nitrate cautery: 81.4% initial control, but 19.3% rebleed → still need OR. Best for older cooperative children, non-brisk bleeding; NOT for active hemorrhage or fresh clot.

2. Tranexamic acid (TXA): emerging evidence, increasingly used; IV or nebulized; may reduce need for operative management.

3. Direct pressure: difficult in children; may temporize before OR.

Silver nitrate cautery

  • Indications: older cooperative child/teenager; non-brisk bleeding with visible source; NO active hemorrhage or fresh clot
  • Technique: apply silver nitrate stick directly to bleeding site, hold 10-15 seconds, may need multiple applications
  • After successful cautery: observe minimum 12-24 hours; NPO initially, then clear liquids; ENT follow-up
  • If cautery fails: proceed to TXA trial or direct to OR
  • Do NOT attempt if: active brisk bleeding, fresh clot present, uncooperative child, or signs of hemodynamic instability

Reference — Airway management considerations (background, not a flow step)

Most deaths from aspiration, not exsanguination
Fatality analysis: aspiration contributed to ~50% of deaths.

Pre-intubation: keep upright if conscious, suction immediately available, avoid manipulation that dislodges clots, senior provider should intubate.

Difficult intubation risk: 2.7% in the surgical cohort — usually due to blood in the upper airway, not anatomic; hypoxia during induction is MORE common than aspiration in peds.

Approach options:
1. RSI with cricoid pressure (traditional) — standard approach, risk of hypoxia during apnea.
2. Controlled RSI with gentle mask ventilation — low-pressure (<12 cmH2O) ventilation before laryngoscopy; may be preferred for slow venous bleed; avoids apnea, allows oxygenation.

Equipment ready: video laryngoscope, bougie, working suction, difficult airway cart, surgical airway backup (rare but possible).

Reference — Admission criteria (background, not a flow step)

  • Admit if: any active bleeding (even if stopped), Hgb <10 g/dL, required blood transfusion, hemostatic intervention performed, young child (higher aspiration risk), unreliable follow-up or distant from hospital, coagulopathy, or parental/caregiver anxiety
  • Admission orders: NPO initially, IV fluids (maintenance), frequent vitals (q1-2h initially), strict intake/output, serial Hgb if significant blood loss, ENT consultation (if not already), bed rest with HOB elevated

ENT/OR decision — OR if active brisk bleeding, fresh clot + active hemorrhage, failed hemostatic measures, airway compromise, or shock.

Proceeding to OR — Senior anesthesia, RSI + VL ready; post-op ICU if significant hemorrhage. Reop rate 0.92%.

Observation protocol — 4-6h (minor) to 12-24h (active, stopped); NPO then clear liquids; transfuse if Hgb <7 (stable) or <10 (active/shock).

Discharge criteria & instructions — No bleeding 4-24h, stable, tolerating PO, reliable caregivers. Avoid NSAIDs. ENT follow-up 1-2 days.

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for airway-management detail, hemorrhage-timing risk factors, pediatric transfusion thresholds, and admission criteria — background material not tied to a specific decision step in this diagram.
