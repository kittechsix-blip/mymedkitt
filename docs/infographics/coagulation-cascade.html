Coagulation Cascade: Pattern Recognition Over Memorization

Clinicians (ED/inpatient) working up an undifferentiated bleeding or thrombotic coagulation consult, including HIT (covered in full below). Educational pattern-recognition framework covering lab interpretation, reversal dosing, and hypercoagulable workup — not a substitute for hematology consultation or the dedicated DIC, TTP, or Anticoagulation Reversal trees, which this consult cross-links to for definitive management of those specific conditions.

⚠ Five labs — PT/INR, aPTT, platelets, fibrinogen, D-dimer — plus a smear and a medication list answer 90% of bleeding/clotting consults. The cell-based model (initiation → amplification → propagation on platelet/endothelial surfaces) has replaced the old intrinsic-vs-extrinsic cascade — pattern recognition over memorization.
⚠ HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) is a paradoxical PROTHROMBOTIC disorder from anti-PF4/heparin antibodies. 4Ts score ≥ 4 (intermediate-high probability): stop ALL heparin — including line flushes, LMWH, and heparin-coated catheters — do NOT transfuse platelets (may worsen thrombosis), and start a non-heparin anticoagulant. Bridge to warfarin only after platelets recover >150 × 10⁹/L.
⚠ Acquired hemophilia A (autoantibody to factor VIII) presents as spontaneous deep-tissue bleeding in a previously well older adult or postpartum woman, with an isolated aPTT that does NOT correct on mixing study — this is a bleeding emergency requiring stat hematology consult and bypassing agents (FEIBA, recombinant FVIIa), not factor VIII concentrate.
⚠ Do NOT transfuse platelets prophylactically in TTP, HIT, or ITP — reserve for life-threatening bleeding only; platelets can worsen thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP) or thrombosis (HIT). The PATCH trial showed platelet transfusion in spontaneous ICH on antiplatelet therapy caused harm, not benefit.
⚠ PT/INR does NOT predict bleeding risk in cirrhosis — the "rebalanced hemostasis" of liver disease also lowers anticoagulant factors (protein C/S, antithrombin). Do not transfuse FFP to correct an isolated abnormal INR in a non-bleeding cirrhotic patient.
⚠ Pregnancy baseline fibrinogen is 4-6 g/L (vs 2-4 g/L non-pregnant) — a "normal" 2.5 g/L in pregnancy is actually a major drop indicating significant consumption. Treat fibrinogen <2 g/L (not the non-pregnant <1.5 g/L threshold) in obstetric DIC/PPH.

1 · Pattern Recognition — 5 Labs, 3 Questions

The cell-based model
Hemostasis happens on platelet/endothelial surfaces in three overlapping phases: initiation (TF/VIIa), amplification (thrombin burst), propagation (fibrin formation) — this has replaced the old "intrinsic vs extrinsic" cascade.[1] PT / INR answers the extrinsic + common pathway (factors VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen); aPTT answers the intrinsic + common pathway (XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen); platelets, fibrinogen, and D-dimer complete the five labs that answer 90% of consults.[2]

Two questions to ask first: (1) Is the patient bleeding or clotting? (2) Which of the five labs is abnormal, and how? The pattern points to the bucket; the bucket points to the workup and treatment.[2]

Set the presentation above — the matching clue-set and downstream workup path highlight below.

Bleeding-dominant clues
Mucosal bleeding (epistaxis, gum, GI, GU, menorrhagia); deep tissue/joint bleeding (hemarthrosis, muscle hematoma); petechiae, purpura, easy bruising; surgical/line oozing; family history of bleeding disorder.[2][3]
Thrombotic-dominant clues
Unprovoked DVT/PE (especially age <50); arterial events at young age; recurrent thrombosis on therapeutic anticoagulation; unusual sites (cerebral, splanchnic, renal vein); family history of clots; recurrent pregnancy loss.[3]
Mixed — bleeding + clotting
Suggests DIC, HIT, antiphospholipid syndrome, or TTP — jump to the dedicated DIC or TTP trees; this pathway continues through the same lab-workup chain as the bleeding-dominant path (skipping the pattern-matrix reference step) for general lab interpretation.[2][3]

Mucosal vs deep-tissue bleeding splits the bleeding bucket further: mucosal/petechiae → platelet or vWF problem; deep tissue/joint → factor problem (hemophilia).[2]

Anticoagulant counterbalance
Antithrombin inhibits IIa, IXa, Xa, XIa, XIIa. Protein C/S (activated by thrombin-thrombomodulin) inactivates Va, VIIIa. TFPI inhibits TF/VIIa-Xa. Plasmin breaks down fibrin → D-dimer.[1]
  1. Is the patient bleeding or clotting? — determines workup direction
  2. Which of the five labs is abnormal? — PT, aPTT, platelets, fibrinogen, D-dimer
  3. Is the bleeding mucosal or deep tissue? — platelet/vWF problem vs factor problem
  4. Is there a known anticoagulant on board? — warfarin, heparin, LMWH, DOAC, fondaparinux, argatroban, bivalirudin; most "abnormal coags" are drug effect
  5. Is this acquired or congenital? — sudden adult onset = acquired; lifelong/family history = congenital

Mixing study interpretation
Mix 1:1 patient plasma + normal pooled plasma, recheck the prolonged PT/aPTT. Corrects (≤4 sec of normal range) → factor deficiency — order specific factor assays. Does NOT correct → inhibitor (drug, lupus anticoagulant, or specific factor inhibitor). Incubation (37°C × 1-2 h) distinguishes lupus anticoagulant (immediate non-correction, no further worsening — paradoxically thrombotic, not bleeding) from a specific factor inhibitor (initial/partial correction, progressive non-correction over incubation — acquired hemophilia A is the most common). Heparin contamination is the most common cause of a non-correcting PTT mixing study in the hospital — always check for line-draw contamination first.[4]

Targeted factor assays by pattern: isolated PT (corrects) → factor VII; isolated aPTT (corrects) → factor VIII (hemophilia A/vWD), factor IX (hemophilia B), factor XI (Rosenthal syndrome — does not bleed if factor XII); both prolonged (corrects) → factor V, X, II, or fibrinogen deficiency, or severe vitamin K deficiency. Special tests: vWF antigen + ristocetin cofactor activity for vWD; factor XIII assay for normal PT/PTT with bleeding; PFA-100/light transmission aggregometry for platelet dysfunction. A single very-low isolated factor (e.g. VIII <1%) with adult onset suggests an inhibitor; multiple factor deficiency suggests liver disease or vitamin K deficiency.[2][4]

3 · Bleeding Disorders by Pattern

Isolated PT prolonged
Most to least common in adults: warfarin/VKA (check med list + INR); early vitamin K deficiency (factor VII half-life ~6 h, falls first — malabsorption, prolonged antibiotics, malnutrition, biliary obstruction); mild liver disease (factor V usually preserved early); congenital factor VII deficiency; rivaroxaban/edoxaban drug effect. Workflow: stop offending drug, vitamin K 10 mg IV/PO if deficiency suspected (response in 6-24 h confirms), mixing study + factor VII level if unclear.[2][4]
Isolated aPTT prolonged
Corrects (factor deficiency): hemophilia A (VIII), hemophilia B (IX), factor XI deficiency, factor XII deficiency (does NOT cause bleeding), severe vWD. Does not correct (inhibitor): heparin contamination (most common), therapeutic heparin/argatroban/bivalirudin, lupus anticoagulant (paradoxically thrombotic, not bleeding), acquired hemophilia A (bleeding emergency, older adults), dabigatran (TT markedly prolonged confirms).[2][4]
Both PT + aPTT prolonged
Severe vitamin K deficiency (II, VII, IX, X all low); liver failure (factor VIII normal/elevated distinguishes from DIC); DIC (see DIC tree — low fibrinogen, high D-dimer, schistocytes); supratherapeutic anticoagulation; common-pathway factor deficiency (V, X, II); massive transfusion/dilutional coagulopathy; severe hypofibrinogenemia (<1 g/L). A vitamin K trial (10 mg IV) with rapid PT correction in 6-24 h confirms vitamin K deficiency.[2][4]

Thrombocytopenia with bleeding is mucocutaneous (petechiae, purpura, mucosal, menorrhagia). Mechanisms: decreased production (aplastic anemia, MDS, chemo, B12/folate deficiency, viral suppression); increased immune destruction (ITP — steroids ± IVIG; drug-induced; post-transfusion purpura); increased non-immune destruction/consumption (DIC, TTP/HUS, HIT, mechanical); sequestration (hypersplenism); dilution (massive transfusion). Always look at the smear — schistocytes change everything; thrombocytopenia + AKI + neuro = TTP until proven otherwise; thrombocytopenia after heparin exposure = calculate 4Ts.[2][4][7][9]

Mucosal bleeding = platelet or vWF problem. von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder (1% population). Type 1 (75%, partial quantitative) — Desmopressin responsive; Type 2 (20%, qualitative, subtypes 2A/2B/2M/2N) — avoid DDAVP in 2B (worsens thrombocytopenia); Type 3 (rare, severe) — needs vWF/FVIII concentrate. Tranexamic acid is an adjunct for mucosal bleeding. Other causes: acquired vWS (Heyde syndrome, LVAD), platelet dysfunction, HHT (Osler-Weber-Rendu), vascular fragility.[2][11]

FFP dosing (use when PCC unavailable)
FFP is banded by INR: 10 mL/kg (INR 2-3.9), 15 mL/kg (INR 4-6), 20 mL/kg (INR >6) — each unit ~250 mL; 4 units raise clotting factors only ~10%. Cannot achieve INR below ~1.7 regardless of volume. Always co-administer vitamin K 10 mg IV.

5 · Thrombotic & Hypercoagulable Workup

Provoked vs unprovoked thrombosis
Provoked (surgery/trauma/immobilization within 3 months, pregnancy/OCP/HRT, active malignancy, hospitalization, long-haul travel): treat 3 months anticoagulation, do NOT routinely test for thrombophilia. Unprovoked/high-risk (age <50 at first VTE, recurrent VTE, unusual sites, strong family history, recurrent pregnancy loss, VTE despite therapeutic anticoagulation, skin necrosis on warfarin, arterial thrombosis at young age): warrants thrombophilia evaluation, timed 4-6 weeks after stopping anticoagulation.[3][13]
Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)
Most clinically important acquired thrombophilia. Sapporo criteria (revised 2006): clinical (vascular thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity) + lab (lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, or anti-β2-glycoprotein-I IgG/IgM ≥1 positive on 2 occasions ≥12 weeks apart). Triple positivity = highest thrombosis risk. Management: lifelong warfarin (target INR 2-3, or 3-4 in arterial/recurrent disease) — avoid DOACs in triple-positive APS (TRAPS/RAPS trials showed inferior outcomes). Catastrophic APS: mortality 50%, needs anticoagulation + steroids + plasmapheresis ± rituximab.[13]

Other acquired thrombophilias: cancer (Trousseau syndrome) — LMWH first-line; HIT — see below; PNH — Coombs-negative hemolysis + thrombosis (splanchnic); myeloproliferative neoplasms (JAK2, paradoxical bleeding + thrombosis); nephrotic syndrome — loss of antithrombin in urine.[13]

In the ED, almost never order thrombophilia panels acutely — counsel the patient and arrange outpatient hematology follow-up. Testing rarely changes duration of anticoagulation (except homozygous/compound-heterozygous defects, APS, antithrombin deficiency).[13]

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)
Paradoxical prothrombotic disorder from anti-PF4/heparin antibodies. Score interpretation: 0-3 low probability, do not test; 4-5 intermediate — send anti-PF4 ELISA, stop heparin while waiting; 6-8 high — empirically stop heparin, start non-heparin anticoagulant, send anti-PF4 + serotonin release assay (SRA, gold standard). Management: stop ALL heparin (including line flushes, LMWH, coated catheters); do NOT transfuse platelets unless life-threatening bleeding; start argatroban, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux; bridge to warfarin only after platelets >150 × 10⁹/L (warfarin alone in HIT can cause venous limb gangrene).[7]

6 · Special Populations

Liver disease — rebalanced hemostasis
Decreased pro-coagulant factors (bleeding tendency) are offset by decreased anti-coagulant factors — protein C/S, antithrombin (thrombotic tendency). PT/INR does NOT correlate with bleeding risk in cirrhosis — do not transfuse FFP just to "correct" labs in a non-bleeding patient. Patients with cirrhosis still develop VTE — prophylaxis is appropriate. Pre-procedure thresholds: INR <2.5 adequate for low-risk procedures, platelets >50 × 10⁹/L, fibrinogen >1.5 g/L. Viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) better predicts bleeding than PT/INR in cirrhosis. When bleeding: vitamin K 10 mg IV if cholestatic, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen <1.5 g/L, platelets if <50 × 10⁹/L, 4F-PCC instead of high-volume FFP if volume-restricted, TXA for mucosal/variceal bleeding.[8]
Pregnancy — hypercoagulable with shifted ranges
Fibrinogen rises to 4-6 g/L (vs 2-4 g/L non-pregnant); factors VII, VIII, X, XII, vWF rise; protein S decreases; net 4-5× increased VTE risk. A "normal" fibrinogen of 2.5 g/L in pregnancy is actually a major drop — treat fibrinogen <2 g/L (vs <1.5 g/L non-pregnant) in obstetric DIC/PPH. D-dimer rises with gestation, limiting its VTE rule-out value. LMWH is first-line anticoagulation — does not cross placenta; warfarin contraindicated in T1 and near term; DOACs contraindicated. APS in pregnancy: LMWH + low-dose aspirin from positive pregnancy test through 6 weeks postpartum. Hold LMWH 24 h before neuraxial anesthesia (12 h for prophylactic dose).[3][12]

Newborns: vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X) start at ~50% adult level — give vitamin K 1 mg IM at birth; PT/aPTT normally prolonged vs adult ranges. Factor levels reach adult range by 6 months. Pediatric-specific disorders: HSP/IgA vasculitis, ITP (usually post-viral, self-limited), NAIT (maternal anti-platelet antibodies, HPA-1a most common). Pediatric thrombosis is usually associated with central lines, malignancy, congenital heart disease, or severe infection — LMWH first-line; warfarin and DOACs are now FDA-approved for children per hematology guidance.[4]

8 · Pitfalls & Pearls

10 common pitfalls in coag interpretation10 items
"Treating the labs" — don't transfuse FFP for asymptomatic INR elevation in cirrhosis or non-bleeding patients; don't transfuse platelets prophylactically in HIT, ITP, or TTP unless life-threatening bleeding.
Forgetting drug effect — most "abnormal coags" are drug effect, check the med list first; heparin contamination is the most common cause of a non-correcting PTT mixing study.
Missing acquired hemophilia — older adult or postpartum woman with new spontaneous deep-tissue bleeding + isolated PTT that does NOT correct → think acquired hemophilia A; stat hematology consult, bypassing agents (FEIBA, rFVIIa).
Misinterpreting fibrinogen in pregnancy — pregnant baseline is 4-6 g/L; "normal" 2.5 g/L is actually consumed. Threshold for cryo in OB is <2 g/L, not <1 g/L.
Missing HIT — any heparin exposure 5-14 days ago + falling platelets + thrombosis = HIT until proven otherwise. 4Ts score; stop ALL heparin; do NOT transfuse platelets; start non-heparin anticoagulant.
Ordering thrombophilia panels acutely — most acute results are uninterpretable; test 4-6 weeks after stopping anticoagulation, only if it would change management.
Lupus anticoagulant misnamed — causes paradoxical thrombosis (NOT bleeding) despite a prolonged PTT; most patients with lupus AC do not have lupus.
Cirrhosis "rebalanced" hemostasis — PT/INR does NOT predict bleeding risk in cirrhosis; patients with cirrhosis still develop VTE; use viscoelastic testing when available.
Forgetting factor XII deficiency does not bleed — markedly prolonged PTT but no bleeding history → think factor XII deficiency.
Bleeding time is obsolete — operator-dependent, poor reproducibility; use PFA-100 or specific platelet function tests instead.
Disposition
Pattern over memorization. The five labs (PT/PTT/platelets/fibrinogen/D-dimer) plus a smear and a med list answer 90% of bleeding/clotting consults. Cross-link to dedicated trees — DIC, TTP, Anticoagulation Reversal, DVT, PE — for definitive management.[2][4][7]

1 · Pattern Recognition — 5 Labs, 3 Questions

Hemostasis happens on platelet/endothelial surfaces in three overlapping phases: initiation (TF/VIIa), amplification (thrombin burst), propagation (fibrin formation) — this has replaced the old "intrinsic vs extrinsic" cascade.[1] PT / INR answers the extrinsic + common pathway (factors VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen); aPTT answers the intrinsic + common pathway (XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen); platelets, fibrinogen, and D-dimer complete the five labs that answer 90% of consults.[2]

Two questions to ask first: (1) Is the patient bleeding or clotting? (2) Which of the five labs is abnormal, and how? The pattern points to the bucket; the bucket points to the workup and treatment.[2]

Set the presentation above — the matching clue-set and downstream workup path highlight below.

Mucosal bleeding (epistaxis, gum, GI, GU, menorrhagia); deep tissue/joint bleeding (hemarthrosis, muscle hematoma); petechiae, purpura, easy bruising; surgical/line oozing; family history of bleeding disorder.[2][3]

Unprovoked DVT/PE (especially age <50); arterial events at young age; recurrent thrombosis on therapeutic anticoagulation; unusual sites (cerebral, splanchnic, renal vein); family history of clots; recurrent pregnancy loss.[3]

Suggests DIC, HIT, antiphospholipid syndrome, or TTP — jump to the dedicated DIC or TTP trees; this pathway continues through the same lab-workup chain as the bleeding-dominant path (skipping the pattern-matrix reference step) for general lab interpretation.[2][3]

Pattern | Likely bucket

Isolated PT prolonged | Factor VII deficiency, early vitamin K deficiency, warfarin, mild liver dysfunction

Isolated aPTT prolonged | Factor VIII/IX/XI deficiency (hemophilia, vWD), heparin, lupus anticoagulant, factor inhibitor

Both PT + aPTT prolonged | Severe vitamin K deficiency, severe liver disease, DIC, common-pathway defect (II, V, X), supratherapeutic anticoagulation

Isolated thrombocytopenia | ITP, drug-induced, HIT, marrow disease, sequestration

Low fibrinogen + high D-dimer | DIC, primary fibrinolysis, severe liver disease

Normal PT/PTT but bleeding | Platelet dysfunction (uremia, ASA, NSAIDs, vWD), factor XIII deficiency, vascular

Normal PT/PTT/plt/fibrinogen + thrombosis | Thrombophilia or DOAC effect

Mucosal vs deep-tissue bleeding splits the bleeding bucket further: mucosal/petechiae → platelet or vWF problem; deep tissue/joint → factor problem (hemophilia).[2]

Antithrombin inhibits IIa, IXa, Xa, XIa, XIIa. Protein C/S (activated by thrombin-thrombomodulin) inactivates Va, VIIIa. TFPI inhibits TF/VIIa-Xa. Plasmin breaks down fibrin → D-dimer.[1]

  • Is the patient bleeding or clotting? — determines workup direction
  • Which of the five labs is abnormal? — PT, aPTT, platelets, fibrinogen, D-dimer
  • Is the bleeding mucosal or deep tissue? — platelet/vWF problem vs factor problem
  • Is there a known anticoagulant on board? — warfarin, heparin, LMWH, DOAC, fondaparinux, argatroban, bivalirudin; most "abnormal coags" are drug effect
  • Is this acquired or congenital? — sudden adult onset = acquired; lifelong/family history = congenital

2 · Lab Workup — Baseline Panel, Mixing Studies & Targeted Testing

Lab | What it measures | Quick interpretation

CBC + platelet count | Number of platelets, anemia | <150 = thrombocytopenia; <50 = bleeding risk

PT / INR | Extrinsic + common pathway (VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) | Sensitive to vitamin K, warfarin, liver

aPTT | Intrinsic + common (XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) | Sensitive to heparin, hemophilia, lupus AC

Fibrinogen | Substrate for clot | <1.5-2 g/L is consumption or synthesis failure

D-dimer | Fibrin breakdown product | High in DIC, PE, DVT, infection, malignancy, pregnancy, surgery

Peripheral smear | Schistocytes, blasts, spherocytes | Schistocytes → MAHA (TTP, DIC, HUS)

A "normal" fibrinogen in a sick patient may still represent consumption.[2]

Mix 1:1 patient plasma + normal pooled plasma, recheck the prolonged PT/aPTT. Corrects (≤4 sec of normal range) → factor deficiency — order specific factor assays. Does NOT correct → inhibitor (drug, lupus anticoagulant, or specific factor inhibitor). Incubation (37°C × 1-2 h) distinguishes lupus anticoagulant (immediate non-correction, no further worsening — paradoxically thrombotic, not bleeding) from a specific factor inhibitor (initial/partial correction, progressive non-correction over incubation — acquired hemophilia A is the most common). Heparin contamination is the most common cause of a non-correcting PTT mixing study in the hospital — always check for line-draw contamination first.[4]

Targeted factor assays by pattern: isolated PT (corrects) → factor VII; isolated aPTT (corrects) → factor VIII (hemophilia A/vWD), factor IX (hemophilia B), factor XI (Rosenthal syndrome — does not bleed if factor XII); both prolonged (corrects) → factor V, X, II, or fibrinogen deficiency, or severe vitamin K deficiency. Special tests: vWF antigen + ristocetin cofactor activity for vWD; factor XIII assay for normal PT/PTT with bleeding; PFA-100/light transmission aggregometry for platelet dysfunction. A single very-low isolated factor (e.g. VIII <1%) with adult onset suggests an inhibitor; multiple factor deficiency suggests liver disease or vitamin K deficiency.[2][4]

Rivaroxaban | Often prolonged | Variable | Normal | Elevated | Drug-specific anti-Xa, PT can screen

Edoxaban | Prolonged | Variable | Normal | Elevated | Drug-specific anti-Xa

Dabigatran | Variable | Prolonged | Markedly prolonged | Normal | dTT or ecarin clotting time — normal TT rules out clinically significant dabigatran

Warfarin | Prolonged (INR) | Variable | Normal | Normal | INR

R time | Time to initial clot (factor function) | Prolonged → FFP / PCC

K / α-angle | Clot kinetics (fibrinogen, platelets) | Slow → cryo or fibrinogen

MA (max amplitude) | Clot strength (platelets + fibrinogen) | Low → platelets

LY30 | % lysis at 30 min | >3% → tranexamic acid

Platelet dysfunction with a normal count: aspirin/NSAIDs, P2Y12 inhibitors, uremia (most reversible — DDAVP, dialysis, conjugated estrogens, cryoprecipitate), cardiopulmonary bypass, liver disease, myeloproliferative disorders, paraproteinemia. PFA-100 screens (sensitive to ASA, vWD; not clopidogrel); light transmission aggregometry is gold standard. Bleeding time is obsolete — operator-dependent, do not order.[2]

3 · Bleeding Disorders by Pattern

Most to least common in adults: warfarin/VKA (check med list + INR); early vitamin K deficiency (factor VII half-life ~6 h, falls first — malabsorption, prolonged antibiotics, malnutrition, biliary obstruction); mild liver disease (factor V usually preserved early); congenital factor VII deficiency; rivaroxaban/edoxaban drug effect. Workflow: stop offending drug, vitamin K 10 mg IV/PO if deficiency suspected (response in 6-24 h confirms), mixing study + factor VII level if unclear.[2][4]

Corrects (factor deficiency): hemophilia A (VIII), hemophilia B (IX), factor XI deficiency, factor XII deficiency (does NOT cause bleeding), severe vWD. Does not correct (inhibitor): heparin contamination (most common), therapeutic heparin/argatroban/bivalirudin, lupus anticoagulant (paradoxically thrombotic, not bleeding), acquired hemophilia A (bleeding emergency, older adults), dabigatran (TT markedly prolonged confirms).[2][4]

Severe vitamin K deficiency (II, VII, IX, X all low); liver failure (factor VIII normal/elevated distinguishes from DIC); DIC (see DIC tree — low fibrinogen, high D-dimer, schistocytes); supratherapeutic anticoagulation; common-pathway factor deficiency (V, X, II); massive transfusion/dilutional coagulopathy; severe hypofibrinogenemia (<1 g/L). A vitamin K trial (10 mg IV) with rapid PT correction in 6-24 h confirms vitamin K deficiency.[2][4]

Thrombocytopenia with bleeding is mucocutaneous (petechiae, purpura, mucosal, menorrhagia). Mechanisms: decreased production (aplastic anemia, MDS, chemo, B12/folate deficiency, viral suppression); increased immune destruction (ITP — steroids ± IVIG; drug-induced; post-transfusion purpura); increased non-immune destruction/consumption (DIC, TTP/HUS, HIT, mechanical); sequestration (hypersplenism); dilution (massive transfusion). Always look at the smear — schistocytes change everything; thrombocytopenia + AKI + neuro = TTP until proven otherwise; thrombocytopenia after heparin exposure = calculate 4Ts.[2][4][7][9]

Mucosal bleeding = platelet or vWF problem. von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder (1% population). Type 1 (75%, partial quantitative) — Desmopressin responsive; Type 2 (20%, qualitative, subtypes 2A/2B/2M/2N) — avoid DDAVP in 2B (worsens thrombocytopenia); Type 3 (rare, severe) — needs vWF/FVIII concentrate. Tranexamic acid is an adjunct for mucosal bleeding. Other causes: acquired vWS (Heyde syndrome, LVAD), platelet dysfunction, HHT (Osler-Weber-Rendu), vascular fragility.[2][11]

Desmopressin (DDAVP) — vWD type 1 / mild hemophilia A responder: 0.3 mcg/kg IV/SC IV/SC — = computed dose at current weight; raises factor VIII 2-6× from baseline. Max 2-3 doses (tachyphylaxis). Avoid in Type 2B vWD.

Hemarthrosis, muscle hematoma, ICH, retroperitoneal bleed. Hemophilia A (factor VIII, X-linked, 80% of hemophilias) — severity by level: severe <1%, moderate 1-5%, mild >5-40%. Hemophilia B (factor IX, ~20%). Acquired hemophilia A (autoantibody, older adults/postpartum) — spontaneous deep tissue bleeding, isolated PTT that fails to correct, Bethesda titer confirms — bleeding emergency, needs bypassing agents (FEIBA, recombinant FVIIa) + immunosuppression, stat hematology consult. A patient on apixaban with retroperitoneal bleed needs urgent FXa-DOAC reversal.[2][12]

Factor VIII concentrate — hemophilia A, major bleed: 50 units/kg IV IV — = computed dose at current weight; target factor level 100%. 1 unit/kg raises factor VIII ~2%.

Factor IX concentrate — hemophilia B, major bleed: 100 units/kg IV IV — = computed dose at current weight; target factor level 100%. 1 unit/kg raises factor IX ~1% (twice the per-kg dose of factor VIII for the same rise).

4 · Reversible Treatments

Defect | First-line treatment

Vitamin K deficiency / warfarin | Vitamin K ± 4F-PCC if bleeding

Heparin | Protamine sulfate

Dabigatran | Idarucizumab

Apixaban / rivaroxaban / edoxaban | Andexanet alfa or 4F-PCC

Fondaparinux / argatroban / bivalirudin | Supportive — no specific reversal; consider 4F-PCC if life-threatening

Low fibrinogen + bleeding | Cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate

Thrombocytopenia + bleeding | Platelet transfusion

Factor VIII deficiency (hem A) | Recombinant FVIII or DDAVP (mild)

Factor IX deficiency (hem B) | Recombinant FIX or 4F-PCC

vWD | DDAVP (type 1) or vWF/FVIII concentrate

Acquired hemophilia A | Bypassing agents (FEIBA, rFVIIa)

Hyperfibrinolysis | Tranexamic acid

Uremic platelet dysfunction | DDAVP, dialysis, conjugated estrogens, cryo

Argatroban and fondaparinux are named above as reversal-context anticoagulants but have no matching myMedKitt drug-store dosing entry for this indication — dosed per hematology/pharmacy guidance, not linked here.

Vitamin K-dependent factors: II, VII, IX, X (and protein C, S). Half-lives longest to shortest: factor II (60 h), factor X (40 h), factor IX (24 h), factor VII (6 h — falls first in vitamin K deficiency). Causes: warfarin, malabsorption, prolonged antibiotics, malnutrition, newborn deficiency (give vitamin K 1 mg IM at birth).[4]

Vitamin K — active bleeding (any INR >1.5): 10 mg IV slow push IV — Always co-administer with 4F-PCC for immediate reversal — vitamin K alone is too slow. No-bleeding INR bands: <4.5 hold only; 4.5-10 hold + 1-2.5 mg PO if high risk; >10 hold + 2.5-5 mg PO.

4F-PCC (Kcentra) — warfarin reversal, INR 2 to <4: 25 units/kg IV (max 2,500 units) IV — = computed dose at current weight, capped at 2,500 units. Always with vitamin K 10 mg IV.

4F-PCC (Kcentra) — warfarin reversal, INR 4-6: 35 units/kg IV (max 3,500 units) IV — = computed dose at current weight, capped at 3,500 units.

4F-PCC (Kcentra) — warfarin reversal, INR >6: 50 units/kg IV (max 5,000 units) IV — = computed dose at current weight, capped at 5,000 units.

Feature | 4F-PCC | FFP

Onset | 10-30 min | Hours

Volume | 100-200 mL | 800-1200 mL (15 mL/kg × 70 kg)

INR correction | Reliable | Often partial

TRALI / TACO risk | Low | Higher

FFP is banded by INR: 10 mL/kg (INR 2-3.9), 15 mL/kg (INR 4-6), 20 mL/kg (INR >6) — each unit ~250 mL; 4 units raise clotting factors only ~10%. Cannot achieve INR below ~1.7 regardless of volume. Always co-administer vitamin K 10 mg IV.

Fibrinogen replacement essential when fibrinogen <1.5-2 g/L with bleeding. Each cryoprecipitate unit contains ~150-250 mg fibrinogen plus FVIII, vWF, FXIII, fibronectin. Fibrinogen concentrate (RiaSTAP/Fibryga) 25-100 mg/kg IV (typical 4 g for 70 kg) is a pathogen-reduced, volume-sparing alternative with no thawing/ABO matching needed. Targets: trauma/cardiac surgery/liver transplant ≥1.5-2 g/L, postpartum hemorrhage ≥2 g/L, APL DIC ≥1.5-2 g/L. Fibrinogen normalizes last in DIC — do not stop monitoring on platelet recovery alone.[10][12]

Cryoprecipitate — standard dose: 10 units (raises fibrinogen 0.5-1 g/L) IV — Recheck fibrinogen 30-60 min post-infusion. Alternative weight-based dosing: 0.06 units/kg = computed dose at current weight.

Idarucizumab (Praxbind) — dabigatran reversal: 5 g IV (two 2.5 g bolus doses) IV — Full reversal within minutes (RE-VERSE AD). Specific to dabigatran only. Hemodialysis also removes dabigatran (~60% protein-bound).

Andexanet alfa — recombinant decoy FXa for apixaban/rivaroxaban/edoxaban. High dose (apixaban >5 mg or rivaroxaban >10 mg within 8 h, or edoxaban >30 mg, or unknown timing): Andexanet 800 mg bolus + 8 mg/min × 2 h. Low dose otherwise: 400 mg bolus + 4 mg/min × 2 h. ANNEXA-I (2024) confirmed effective hemostasis in major FXa-DOAC bleeding. 4F-PCC 50 units/kg (off-label) is the alternative when andexanet is unavailable.[14]

4F-PCC (Kcentra) — off-label FXa-DOAC reversal (andexanet unavailable): 50 units/kg IV (max 5,000 units) IV — = computed dose at current weight, capped at 5,000 units. Less effective than andexanet for Xa inhibitors but a reasonable alternative.

LMWH (enoxaparin): Protamine partially reverses (~60%) — 1 mg per 1 mg enoxaparin if dose <8 h ago, half dose if 8-12 h. Fondaparinux: no specific reversal — consider 4F-PCC 50 units/kg or rFVIIa for life-threatening bleeding. UFH: protamine 1 mg per 100 units of heparin given in the last 2-3 hours, max 50 mg per dose.[12]

Setting | Platelet target

Active bleeding | ≥50 × 10⁹/L

ICH / TBI / neuraxial procedure / neurosurgery | ≥100 × 10⁹/L

Pre-procedure (LP, central line, surgery) | ≥50 × 10⁹/L

Stable non-bleeding ICU | ≥20 × 10⁹/L (prophylaxis)

Stable outpatient hem-onc | ≥10 × 10⁹/L

Avoid prophylactic platelet transfusion in ITP, HIT (do NOT transfuse unless life-threatening — may worsen thrombosis), and TTP (may worsen thrombotic microangiopathy). ICH on antiplatelet agents: the PATCH trial showed platelet transfusion caused harm, not benefit. "Treat the patient, not the number."[2][12]

5 · Thrombotic & Hypercoagulable Workup

Provoked (surgery/trauma/immobilization within 3 months, pregnancy/OCP/HRT, active malignancy, hospitalization, long-haul travel): treat 3 months anticoagulation, do NOT routinely test for thrombophilia. Unprovoked/high-risk (age <50 at first VTE, recurrent VTE, unusual sites, strong family history, recurrent pregnancy loss, VTE despite therapeutic anticoagulation, skin necrosis on warfarin, arterial thrombosis at young age): warrants thrombophilia evaluation, timed 4-6 weeks after stopping anticoagulation.[3][13]

Most clinically important acquired thrombophilia. Sapporo criteria (revised 2006): clinical (vascular thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity) + lab (lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, or anti-β2-glycoprotein-I IgG/IgM ≥1 positive on 2 occasions ≥12 weeks apart). Triple positivity = highest thrombosis risk. Management: lifelong warfarin (target INR 2-3, or 3-4 in arterial/recurrent disease) — avoid DOACs in triple-positive APS (TRAPS/RAPS trials showed inferior outcomes). Catastrophic APS: mortality 50%, needs anticoagulation + steroids + plasmapheresis ± rituximab.[13]

Other acquired thrombophilias: cancer (Trousseau syndrome) — LMWH first-line; HIT — see below; PNH — Coombs-negative hemolysis + thrombosis (splanchnic); myeloproliferative neoplasms (JAK2, paradoxical bleeding + thrombosis); nephrotic syndrome — loss of antithrombin in urine.[13]

In the ED, almost never order thrombophilia panels acutely — counsel the patient and arrange outpatient hematology follow-up. Testing rarely changes duration of anticoagulation (except homozygous/compound-heterozygous defects, APS, antithrombin deficiency).[13]

Paradoxical prothrombotic disorder from anti-PF4/heparin antibodies. Score interpretation: 0-3 low probability, do not test; 4-5 intermediate — send anti-PF4 ELISA, stop heparin while waiting; 6-8 high — empirically stop heparin, start non-heparin anticoagulant, send anti-PF4 + serotonin release assay (SRA, gold standard). Management: stop ALL heparin (including line flushes, LMWH, coated catheters); do NOT transfuse platelets unless life-threatening bleeding; start argatroban, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux; bridge to warfarin only after platelets >150 × 10⁹/L (warfarin alone in HIT can cause venous limb gangrene).[7]

6 · Special Populations

Decreased pro-coagulant factors (bleeding tendency) are offset by decreased anti-coagulant factors — protein C/S, antithrombin (thrombotic tendency). PT/INR does NOT correlate with bleeding risk in cirrhosis — do not transfuse FFP just to "correct" labs in a non-bleeding patient. Patients with cirrhosis still develop VTE — prophylaxis is appropriate. Pre-procedure thresholds: INR <2.5 adequate for low-risk procedures, platelets >50 × 10⁹/L, fibrinogen >1.5 g/L. Viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) better predicts bleeding than PT/INR in cirrhosis. When bleeding: vitamin K 10 mg IV if cholestatic, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen <1.5 g/L, platelets if <50 × 10⁹/L, 4F-PCC instead of high-volume FFP if volume-restricted, TXA for mucosal/variceal bleeding.[8]

Fibrinogen rises to 4-6 g/L (vs 2-4 g/L non-pregnant); factors VII, VIII, X, XII, vWF rise; protein S decreases; net 4-5× increased VTE risk. A "normal" fibrinogen of 2.5 g/L in pregnancy is actually a major drop — treat fibrinogen <2 g/L (vs <1.5 g/L non-pregnant) in obstetric DIC/PPH. D-dimer rises with gestation, limiting its VTE rule-out value. LMWH is first-line anticoagulation — does not cross placenta; warfarin contraindicated in T1 and near term; DOACs contraindicated. APS in pregnancy: LMWH + low-dose aspirin from positive pregnancy test through 6 weeks postpartum. Hold LMWH 24 h before neuraxial anesthesia (12 h for prophylactic dose).[3][12]

Newborns: vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X) start at ~50% adult level — give vitamin K 1 mg IM at birth; PT/aPTT normally prolonged vs adult ranges. Factor levels reach adult range by 6 months. Pediatric-specific disorders: HSP/IgA vasculitis, ITP (usually post-viral, self-limited), NAIT (maternal anti-platelet antibodies, HPA-1a most common). Pediatric thrombosis is usually associated with central lines, malignancy, congenital heart disease, or severe infection — LMWH first-line; warfarin and DOACs are now FDA-approved for children per hematology guidance.[4]

7 · One-Glance Algorithm

5 labs answer 90% of coag consults — PT/INR, aPTT, platelets, fibrinogen, D-dimer. Cell-based model: initiation → amplification → propagation.

Bleeding vs thrombotic vs mixed? — The only true branch point in this consult — determines the entire downstream workup.

Pattern matrix: PT/PTT/plt/fibrinogen/D-dimer — Lab pattern → likely bucket table; cell-based cascade mechanism map.

Five-questions framework — Bleed/clot? Which lab? Mucosal/deep? Anticoagulant on board? Acquired/congenital?

Baseline panel + mixing study — CBC/plt, PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, smear. Mixing study: corrects = factor deficiency, doesn't correct = inhibitor.

Factor assays, DOAC detection & viscoelastic testing — Targeted factor levels, DOAC-specific tests (TT for dabigatran, anti-Xa for FXa-DOACs), TEG/ROTEM, platelet function testing.

PT/PTT prolongation differential — Isolated PT, isolated PTT, and both-prolonged differentials, split by mixing-study correction.

Thrombocytopenia, mucosal & deep-tissue bleeding — vWD types, hemophilia A/B, acquired hemophilia A (bleeding emergency).

Reversal overview: vitamin K & PCC — Defect-to-treatment matrix; vitamin K half-lives; 4F-PCC INR-banded dosing vs FFP.

Cryo/fibrinogen, DOAC reversal & platelet transfusion — Idarucizumab, andexanet alfa, protamine; platelet transfusion thresholds.

Thrombotic pattern recognition — Reached directly if thrombotic-dominant, or after the full lab workup if bleeding-dominant/mixed. Provoked vs unprovoked triage.

Acquired & inherited thrombophilias — APS (Sapporo criteria), Trousseau, HIT, PNH, MPN; Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, protein C/S, antithrombin deficiency.

When (and when not) to test — Test 4-6 weeks after stopping anticoagulation, only if it changes management.

HIT — 4Ts score — Score ≥4 → stop ALL heparin, no platelet transfusion, start non-heparin anticoagulant.

Special populations: liver, pregnancy, pediatric — Cirrhosis rebalanced hemostasis; pregnancy fibrinogen threshold <2 g/L; neonatal vitamin K.

Pitfalls & pearls — disposition — Cross-link to DIC, TTP, Anticoagulation Reversal, DVT, PE trees for definitive management.

8 · Pitfalls & Pearls

10 common pitfalls in coag interpretation

"Treating the labs" — don't transfuse FFP for asymptomatic INR elevation in cirrhosis or non-bleeding patients; don't transfuse platelets prophylactically in HIT, ITP, or TTP unless life-threatening bleeding.

Forgetting drug effect — most "abnormal coags" are drug effect, check the med list first; heparin contamination is the most common cause of a non-correcting PTT mixing study.

Missing acquired hemophilia — older adult or postpartum woman with new spontaneous deep-tissue bleeding + isolated PTT that does NOT correct → think acquired hemophilia A; stat hematology consult, bypassing agents (FEIBA, rFVIIa).

Misinterpreting fibrinogen in pregnancy — pregnant baseline is 4-6 g/L; "normal" 2.5 g/L is actually consumed. Threshold for cryo in OB is <2 g/L, not <1 g/L.

Missing HIT — any heparin exposure 5-14 days ago + falling platelets + thrombosis = HIT until proven otherwise. 4Ts score; stop ALL heparin; do NOT transfuse platelets; start non-heparin anticoagulant.

Ordering thrombophilia panels acutely — most acute results are uninterpretable; test 4-6 weeks after stopping anticoagulation, only if it would change management.

Lupus anticoagulant misnamed — causes paradoxical thrombosis (NOT bleeding) despite a prolonged PTT; most patients with lupus AC do not have lupus.

Cirrhosis "rebalanced" hemostasis — PT/INR does NOT predict bleeding risk in cirrhosis; patients with cirrhosis still develop VTE; use viscoelastic testing when available.

Forgetting factor XII deficiency does not bleed — markedly prolonged PTT but no bleeding history → think factor XII deficiency.

Bleeding time is obsolete — operator-dependent, poor reproducibility; use PFA-100 or specific platelet function tests instead.

Pattern over memorization. The five labs (PT/PTT/platelets/fibrinogen/D-dimer) plus a smear and a med list answer 90% of bleeding/clotting consults. Cross-link to dedicated trees — DIC, TTP, Anticoagulation Reversal, DVT, PE — for definitive management.[2][4][7]
