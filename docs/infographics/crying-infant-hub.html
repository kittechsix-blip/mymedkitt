Crying Infant Hub: Sick Check First, Then Pick the Dangerous Lane

Infants presenting to the ED with excessive/inconsolable crying. A triage router into age-based fever, respiratory/cardiac, GI, GU, trauma/NAT, occult-pain, neurologic, feeding, and colic pathways — not a standalone treatment protocol. Colic is a diagnosis of exclusion reached only after the other lanes are screened.

⚠ Sick until proven otherwise. Fever <60 days, hypothermia, ill appearance, lethargy, poor feeding, or respiratory distress must be ruled out before any benign label is used. Completely undress every crying infant — scalp, eyes, ears, mouth, neck, chest, abdomen, groin, diaper area, extremities, digits, and genitalia.
⚠ Pick the first dangerous lane before labeling it colic. Fever/toxic, respiratory/cardiac, GI obstruction, GU emergency, trauma/NAT, neurologic, and occult-pain-source lanes must each be screened; colic is the last lane, never the default.
⚠ Fever <60 days, hypothermia, ill appearance, lethargy, poor feeding, abnormal perfusion, respiratory distress, petechiae/purpura, bulging fontanelle, or seizure in a young infant is an infectious-workup lane — do not let a transiently consolable exam override abnormal vitals or a high-risk age.
⚠ Tachypnea, retractions, grunting, hypoxia, cyanosis, apnea, feeding fatigue/sweating with feeds, poor pulses, murmur, or shock can present as crying — treat as a respiratory or cardiac cause until excluded.
⚠ Bilious emesis, episodic severe crying with leg-drawing, bloody stool, or lethargy between episodes is an obstruction/intussusception pathway — NPO if concerning, IV access if ill/dehydrated, glucose if lethargic, and urgent imaging/surgical consultation.
⚠ Scrotal/labial swelling, testicular tenderness or high-riding testis, urinary retention, or painful urination needs immediate GU/hernia evaluation — a diaper-area exam is mandatory in the inconsolable infant.
⚠ Unexplained bruising in a non-mobile infant, burns, scalp swelling, rib/limb tenderness, or an inconsistent/changing story requires a safety workup, not reassurance — follow the local child-protection pathway and mandatory reporting when the suspicion threshold is met.
⚠ Bulging fontanelle, seizure, abnormal tone, persistent lethargy, altered responsiveness, or rapidly increasing head circumference should never be called colic — treat as CNS infection, hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, or trauma until excluded.
⚠ Persistent high-risk crying is not a discharge diagnosis. Admit, transfer, or consult for a fever/high-risk young-infant pathway, ill/toxic appearance, abnormal vitals, poor feeding/dehydration, suspected obstruction/torsion, neurologic signs, trauma/NAT concern, unsafe home, or persistent unexplained inconsolability.

1 · Sick check first — undress, vitals, 5 do-not-miss

Crying is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Screen the 5 DO NOT MISS categories below before calling it colic. First 60 seconds: age in days/weeks, rectal temperature, full vitals, appearance, perfusion, work of breathing, oxygen saturation, feeding/urine output/vomiting/stool/weight gain, birth history, prematurity, maternal GBS/HSV, immunizations. Completely undress the infant and check scalp, mouth, eyes, ears, neck, chest, abdomen, groin, diaper area, extremities, digits, and genitalia. Observe caregiver interaction and ability to soothe/feed. The ED job is to find the painful or dangerous cause before calling it colic.[1][2][3]

Intussusception / volvulusIntermittent severe crying, bilious emesis, bloody stool
Incarcerated hernia / testicular torsionExamine groin and genitalia every time
Occult causes: hair tourniquet, corneal abrasion, SVTCheck digits, eyes, and ECG

Fever / toxic / young infant
Open Fever < 6 Months. High-risk signs: age <60 days with fever, hypothermia, ill appearance, lethargy, poor feeding, abnormal perfusion, respiratory distress, petechiae/purpura, bulging fontanelle, seizure, or a caregiver report that the infant is not acting right. Follow the age-based febrile infant pathway and local protocols. Do not let a transiently consolable exam override abnormal vitals or a high-risk age group.[3]
Respiratory / cardiac cause
Open Bronchiolitis, BRUE, or Neonatal Resuscitation when the phenotype fits. Look for tachypnea, retractions, grunting, hypoxia, cyanosis, apnea, sweating or fatigue with feeds, hepatomegaly, poor pulses, murmur, shock, or differential saturation/pulses. Crying can be the presenting symptom of hypoxia, heart failure, congenital heart disease, respiratory infection, or metabolic stress.[1][2]
GI emergency / obstruction
Open Intussusception when episodic severe crying, leg drawing, vomiting, bloody stool, or lethargy between episodes fits. Do not miss bilious emesis, volvulus/malrotation, incarcerated hernia, intussusception, constipation with fissure, anal fissure, or feeding intolerance. Actions: NPO for concerning obstruction, IV access if ill/dehydrated, glucose if lethargic, abdominal exam including groin, ultrasound for intussusception, abdominal imaging/surgical consultation for bilious emesis or obstruction signs.[1][2][6]
GU emergency
Open Testicular Torsion when a male infant has scrotal swelling, testicular tenderness, high-riding/abnormal lie, discoloration, or unexplained severe distress. Also screen for incarcerated inguinal hernia, UTI/pyelonephritis, urinary retention, labial adhesions with retention, diaper dermatitis, balanitis/vulvovaginitis, and genital hair tourniquet. A diaper-area exam is mandatory in the inconsolable infant.[1][2]
Trauma / non-accidental trauma
Look for bruising in non-mobile infants, oral frenulum injury, burns, scalp swelling, tenderness with limb movement, rib tenderness, abdominal tenderness, retinal/neuro symptoms, an inconsistent or changing story, delay in care, or caregiver impairment. Actions: pain control, protect the child, follow the local child-protection pathway, skeletal survey/head imaging/labs as indicated by age and findings, social work/child abuse pediatrics when available, mandatory reporting when the suspicion threshold is met.[4]
Occult pain source on full exam
Completely undress and inspect: hair tourniquet (toes, fingers, penis, clitoris/labia); corneal abrasion/foreign body — fluorescein if eye redness, tearing, rubbing, photophobia, or unexplained crying; mouth ulcers/thrush/trauma (teething only after dangerous causes excluded); ears (otitis media/externa when fever/URI/ear findings fit); skin (diaper dermatitis, cellulitis, burns, bites, pressure injury); extremities (fracture, nursemaid elbow in older infant/toddler, swelling, focal tenderness). Many fixable causes are only found when the diaper and socks come off.[1][2]
Neurologic / CNS concern
Bulging fontanelle, seizure, abnormal tone, persistent lethargy, altered responsiveness, vomiting with neurologic signs, rapidly increasing head circumference, or inconsolability with an abnormal exam should trigger CNS infection, intracranial hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, metabolic disease, or trauma thinking. Open VP Shunt Complications if a shunt is present. Use fever, trauma, seizure, and age-based pathways as applicable.[1][2][3][4]
Feeding / dehydration / reflux mimics
Assess feeding volume/frequency, latch/formula preparation, vomiting, choking/coughing feeds, wet diapers, stooling, weight trend, jaundice, and caregiver technique. Escalate for dehydration, weight loss/failure to gain, bilious emesis, forceful/projectile vomiting, blood in stool, fever, lethargy, hypoglycemia, or an unsafe feeding environment. Reflux and formula intolerance are diagnoses only after dangerous feeding, infection, GI obstruction, and cardiopulmonary causes are screened.[1][2]
Colic / benign crying pattern
Use this lane only when the infant is well appearing, afebrile, normally perfused, feeding and voiding adequately, gaining weight, has a normal full undressed exam, no trauma/safety concern, and caregivers can return if worse. Typical colic is recurrent crying in an otherwise healthy infant, often evening-predominant — it is a diagnosis of pattern plus normal exam, not a shortcut diagnosis.[1][2]

3 · ED bundle, imaging & disposition (reference)

  • Rectal temperature and full vitals
  • Glucose if ill appearing, poor feeding, lethargic, young infant, or abnormal vitals
  • Completely undress; inspect hair tourniquets, eyes, mouth, ears, abdomen/groin, diaper area, extremities, bruising/burns
  • Feed observation when stable; assess hydration and caregiver technique
  • Analgesia when a painful cause is suspected — do not withhold pain control while diagnosing
  • Targeted labs/imaging only after age, vitals, and exam define the lane

In practice, this default ED bundle runs alongside picking a danger lane above — vitals, glucose if indicated, and analgesia should not wait on a final diagnosis. This section is reference material for the next stage of care; the source tree does not formally chain a lane terminal into it, so it is not shown as a connected flow step. Reassess after feeding, analgesia, fever treatment, diaper change, swaddling, or treatment of a found source.[1][2][3]

Can the infant feed, console, maintain vitals, and remain well appearing after initial care? Still inconsolable, abnormal vitals, poor feeding, lethargy, or a concerning exam returns to the time-critical exclusions above. Improving but workup/imaging still needed moves to the imaging strategy. Well appearing with a source found or a benign pattern and a safe caregiver moves to disposition.[1][2]

5 · Disposition

Disposition depends on age, vitals, feeding, exam, diagnosis, caregiver reliability, and safety.[1][2][3]

Disposition tierCriteria
Admit / consult / transferToxic/fever high-risk age, abnormal vitals, poor feeding/dehydration, GI/GU/resp/neuro/trauma concern, unsafe caregiver
ObservationImproving but diagnostic uncertainty remains or caregiver needs observation/support
Discharge checklistWell appearing, normal vitals/exam, feeding, consolable, safe caregiver
Admit / consult / transfer
Admit, transfer, or consult for the fever/high-risk young-infant pathway, ill/toxic appearance, abnormal vitals, poor feeding/dehydration, hypoglycemia, respiratory distress, suspected intussusception/obstruction, incarcerated hernia, torsion, neurologic signs, trauma/NAT concern, unsafe home, or persistent unexplained inconsolability. Persistent high-risk crying is not a discharge diagnosis.[1][2][3][4]
Observation / serial exam
Observe when symptoms are improving but the infant is not clearly baseline, feeding/hydration needs demonstration, caregiver confidence is low, fever workup is pending, pain-source treatment response is uncertain, or serial abdominal/neuro exams are needed. A period of observation can convert vague crying into a diagnosis or a safe discharge.[1][2]
Discharge checklist
Discharge only when the infant is well appearing, afebrile or the fever pathway is completed, vitals are normal, the full undressed exam is normal or the benign source is treated, feeding is observed or reliable, urine output is adequate, the caregiver can soothe and return, and safety concerns are absent. Return now for: fever, poor feeding, fewer wet diapers, lethargy, breathing trouble, blue color, bilious/green vomiting, blood in stool, swollen groin/scrotum, persistent inconsolability, seizure, new rash/bruising, or caregiver concern that something is wrong.[1][2]

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT diagnose colic until time-sensitive infection, GI, GU, trauma, respiratory, and neurologic causes are screened.

Do NOT let a transiently consolable exam override abnormal vitals or a high-risk age group in a febrile or ill-appearing young infant.

Do NOT withhold pain control while diagnosing — analgesia is part of the default ED bundle, not something to wait on.

Do NOT under-test abnormal vitals or a high-risk age, but also do not shotgun-test a well infant with a normal exam.

Do NOT skip the diaper-area exam in an inconsolable infant — scrotal, labial, and groin findings are only found undressed.

Do NOT reassure a crying infant with unexplained injury or an unsafe story — that pattern needs a safety workup, not reassurance.

Do NOT discharge persistent high-risk crying — it is not a discharge diagnosis.
