Pediatric Intussusception: Recognize, Confirm, Reduce, Dispo

Infants and children with suspected ileocolic intussusception in the ED, typically 6-36 months (peak 4-9 months). Excludes adults and isolated transient small bowel-small bowel intussusception (self-resolving, not modeled as a reduction pathway).

⚠ Consider intussusception in ANY infant with episodic inconsolable crying, lethargy, or altered mental status — the classic triad (pain + currant jelly stool + mass) is present in <25% of cases; lethargy alone may be the ONLY symptom in young infants.
⚠ ABSOLUTE contraindications to enema reduction: peritonitis, pneumoperitoneum (free air), or hemodynamic instability/shock — these patients go straight to surgery, not the enema suite.
⚠ Age <3 months or >5 years carries a higher risk of a pathologic lead point — obtain surgical consultation early, even before an enema attempt.
⚠ Perforation during reduction is a surgical emergency: STOP the procedure, decompress tension pneumoperitoneum, resuscitate, give antibiotics, and go emergently to the OR.
⚠ Post-reduction: observe a minimum of 4 hours, trial PO at 2 hours; recurrence rate is 8-10%, most within 24-48 hours.

Recognition

Telescoping of proximal bowel (intussusceptum) into distal bowel (intussuscipiens) — most commonly ileocolic (ileum into cecum/colon). Most common cause of bowel obstruction in children 6-36 months. Peak incidence: 4-9 months. 80-90% occur before age 2 years. Male:Female ratio 3:2. If untreated: perforation and death within 2-5 days.

PresentationFindings
Classic Triad (present in <25%):Colicky pain + inconsolable crying, currant jelly stool, palpable sausage-shaped RUQ mass
More commonEpisodic crampy pain (draws legs up, screaming), pain-free intervals, vomiting (non-bilious → bilious), lethargy between episodes, decreased intake
Atypical (younger infants)Lethargy or altered mental status may be the ONLY symptom; pallor, hypotonia; painless intussusception
Late / concerningBloody stool or currant jelly, bilious vomiting, abdominal distension, peritoneal signs

In young infants, lethargy or altered mental status may be the ONLY presenting symptom — a diagnostic pitfall. A well-appearing infant between episodes does NOT rule out intussusception; the episodic nature is characteristic. Maintain high suspicion and proceed to ultrasound.

Concerning / late findings
Bloody stool / currant jelly (mix of blood, mucus, sloughed mucosa) indicates mucosal ischemia — present in <50% and is a LATE finding; absence does not rule out. Bilious vomiting indicates complete small bowel obstruction. Abdominal distension may indicate perforation if sudden. Peritoneal signs suggest possible necrosis/perforation and are a contraindication to enema reduction. Shock suggests bowel compromise or sepsis. Action: immediate surgical consult, IV access, fluid resuscitation — do NOT delay for imaging if clinically unstable.

Sausage-shaped mass: RUQ, palpable in ~60%. Dance's sign: empty RLQ (cecum displaced). May be soft and non-tender between episodes. Rectal exam may show gross blood, currant jelly, or guaiac positive stool. A normal abdominal exam between episodes does NOT exclude intussusception.

Ultrasound diagnosis

Ultrasound is the gold standard. Sensitivity: >97%. Specificity: >97%. Negative predictive value: 99.7%. Focus on the RUQ (most common location).

ViewFinding
TransverseTarget/donut sign — concentric rings of alternating echogenicity. Usually ~3 cm diameter
LongitudinalPseudokidney sign — elongated, kidney-shaped structure ("sandwich"/"hayfork" sign)
AdditionalTrapped lymph nodes, free fluid (concerning), absent blood flow on Doppler (very concerning)
Concerning ultrasound features
Free peritoneal fluid, absent blood flow on Doppler, large trapped fluid, left-sided location, or a visible lead point are associated with higher failure/surgical need — NOT an absolute contraindication to enema. Obtain surgical consultation early and have surgical backup ready before attempting reduction.

Small bowel-small bowel intussusception: usually <2.5 cm diameter, no lead point visible, often incidental (common with gastroenteritis). Usually self-resolves within minutes to hours — does NOT require enema reduction. If symptomatic: repeat US in 1-2 hours; if persistent (>1 hour) and symptomatic, or >3 cm, or visible lead point: surgical consultation.

Negative ultrasound (experienced sonographer, adequate views) has a 99.7% NPV and effectively rules out ileocolic intussusception. Consider alternative diagnoses: gastroenteritis, constipation, colic, incarcerated hernia, testicular torsion, ovarian pathology, or malrotation with volvulus (more urgent). Repeat imaging only if symptoms persist/worsen or clinical suspicion remains strong.

Recent evidence (APSA 2021): Hemodynamically stable patients without critical illness can be safely observed in ED after enema reduction without requiring hospital admission.

  • Return IMMEDIATELY for: severe crying/drawing up legs, vomiting (especially bilious), bloody or currant jelly stool, abdominal distension, lethargy, fever, or refusal to eat/drink.
  • Recurrence happens in ~10% of cases, most within 24-48 hours; recurrences can usually be treated with repeat enema.
  • PCP follow-up within 24-48 hours; pediatric surgery if lead point suspected.
  • Inpatient monitoring: serial abdominal exams, advance diet as tolerated, IV fluids until tolerating PO.
  • If symptoms recur: repeat ultrasound; repeat enema reduction can be attempted; after 3 recurrences, surgical consultation.
  • Discharge criteria: tolerating regular diet, no abdominal symptoms, 12-24 hours without recurrence.

Surgical intervention detailindications and pre-op management

Indications: peritonitis, pneumoperitoneum, hemodynamic instability despite resuscitation, failed enema reduction (2-3 attempts), confirmed pathologic lead point, recurrent intussusception (3+ episodes). Pre-op: NPO, IV access x2, fluid resuscitation, type and screen, antibiotics if perforation suspected, NG tube for decompression if obstructed. Surgical approach: laparoscopic reduction preferred when possible; open laparotomy if unstable/complex; resection if necrotic bowel or lead point.
