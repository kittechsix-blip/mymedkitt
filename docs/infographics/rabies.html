Rabies: Clinical Illness or Exposure — Diagnose, Wound Care, and PEP

Any patient with a suspected animal exposure or an acute unexplained neuropsychiatric illness with possible exposure history. Excludes pediatric weight-based sedation dosing for ICU rabies management (adult ICU protocol only) and non-mammalian exposures.

⚠ Once symptomatic, no effective therapy exists — rabies is nearly uniformly fatal. Palliative care is appropriate for most; a modified Milwaukee Protocol (ketamine + midazolam + nimodipine, ICU) is an option in select young/immunocompetent/early-detection cases but outcomes remain extremely poor — the original therapeutic-coma protocol has failed in ≥26 cases and is no longer recommended.
⚠ Category III exposures (transdermal bite/scratch, mucous membrane contamination, or any bat contact) require RIG + vaccine regardless of species — dogs/cats/ferrets can still be observed 10 days, but START PEP immediately; do not wait for observation to begin treatment.
⚠ Do NOT delay RIG for Category III exposures, and do NOT give RIG after day 7 of the vaccine series — RIG must be infiltrated into the wound and given IM (distant site) with the first vaccine dose only; late RIG interferes with the patient's own developing active immunity.
⚠ Bat bites can be virtually invisible. Treat ALL bat exposures as Category III (RIG + vaccine) unless the patient can definitively confirm no bite, scratch, or mucous-membrane contact occurred — PEP is indicated for a bat found with a sleeping person, unattended child, or intoxicated/cognitively impaired person even without a confirmed bite.
⚠ RIG 20 IU/kg — do not exceed this dose. Exceeding 20 IU/kg may suppress the patient's own active antibody production from the vaccine.

1 · Rabies concern — clinical illness or animal exposure?

Two distinct scenarios, one tree
Rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic — but post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is highly effective if given promptly, with no confirmed PEP failures when administered correctly. This tool covers both: (1) suspected active clinical rabies (established infection) and (2) a recent animal exposure requiring a PEP decision.[1][2]

Suspect clinical rabies when: acute neuropsychiatric illness + travel to a rabies-endemic area, pain/paresthesia/pruritus at a prior bite site (relatively specific), or an animal exposure weeks to months prior — patients may not recall or report it. Rabies is frequently missed at initial presentation and is nationally notifiable. Use standard + contact precautions.[3][4]

2 · Suspected clinical rabies (established infection)

FormKey featuresAverage survival
Furious (encephalitic) — 80% develop furiousHydrophobia, aerophobia, fluctuating consciousness, inspiratory spasms, hypersalivation, autonomic dysfunction (tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia)5-6 days
Paralytic (dumb) — 20%Ascending flaccid paralysis mimicking GBS; preserved consciousness until preterminal phase; pain/paresthesia at bite site; lower diagnostic sensitivity across specimen types~11 days
Atypical (bat variants)Focal brainstem signs, myoclonus, hemichorea, Horner's syndrome — may mimic transverse myelitis, NMO, or tetanus with locked jawVariable

Key mimics: HSV encephalitis lacks hydrophobia/aerophobia and shows temporal-lobe MRI findings; tetanus has continuous rigidity between spasms (absent in rabies) with normal consciousness; anti-NMDAR autoimmune encephalitis has prominent seizures (85%) and responds to immunotherapy.[3][4]

No single test is sufficient — collect all 4 specimens
Immediately consult local public health laboratory or CDC — testing is not available at most hospital labs. Saliva RT-PCR (≥3 serial samples, >98% sensitivity), nuchal skin biopsy DFA (~100% sensitivity with adequate tissue), CSF antibody (~100% sensitivity after day 12, diagnostic in unvaccinated patients), and serum antibody. Combined sensitivity is 100% when all 4 are tested. Early illness (week 1) → prioritize saliva + nuchal biopsy; later illness (>7-12 days) → antibody testing has higher yield. Prior IVIG can cause false-positive serology.[8][9][10][11]
Once symptomatic: no effective therapy exists
Rabies is nearly uniformly fatal once symptomatic. Of ~10 documented survivors, most had received partial PEP before symptom onset, and nearly all had permanent neurological deficits.[2][3][12]
Palliative care — appropriate for most
Liberal sedatives/analgesics, antispasmodics for pharyngeal spasms, anxiolytics for agitation, family counseling on prognosis, avoid unnecessary invasive procedures. Standard + contact precautions throughout; careful handling of all secretions; report to public health — rabies is nationally notifiable. A 2024 Kentucky/Ohio case had 52 of 60 healthcare workers requiring PEP after inconsistent adherence to standard precautions around saliva exposure.[2][13]
Aggressive / ICU approach — consider in young, immunocompetent, early-detection cases
The original Milwaukee Protocol (therapeutic coma with burst suppression) has failed in ≥26 subsequent cases and is no longer recommended. Modified current approach: ketamine + midazolam titrated for sedation/dysautonomia, nimodipine 60 mg PO/NG q4h (use with caution — hypotension risk), ICU supportive care, ID + neurology consultation. Outcomes remain extremely poor even with factors favoring therapy (prior partial PEP, young/healthy/immunocompetent, mild disease at initiation, New World bat variant, early neutralizing antibodies).[2][3][12][14]

3 · Animal exposure — WHO category, species, and wound care

WHO categoryDefinitionPEP
I — No exposureTouching, feeding, licks on intact skinNone indicated
II — MinorNibbling uncovered skin, minor scratches/abrasions without bleedingVaccine only (species-dependent)
III — SevereTransdermal bites/scratches, mucous-membrane contamination, broken skin + saliva, ANY direct bat contactRIG + vaccine (species-dependent)

Wound care is the single most effective rabies prevention measure — reduces risk by up to 90% in animal studies. Wash wound with soap and water for at least 15 minutes, then irrigate with povidone-iodine; do NOT apply caustic agents (bleach, acid). Delay primary closure if possible; if suturing is required, infiltrate RIG into the wound bed first.[1][15][18]

Healthy dogs, cats, and ferrets can be observed for 10 days. No reliable observation period exists for wildlife — observation applies ONLY to dogs, cats, and ferrets.[1][16][17]

4 · PEP regimen — what to give right now

Applies once Category II or III PEP is indicated (per Section 3) and wound care is done. Set the toggles above — exactly one regimen below applies.

Unvaccinated, immunocompetent → RIG + 4-dose vaccine
Rabies Immune Globulin (HRIG) 20 IU/kg — infiltrate as much as anatomically feasible into and around the wound(s), inject the remainder IM at a site distant from the vaccine injection. Give with the first vaccine dose only — do NOT give RIG after day 7 of the vaccine series; do not exceed 20 IU/kg. Rabies Vaccine (HDCV or PCECV) 1.0 mL IM deltoid (adults) or anterolateral thigh (children) on Days 0, 3, 7, and 14 (4-dose series). Pregnancy is NOT a contraindication.[1][18][19][20]
Immunocompromised → RIG + 5-dose vaccine + serology
Give RIG even if previously vaccinated — Rabies Immune Globulin (HRIG) 20 IU/kg, same infiltration protocol. Rabies Vaccine (HDCV or PCECV) 1.0 mL IM on Days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28 (5-dose series, additional day-28 dose). Check RVNA titer 7-14 days after the last dose — adequate response ≥0.5 IU/mL; if inadequate, give an additional dose and recheck. ID consultation recommended.[1][19]
Previously vaccinated, immunocompetent → 2-dose booster, no RIG
No RIG needed — an anamnestic (memory B-cell) response is expected. Rabies Vaccine (HDCV or PCECV) 1.0 mL IM deltoid (adults) or anterolateral thigh (children) on Days 0 and 3 only (2-dose series).[1][18]

5 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT wait for the 10-day observation period before starting PEP on a Category III exposure** — start RIG + vaccine immediately even if the dog/cat/ferret is available and healthy; discontinue only if it remains healthy at day 10.

**Do NOT dismiss a bat exposure because no bite is visible** — bat fang marks can be invisible; treat as Category III unless the patient can definitively exclude any bite, scratch, or mucous-membrane contact.

**Do NOT give RIG after day 7 of the vaccine series** — it interferes with the patient's own developing active immunity. Give RIG with the first vaccine dose only.

**Do NOT exceed 20 IU/kg RIG** — excess may suppress active antibody production.

**Do NOT inject rabies vaccine in the gluteal area** — documented poor immunogenicity. Deltoid (adults) or anterolateral thigh (children) only, and always at a site distant from RIG.

**Do NOT withhold PEP for pregnancy** — pregnancy is NOT a contraindication to rabies PEP; rabies is fatal.
