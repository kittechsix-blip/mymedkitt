Shoulder Dystocia: Start the Clock, Work the Sequence

Any clinician managing a vaginal delivery complicated by shoulder dystocia (ED, L&D, or prehospital). A time-critical obstetric emergency that cannot be predicted or prevented. Assumes the fetal head has already delivered. Not a substitute for immediate obstetric, anesthesia, and NICU support.

1 · Recognize

What it is
Shoulder dystocia occurs when the baby's anterior shoulder impacts behind the maternal pubic symphysis after the head delivers. It complicates about 1.5% of vaginal deliveries and cannot be predicted or prevented.[1][9]
  1. Turtle sign — the head delivers then retracts tightly against the perineum.
  2. Failure of restitution — the head does not spontaneously rotate, and gentle downward (axial) traction fails to deliver the anterior shoulder.
Traction
Apply downward axial traction (below the horizontal), never lateral bending. There is no validated threshold for "excessive" force — escalate to maneuvers rather than pulling harder.[1]

2 · Call for help & start the clock

  • Announce "shoulder dystocia" to the room.
  • Call a second obstetrician, pediatrics/NICU, and anesthesia.
  • Designate a timekeeper — call out elapsed time every 30 seconds.
  • Designate a note-taker — record every maneuver and its timing.
The clock is the emergency
You have roughly 4–5 minutes from head delivery before the risk of hypoxic brain injury climbs steeply. Work the sequence deliberately — about 30 seconds per maneuver.[2][3]

3 · What to do right now

0–1 min
McRoberts + suprapubic pressure, simultaneously. Hyperflex both hips onto the abdomen and apply steady suprapubic pressure toward the fetal chest.
1–2 min
First-line failed → rotational maneuvers (Wood's screw / Rubin) and move toward the posterior arm. Try rotation in both directions.
2–4 min
Deliver the posterior arm now — it is your highest-yield move (84.4% single-maneuver success). Do not keep re-trying lower-yield maneuvers.
≥4 min — last resort
Standard maneuvers have failed and time is critical: set up for emergency cesarean and prepare the Zavanelli maneuver (cephalic replacement + tocolysis). Get the whole team in the room.

4 · First-line: McRoberts + suprapubic

Do these together, first
McRoberts + suprapubic pressure resolve 50–60% of all shoulder dystocias and are the fastest, lowest-risk moves.[5][6]
  1. McRoberts: sharply flex the mother's legs onto her abdomen — this rotates the symphysis cephalad and straightens the sacrum (it changes the angle, not the pelvic dimensions).
  2. Suprapubic pressure: press just above the symphysis, directed toward the fetal chest, to push the anterior shoulder into the oblique diameter.
  3. Do NOT apply fundal pressure — it deepens the impaction.

5 · Rotational & posterior arm

Rotational maneuvers
Wood's screw: press the anterior surface of the posterior shoulder and rotate it 180° in a corkscrew. Rubin: press the posterior surface of the posterior shoulder to flex the shoulders across the chest and narrow the bisacromial diameter.
⭐ Delivery of the posterior arm — MOST RELIABLE
Highest single-maneuver success of any shoulder-dystocia maneuver — 84.4% (Hoffman 2011, NICHD). If first-line fails, prioritize this rather than repeating lower-yield maneuvers.[9]
  1. Reach into the posterior vagina and apply antecubital pressure to flex the fetal elbow.
  2. Grasp the forearm or wrist only (never pull on the upper arm — it fractures the humerus).
  3. Sweep the arm across the fetal chest and out, delivering the posterior arm and shoulder.
Arm not reachable → Menticoglou
If you cannot reach the forearm, use Menticoglou axillary traction: hook a finger (or a soft catheter sling) through the posterior axilla to deliver the posterior shoulder first.

6 · Last resort

All standard maneuvers failed
Each option below carries significant maternal and/or fetal risk. Choose based on the resources in the room and get the full team present.
  1. Zavanelli: set up for emergency cesarean first, rotate the head to occiput-anterior, then flex and push it back into the vagina with constant firm pressure.
  2. All-fours (Gaskin): move the mother onto hands and knees, which can free the posterior shoulder.
  3. Deliberate clavicle fracture or symphysiotomy where cesarean is unavailable — high complication rates.
