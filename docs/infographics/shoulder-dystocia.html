Shoulder Dystocia — Recognition & Stepwise Resolution

Any clinician managing a vaginal delivery complicated by shoulder dystocia (ED, L&D, or prehospital), after the fetal head has already delivered. A time-critical obstetric emergency that cannot be predicted or prevented by antenatal screening. Not a substitute for immediate obstetric, anesthesia, and NICU support, or for hands-on simulation training.

⚠ Cannot be predicted with any degree of accuracy and cannot be prevented by any specific strategy or maneuver — every vaginal delivery team must be prepared.
⚠ You have approximately 4–5 minutes to deliver a previously well-oxygenated term infant before the risk of hypoxic brain injury becomes significant — announce the emergency, call for help, and start the clock immediately.
⚠ Grasp the forearm or wrist only when delivering the posterior arm — traction on the upper arm (humerus) carries much higher fracture risk.
⚠ Zavanelli maneuver requires emergency cesarean section setup BEFORE cephalic replacement — do not attempt without OR readiness.
⚠ When all standard maneuvers have failed, the risk of brachial plexus or other injury must be accepted to save the baby's life.

Recognition

Shoulder dystocia occurs when the baby's anterior shoulder becomes impacted behind the mother's pubic symphysis after delivery of the fetal head — the body does not follow as expected. It cannot be predicted or prevented by any specific strategy or maneuver, and occurs in approximately 1.5% of vaginal deliveries.

  • Failure to deliver — the body does not emerge with standard traction and maternal pushing after the head delivers
  • Turtle sign — the fetal head retracts back against the perineum after emerging, cheeks bulging, as the anterior shoulder catches on the pubic bone
No reliable prediction or prevention
Cannot be predicted with any degree of accuracy and cannot be prevented by any specific strategy or maneuver.

Initial response

  1. Announce "shoulder dystocia" clearly to the room
  2. Call for help — request a second obstetrician if available
  3. Call pediatrics/NICU for neonatal evaluation and potential resuscitation
  4. Call anesthesia in case procedural intervention is needed
  5. Designate a timekeeper — call out elapsed time every 30 seconds
  6. Designate a note taker to record timing of events and maneuvers attempted
4–5 minute window
You have approximately 4–5 minutes to deliver a previously well-oxygenated term infant before the risk of hypoxic brain injury becomes significant. This window depends on the fetus's oxygenation and acidosis status prior to onset.

Some traction is always applied to the infant's head during delivery — this is standard obstetric practice. ACOG describes applying axial traction below the horizontal plane. There is no universally accepted threshold for "excessive force." When all maneuvers are exhausted and the only options are maximal extraction effort, hypoxic injury, or fetal death, the risk of brachial plexus or other injury must be accepted to save the baby's life.

Management algorithm

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for post-delivery documentation detail and the complete last-resort workup.

First-line: McRoberts + suprapubic pressure

McRoberts maneuver and suprapubic pressure should be implemented rapidly and simultaneously as the first intervention. Together they resolve 50–60% of all shoulder dystocias.

  1. Sharply flex the mother's legs onto her abdomen (McRoberts) — rotates the symphysis cephalad and straightens the sacrum
  2. An assistant places a fist just above the pubic bone and pushes the fetal anterior shoulder toward the oblique diameter (suprapubic pressure)
  3. McRoberts alone resolves 39–42% of cases; combined with suprapubic pressure, 50–60%
Rare complications
Symphyseal separation and transient femoral neuropathy from overly aggressive hyperflexion are rare but reported.

Second-line: rotational + posterior arm

Rotational maneuvers use progressive corkscrew rotation of the posterior shoulder to release the impacted anterior shoulder. Try both Wood's screw (pressure on the anterior surface of the posterior shoulder) and Rubin's maneuver (pressure on the posterior surface, flexing the shoulders across the chest) — try both clockwise and counter-clockwise, since one direction may succeed where the other fails.

Delivery of the posterior arm has the highest single-maneuver success rate of any shoulder dystocia maneuver — 84.4% (Hoffman 2011 NICHD Consortium on Safe Labor) — far exceeding McRoberts (24.3%), suprapubic pressure (62.2%), or rotational maneuvers (66.0-72.0% — Rubin/Woods corkscrew). Poggi (2003) and Chauhan (2010) argue it should be prioritized earlier in the sequence rather than reserved as a last resort.

  1. Reach along the posterior vaginal wall (sacral hollow) with the hand matching the fetal ventral surface
  2. Trace the posterior shoulder down to the elbow; identify the antecubital fossa
  3. Apply firm pressure to the antecubital fossa to flex the forearm across the fetal chest
  4. Grasp the forearm or wrist only — never the upper arm (humerus)
  5. Sweep the arm across the fetal chest in an arc, delivering the hand first, then forearm and shoulder
  6. If the anterior shoulder is still impacted, rotate the baby 180° so the delivered posterior shoulder becomes anterior
Arm not reachable → Menticoglou modification
Hook the index and middle fingers into the posterior axilla and apply gentle traction along the long axis of the humerus to deliver the posterior shoulder first — then sweep the arm out as usual. Menticoglou reported 100% success in 6 consecutive severe cases where conventional posterior arm delivery had failed.
Grasp forearm/wrist only
Humeral fracture risk is much higher with upper-arm traction. With proper forearm-grasp technique the rate is ~4%; in the older Gherman series using upper-arm traction it was 12.4%. Almost all humeral fractures heal completely with simple immobilization.

Last resort

If all standard maneuvers fail, last-resort options carry significant maternal and/or fetal risk.

Consider earlier posterior arm delivery
Posterior arm delivery has the highest single-maneuver success rate (84.4%, Hoffman 2011) — consider prioritizing it earlier in the sequence if first-line maneuvers fail, rather than working through every rotational attempt first.

Resolved — postpartum management

  1. Immediate neonatal assessment (pediatrics/NICU) with Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes
  2. Evaluate for brachial plexus palsy (Erb's) — asymmetric arm movement, "waiter's tip" posture
  3. Evaluate for clavicle fracture — crepitus, asymmetric Moro reflex
  4. Evaluate for humeral fracture if posterior arm delivery was performed
  5. Maternal assessment — vaginal/cervical lacerations, postpartum hemorrhage
  6. Document head-to-body interval, all maneuvers attempted and their order, personnel and roles, neonatal condition, and estimated blood loss
Injury is not always substandard care
Sometimes, even in the most expert hands and with relatively mild shoulder dystocias, fetal or maternal injury will occur. Injury does not necessarily indicate substandard care.
