Acute Limb Ischemia & Chronic PAD/CLTI — ED Management

Adults ≥18 in the ED with suspected acute limb ischemia or the chronic PAD/CLTI spectrum. Differentiates true arterial ischemia from mimics (phlegmasia cerulea dolens, compartment syndrome from another cause, aortic dissection extending to the iliacs, neurologic causes of weakness) rather than managing those conditions directly. Excludes pediatric limb ischemia.

⚠ Acute limb ischemia = vascular emergency. Pale, painful, pulseless, cold limb until proven otherwise. Skeletal muscle tolerates only about 6 hours of ischemia before irreversible necrosis.[4][5][7]
⚠ Start IV unfractionated heparin immediately unless contraindicated — do NOT wait for imaging to anticoagulate or obtain vascular consult.[2][7]
⚠ Rutherford IIb = EMERGENT revascularization (surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy or bypass) — catheter-directed lysis alone is too slow for a motor-deficit limb. Rutherford III = primary amputation pathway.[1][2][6]
⚠ Emergent vascular surgery / endovascular consult for any Rutherford IIa/IIb/III (Category I urgently) — transfer immediately if no vascular service.[1][6][7]
⚠ Do NOT reperfuse a nonviable (Rutherford III) limb — releases potassium, myoglobin, and acid load and can be lethal. Primary amputation, not revascularization.[1][6]
⚠ After revascularization, watch for reperfusion syndrome: hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis/AKI, and compartment syndrome — fasciotomy for delta pressure <30 mmHg (gold standard).[2][5][18]
⚠ HIT history (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) → use a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban, bivalirudin) instead of heparin.[2]

1 · Recognize acute limb ischemia

  • Pain — early, severe
  • Pallor — pale/marble-white skin
  • Pulselessness — absent distal pulses
  • Paresthesia — sensory loss (early, ominous)
  • Paralysis — motor deficit (late, limb-threatening)
  • Poikilothermia — perishingly cold

A sudden pale, painful, pulseless, cold limb is a true vascular emergency. Skeletal muscle tolerates only about 6 hours of ischemia before irreversible necrosis — paresthesia and paralysis are the early ominous markers.[4][5]

  • Two large-bore IVs, IV analgesia, NPO (likely OR)
  • Keep the limb dependent and warm-not-hot — never apply direct heat to insensate skin (burn risk)
  • Handheld Doppler of pedal arterial + venous signals — the priority bedside test
  • Send labs and call vascular EARLY

Acute = sudden, symptoms <2 weeks, often no collaterals. Chronic PAD = gradual claudication history, collateral-supported, prior known disease. Fixed mottling/skin staining that does NOT blanch = irreversible (Rutherford III).[1][4]

3 · What to do now — by category

Set the pathway above (acute vs chronic) and, if acute, the Rutherford category — the exact next action appears below.

Category I — Viable
Not immediately threatened: no sensory/motor loss, audible arterial AND venous Doppler. Start IV heparin now. Urgent (not emergent) revascularization, often after CT angiography. Admit, serial vascular exams.[1][2]
Category IIa — Marginally threatened
Salvageable if promptly treated: minimal toe sensory loss, no motor deficit, often inaudible arterial/audible venous. Heparin immediately. Urgent endovascular or open revascularization; catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) well-suited — there is time and no motor threat. Emergent vascular consult.[1][2]
Category IIb — Immediately threatened
Salvageable only with IMMEDIATE revascularization: sensory loss beyond the toes + rest pain, mild-to-moderate motor deficit, usually inaudible arterial Doppler. Heparin NOW. EMERGENT surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy (Fogarty) or bypass; mechanical/aspiration thrombectomy. Lysis alone is usually too slow for a motor-deficit limb. Vascular surgery to the bedside immediately; mobilize the OR.[1][2]
Category III — Irreversible
Major tissue and nerve loss is inevitable: profound anesthesia and paralysis (rigor), inaudible arterial AND venous Doppler, fixed non-blanching staining. → Primary amputation pathway. 🛑 Do NOT reperfuse a dead limb. Supportive IV fluids; vascular surgery for amputation planning; ICU as needed.[1][6]
Chronic PAD / CLTI — no acute threat
Gradual claudication, rest pain, or tissue loss with no acute threat: go to the Chronic PAD/CLTI pathway below — start guideline-directed medical therapy and refer for vascular evaluation on the urgency the tissue findings dictate.[13]

4 · Anticoagulation — the cornerstone

Start IV unfractionated heparin immediately unless contraindicated — do NOT wait for imaging. Class 1 recommendation. It halts thrombus propagation and recurrent embolism.[2][7]

Dosing (follow your institutional heparin protocol): bolus ~80 U/kg IV, then infusion ~18 U/kg/hr, titrated to aPTT 1.5–2.5× control. These figures are an ESVS-aligned/institutional range, not a single guideline-stamped number — use your hospital's weight-based heparin nomogram.[2]

5 · Revascularization & reperfusion

Category I: anticoagulate; urgent (not emergent) endo or open revascularization, often after imaging. Category IIa: urgent endovascular or open; CDT well-suited when there is time and no motor threat. Category IIb: EMERGENT — surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy (Fogarty) or bypass; mechanical/aspiration thrombectomy; lysis alone usually too slow. Etiology nuance: embolic classically favors embolectomy; thrombotic-on-PAD often favors thrombolysis/bypass/hybrid based on anatomy.[1][2][3]

Reperfusion washes potassium, myoglobin, and reactive oxygen species into the circulation → hyperkalemia (arrhythmia), metabolic acidosis, myoglobinuria/AKI, and DIC.[5][12]

Compartment syndrome
Primarily a clinical diagnosis (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch). Delta pressure (diastolic BP − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg is more reliable than an absolute compartment pressure >30 mmHg. Fasciotomy is the gold standard; the calf is most vulnerable (4-compartment release). Already-ischemic muscle tolerates pressure worse — post-reperfusion clinicians may use a delta-P cutoff up to ~40 mmHg, but prophylactic fasciotomy after prolonged ischemia is common regardless of the number. 2024 ACC/AHA: fasciotomy Class 1 after revascularization; prophylactic fasciotomy reasonable for Category IIa/IIb, especially with ischemia time >6 h, Class 2a.[2][5][7][18]
  • Aggressive IV fluids; monitor CK, myoglobin, K⁺, creatinine
  • Treat hyperkalemia aggressively with ECG monitoring
  • Dialysis for refractory hyperkalemia, oliguric AKI, or severe acidosis

6 · Workup — labs, imaging, source hunt

Labs, imaging, embolic source huntCTA first-line

Labs (reperfusion/rhabdo baseline — NOT gatekeepers for revascularization): CK, myoglobin, potassium, creatinine, lactate, CBC, coags (PT/PTT before heparin), type & screen. Do not use CK/lactate to decide whether to revascularize.[2][5][12]

CT angiography = first-line (ESVS 2020): fast, available, single acquisition of aorta + iliacs + runoff; finds the embolic source and relevant findings in up to 74%. Extend through the entire aorta/iliacs.[8]

Handheld / duplex ultrasound — best initial bedside test for flow; helps plan in still-viable limbs. Catheter angiography (DSA) — preferred for the immediately-threatened limb because it doubles as the therapeutic platform (lysis/thrombectomy). Do NOT delay revascularization for imaging when ischemia is severe.[1][3][8]

Embolic source hunt: ECG (AFib), echocardiography (mural thrombus, valve, LV aneurysm), and the CTA aorta itself. Check perfusion non-invasively with the ABI calculator when the limb is viable.[3][7]

7 · Chronic PAD / CLTI pathway

FontaineRutherford (chronic)
I asymptomatic0 asymptomatic
IIa claudication >200 m1–3 mild→severe claudication
IIb claudication <200 m—
III rest pain4 rest pain
IV ulcer/gangrene5 minor / 6 major tissue loss

Chronic disease runs asymptomatic → intermittent claudication → ischemic rest pain → tissue loss (ulcer/gangrene). End stage = Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia (CLTI) = Rutherford 4–6 / Fontaine III–IV. CLTI hemodynamic criteria (Global Vascular Guidelines 2019): ischemic rest pain >2 weeks plus ABI <0.40, ankle pressure <50 mmHg, or toe pressure <30 mmHg (toe pressure preferred in diabetics with incompressible vessels).[10][17]

  • Antiplatelet — aspirin or clopidogrel for all symptomatic PAD/CLTI
  • Moderate-to-high-intensity statin — reduces all-cause and CV mortality (Class 1)
  • Smoking cessation — offered to every tobacco user
  • BP to <140/90; metformin first-line for diabetic CLTI
  • Cilostazol 100 mg BID — improves pain-free walking distance and ABI in claudication; avoid in HFrEF; benefit in CLTI less clear
  • Supervised exercise therapy — Class 1A first-line for intermittent claudication (30–60 min sessions, walk-to-moderate-discomfort/rest cycles, ~+180 m max walking distance)
  • Avoid vitamin K antagonists for the atherosclerosis itself

Refer to vascular surgery for ischemic rest pain, non-healing ulcer/gangrene (CLTI), lifestyle-limiting claudication failing medical therapy, or any ABI/TBI meeting CLTI criteria. CLTI without an acute threat → urgent (not emergent) vascular referral; start GDMT in parallel.[11][13]

9 · Disposition, mimics & Do NOT

Emergent vascular surgery / endovascular consult NOW for any Rutherford IIa/IIb/III (and Category I urgently). ICU for hemodynamic instability, large muscle mass at risk, anticipated reperfusion syndrome, or post-revascularization monitoring. Floor/telemetry for stable Category I on heparin awaiting planned intervention. No vascular service → transfer immediately — start heparin and analgesia before/during transfer; keep the limb warm-not-hot and dependent. Category III → surgery for amputation planning + supportive care. CLTI without acute threat → urgent (not emergent) vascular referral; start GDMT.[1][2][6][7]

  • Phlegmasia cerulea dolens (massive iliofemoral DVT): swollen, WARM, cyanotic limb with initially audible arterial Doppler — the opposite of the pale/cold/pulseless arterial limb. Up to 40% mortality, ~50% amputation; can coexist with ALI. Confirm with venous duplex. Malignancy in 20–40%; left leg favored (May-Thurner).
  • Acute compartment syndrome from another cause (trauma, crush, prolonged immobilization) — pain out of proportion, pressure >30 mmHg.
  • Aortic dissection extending to the iliacs — can present as a unilateral pulseless cold leg; CTA aorta catches it.
  • Neurologic causes of weakness — cord compression, cauda equina, peripheral nerve compression — mimic "paralysis" but with preserved pulses/perfusion. A saddle embolus can itself cause paraplegia — don't anchor on a pure neuro cause.
  • Necrotizing soft-tissue infection can also masquerade as ischemic skin change.
Additional critical pitfalls5 items
