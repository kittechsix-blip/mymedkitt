Altitude Sickness — AMS / HACE / HAPE

Adults presenting to the ED with acute altitude illness (AMS, HACE, or HAPE) after recent gain in altitude. Based on Wilderness Medical Society 2024 guidelines and the 2018 Lake Louise AMS Score. Pediatric dosing is referenced only for acetazolamide prophylaxis/treatment.

⚠ Ataxia in a patient with AMS is HACE, not AMS — HACE can be fatal within 24 hours if untreated. HACE and HAPE can coexist; treat mixed symptoms as critical.
⚠ HACE: DESCEND IMMEDIATELY (minimum 1000m / 3000 ft) — this is the definitive treatment; do not wait for medications to work. Do NOT give sedatives (mask neurologic decline) or excessive IV fluids (worsen edema).
⚠ HAPE diagnostic criteria: ≥2 symptoms + ≥2 signs. DESCEND IMMEDIATELY (minimum 1000m / 3000 ft); supplemental O2 to SpO2 >90% is the most important intervention if descent is delayed.
⚠ HAPE: do NOT give diuretics (patient is volume depleted) or dexamethasone alone (does not treat HAPE) — unless HACE coexists.
⚠ Do NOT combine nifedipine with a PDE5 inhibitor (sildenafil/tadalafil) for HAPE — additive hypotension. Pick one agent.
⚠ Do NOT delay descent for moderate-severe AMS, HACE, or HAPE if descent is available — medications and hyperbaric therapy are adjuncts, not substitutes.

Overview & Initial Assessment

Altitude Illness Spectrum: AMS (Acute Mountain Sickness — mild), HACE (High Altitude Cerebral Edema — severe), HAPE (High Altitude Pulmonary Edema — severe).

Key history: current/recent altitude exposure, rate of ascent, time at altitude, prior altitude illness, acclimatization attempts.

Risk factors: rapid ascent, sleeping at altitude, prior AMS/HACE/HAPE, exertion, dehydration.

Lake Louise AMS Score

Recent gain in altitude PLUS headache AND ≥1 of the symptoms below. Any ataxia = HACE (not AMS).

Scoring
Score 3-5 = Mild AMS. Score 6+ = Moderate-Severe AMS. Any ataxia = HACE, not AMS.

AMS Management

Mild AMS (Lake Louise 3-5): STOP ascent — do not go higher. Symptomatic treatment: Acetaminophen or Ibuprofen for headache; ondansetron for nausea. Rest at current altitude, hydrate, avoid alcohol.

Key point: AMS is self-limiting if ascent stops. Most improve within 24-72h at the same altitude.

HACE — High Altitude Cerebral Edema

HACE is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY
AMS progressed to encephalopathy. Diagnostic criteria: recent altitude gain + (mental status change and/or ataxia in a patient with AMS) OR both ataxia AND mental status change even without AMS history.

Clinical features: truncal ataxia (cannot walk heel-to-toe), confusion, drowsiness progressing to coma, behavioral changes, hallucinations, papilledema.

Mortality: can be fatal within 24 hours if untreated.
  1. DESCEND IMMEDIATELY — minimum 1000m (3000 ft). This is definitive treatment; do not wait for medications to work.
  2. Dexamethasone 8mg IV/IM/PO x1, then 4mg q6h.
  3. Supplemental O2 — maintain SpO2 >90%.
  4. Keep patient warm (hypothermia worsens outcome).

If descent impossible
Portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag) at 2-4 PSI — temporary measure only, still need descent ASAP. Continue dexamethasone. Evacuation: helicopter if available.

Monitoring: neurologic checks q1-2h, GCS tracking, pulse oximetry, watch for concurrent HAPE (common).

Response to descent: should improve within hours; full recovery expected if treated promptly. Persistent symptoms: consider CT/MRI (white matter edema, especially corpus callosum; may be normal early; not required for diagnosis if classic presentation).

Return to altitude: avoid re-ascent for at least 72 hours after resolution, slower ascent rate, prophylaxis (acetazolamide or dexamethasone). Some recommend avoiding high altitude permanently after severe HACE.

HAPE — High Altitude Pulmonary Edema

HAPE is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY
Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema from hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Diagnostic criteria (≥2 symptoms + ≥2 signs): dyspnea at rest, cough (dry→productive), decreased exercise tolerance, chest tightness / crackles or wheezing, central cyanosis, tachypnea, tachycardia.

Typically occurs: 2-4 days after arrival, more common >2500m (8000 ft), often at night. Risk factors: prior HAPE, rapid ascent, exertion, cold, viral URI, unilateral PA.
  1. DESCEND IMMEDIATELY — minimum 1000m (3000 ft). Definitive treatment; patient may need to be carried.
  2. Supplemental O2 — high flow, target SpO2 >90%. Most important intervention if descent is delayed.
  3. Minimize exertion — even walking worsens hypoxia.
  4. Keep patient warm.

Medications Reference

Altitude medications — full dosing detail (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, sildenafil/tadalafil)Prevention doses, pediatric dosing, drug interactions

Acetazolamide (Diamox): carbonic anhydrase inhibitor — induces metabolic acidosis → hyperventilation. Prevention: 125mg PO BID, start 1 day before ascent (RADICAL trial: 125mg superior to 62.5mg). Treatment: 250mg PO BID. Side effects: paresthesias, dysgeusia, polyuria. Absolute contraindication: prior anaphylaxis or SJS to a sulfonamide; non-anaphylactic sulfa allergy is a relative CI (supervised pre-trip trial acceptable per WMS 2024). Severe renal/hepatic disease.

—Pediatric acetazolamide — treatment

Dexamethasone: reduces vasogenic edema, stabilizes BBB. Prevention: 2mg q6h or 4mg q12h. AMS treatment: 4mg q6h. HACE treatment: 8mg x1, then 4mg q6h. Caution: does not aid acclimatization.

Nifedipine (for HAPE): reduces pulmonary artery pressure. Dose: 30mg SR q12h or 20mg SR q8h. Use extended-release only.

Sildenafil/Tadalafil (for HAPE): alternative to nifedipine; tadalafil 10mg q12h elevated to alternative for prevention (WMS 2024 strong rec). Treatment use is a weak recommendation — only if descent/O2/hyperbaric/nifedipine unavailable. Sildenafil 50mg q8h, Tadalafil 10mg q12h. Do NOT combine nifedipine with a PDE5 inhibitor — additive hypotension. Pick one.

Disposition

Admit ICU: HACE with altered mental status, severe HAPE requiring intubation/high O2, concurrent HACE + HAPE, hemodynamically unstable.

Admit floor: moderate HAPE with O2 requirement, HACE responding to treatment, need for observation after descent.

Discharge: mild AMS improved with treatment, patient has descended to safe altitude, no hypoxia, normal mental status, can return to lower altitude.

ICU orders: continuous pulse oximetry; neuro checks q1-2h (HACE); supplemental O2 to maintain SpO2 >90%; IV access, labs (CBC, BMP, ABG); CXR; CT head if focal neuro signs or not improving. Avoid excessive IV fluids and sedatives. Consults: pulmonology (HAPE), neurology (HACE with persistent deficits). Expected course: significant improvement within 24-48h with supportive care at low altitude.
