Syncope Evaluation — ED Management

Adults presenting to the ED with transient loss of consciousness (TLOC) concerning for syncope. Excludes confirmed seizure (route to seizure pathway), TLOC clearly due to trauma or intoxication as the primary mechanism, and cardiac arrest. Pediatric and elderly nuances are called out separately — this is not a pediatric- or geriatric-specific protocol.

⚠ TLOC with persistent abnormal vitals, neuro deficit, chest pain, or hemorrhage is NOT simple syncope until life-threatening causes are excluded
⚠ ECG is mandatory for ALL syncope patients — even those with a classic vasovagal story
⚠ QTc >460 ms is concerning; QTc >500 ms is high-risk for arrhythmia
⚠ Normal orthostatic vitals do NOT predict 30-day outcomes in elderly patients — do not rely on them alone to exclude serious disease
⚠ Head CT is NOT indicated for asymptomatic syncope with a normal neurologic exam and no significant head trauma
⚠ Routine D-dimer is NOT indicated in syncope unless PE is independently suspected by PERC/Wells

Life-threat screen

Patient presents with transient loss of consciousness (TLOC). First priority: exclude immediately life-threatening conditions before treating this as simple syncope.

  • Persistent abnormal vital signs (hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, hypoxia, tachypnea)
  • Neurologic deficit or headache
  • Chest pain or dyspnea
  • Abdominal pain or pulsatile mass
  • Evidence of hemorrhage or hypovolemia
TLOC is NOT simple syncope yet
Direct the workup at the presenting cluster: hypotension + tachycardia → hemorrhage, PE, sepsis, AAA. Chest pain → ACS, aortic dissection, PE, tension pneumothorax. Headache + neuro deficit → SAH, stroke, ICH. Abdominal pain → ruptured AAA, ectopic pregnancy. Hypoxia + tachypnea → PE, pneumothorax. Persistent bradycardia → high-grade AV block, sick sinus. Do not diagnose syncope until life-threatening causes are excluded.

Seizure suspected
Manage per seizure pathway. Serum lactate within 2 hours of the event is superior to CK for distinguishing seizure from syncope. If seizure is excluded after further workup, return to syncope evaluation.

ECG assessment

ECG is mandatory for ALL syncope patients, even suspected vasovagal syncope. Place on continuous telemetry — in low-risk patients, 50% of arrhythmic causes are detected within 2 hours; 6 hours for medium/high-risk.

  • Nonsinus rhythm or significant ectopy
  • Conduction abnormalities (AV block, BBB, pre-excitation)
  • QTc prolongation (>460 ms concerning, >500 ms high-risk)
  • ST changes or Q waves suggesting ischemia
  • High-risk patterns: Brugada, HCM, epsilon waves
Abnormal ECG — high-risk feature
Abnormal ECG is a high-risk feature in ALL syncope risk stratification tools. Findings associated with serious arrhythmias within 30 days: nonsinus rhythm, Mobitz II/third-degree AV block, bundle branch block (especially new LBBB), QTc >460 ms, pre-excitation (WPW), Brugada type 1, epsilon waves, dagger Q waves (HCM), ST depression/T-wave inversion. Atrial fibrillation, IVCD, LVH, and ventricular pacing are independent predictors of mortality.

Focused history

The history is the single most important tool in identifying the cause of syncope. Assess activity at onset, prodrome, events during and after the episode, prior episodes, medications, cardiac history, and family history of sudden cardiac death.

PE screening
PE is uncommon among syncope patients and existing clinical decision rules (PERC) adequately identify those who need PE workup. Routine D-dimer is NOT indicated without independent suspicion. Consider PE for unexplained persistent tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnea, or hypoxia; recent surgery/immobilization; or syncope with dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain — apply standard PERC → Wells → CTPA only when clinically indicated.
Head CT is usually not indicated
ACEP Choosing Wisely: avoid head CT in asymptomatic patients with syncope, minimal trauma, and a normal neurologic exam — head CT has very low yield (<0.1%) in determining the cause of syncope. Only obtain head CT for significant head trauma from the fall, a neurologic deficit on exam, or suspicion for SAH (thunderclap headache).

Labs are low-yield unless directed by history/exam. Troponin (hs-cTnT) elevation is an independent predictor of 30-day mortality; elevated BNP/NT-proBNP confers ~8× increased risk of serious outcomes. Do NOT routinely obtain D-dimer, head CT, EEG, or stress testing unless clinically directed.

Vasovagal / reflex syncope

Neurally mediated (reflex) syncope
Most common identified cause of syncope, with good long-term prognosis. Subtypes: vasovagal (pain/blood/emotion/procedure trigger, prodrome, age <40), situational (Valsalva, post-exercise, cough, swallow, defecation), and carotid sinus (head-turning, shaving, age >40). Recurrence is common but not a predictor of mortality. If ECG is normal and history is classic, this is a low-risk diagnosis — discharge with reassurance, trigger avoidance, and counterpressure maneuver counseling.
Carotid sinus massage (age >40)
AHA and ESC recommend carotid sinus massage in patients >40 years with undiagnosed syncope after initial evaluation — diagnostic in up to 60% of appropriate candidates. Positive result: syncope reproduced, asystole >3 seconds, AV block, or SBP drop ≥50 mmHg. Contraindicated with carotid bruit, known carotid stenosis >70%, TIA/stroke or MI within 3 months, or history of VT/VF. Complication rate is ~0.1% neurologic complications — perform with monitoring and resuscitation equipment available.

Orthostatic hypotension

Orthostatic hypotension = SBP drop >20 mmHg (or DBP drop >10 mmHg) within 3 minutes of standing, or symptoms with position change. Most common cause is medications (~40% of cases): antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers, nitrates, TCAs/MAOIs, antipsychotics. Other causes: volume depletion, autonomic failure, adrenal insufficiency.

Orthostatics do not clear the elderly patient
Orthostatic hypotension does NOT exclude more serious causes, especially in the elderly — one study found no difference in 30-day serious outcomes between elderly patients with normal vs abnormal orthostatic vitals. Route these patients to full risk stratification rather than a reassuring diagnosis on orthostatics alone.
Modifiable cause — treat and discharge
Give IV fluids if volume-depleted, review and adjust offending medications, educate on slow position changes, and consider compression stockings plus increased salt/fluid intake for recurrent episodes. PCP follow-up for medication reconciliation. Identifying orthostasis allows modification of fall risk factors, but orthostasis alone should NOT be the sole risk-stratification factor.

Cardiac syncope

Cardiac syncope carries the highest morbidity of all causes. Features suggesting it: syncope during exertion, palpitations immediately before, syncope while supine, no prodrome (abrupt), structural heart disease, family history of sudden cardiac death, and abnormal ECG. Arrhythmias are the most common cardiac cause and may have resolved by ED evaluation; structural causes (PE, aortic stenosis, HCM, dissection) typically persist and are more easily identified.

Structural heart disease + syncope = HIGH RISK
Recommended workup: continuous telemetry (minimum 6 hours for high-risk), troponin (hs-cTnT), BNP/NT-proBNP, and echocardiography — high yield when HF, CAD, abnormal ECG, or elevated biomarkers are present. Otherwise echo has low yield for most syncope patients.
Arrhythmia suspected
Clues: abrupt onset without prodrome, syncope while sitting/supine, palpitations preceding the event, abnormal ECG, or a known cardiac device. Continuous telemetry detects 50% of arrhythmic causes within 2 hours (low-risk) or 6 hours (medium/high-risk); consider ambulatory monitoring referral — higher diagnostic yield if initiated within 15 days of the event. If arrhythmia is captured, manage per the specific rhythm; if not, proceed to risk stratification.

Risk stratification & disposition

For unexplained syncope after history, exam, and ECG, apply risk stratification. Tools do not consistently outperform clinical judgment but help identify high-risk features: Canadian Syncope Risk Score (best validated) and San Francisco Syncope Rule (simple binary screen). High-risk (ESC): major structural/coronary disease, clinical/ECG features suggesting arrhythmic syncope, syncope causing severe injury, or important comorbidities (severe anemia, electrolyte disturbance).

High-risk — admit
Admit to a monitored bed with cardiology consultation, continuous telemetry, and echocardiography if not already obtained. Indications include sustained/symptomatic VT, symptomatic conduction disease or high-grade AV block, symptomatic bradycardia not reflex-mediated, pacemaker/ICD malfunction, inheritable arrhythmia syndromes, cardiac ischemia, severe aortic stenosis, tamponade, HCM, aortic dissection, PE, or significant hemorrhage.
Intermediate risk — observation vs. outpatient
Shared decision-making. Intermediate-risk factors: age ≥50, prior cardiac disease, cardiac device without dysfunction, family history of early sudden cardiac death, symptoms not consistent with reflex syncope. Patients with cardiac PMH but a benign ED cause identified (e.g., dehydration) may be discharged safely. A structured ED observation protocol may reduce admissions; if discharged, ensure close follow-up and consider ambulatory monitoring, tilt-table testing, or EP referral.
Low risk — discharge
Low-risk patients should generally be discharged, even with unexplained syncope: young age with typical reflex features, no cardiac history, syncope only when standing, identifiable trigger, normal ECG, normal vital signs. Counsel on trigger avoidance, counterpressure maneuvers, hydration/salt intake, and fall/injury prevention. Recurrence is ~9% recurrence within 6 months; 3+ lifetime episodes is most predictive of future recurrence. Address driving restrictions per state law for commercial drivers or high-risk occupations. PCP follow-up for recurrent or unexplained syncope.

Special populations

Pediatric syncope mirrors the adult approach — history, exam, and ECG are the most useful components. 80% of pediatric syncope is neurally mediated. Features suggesting a cardiac cause in children: exertional syncope, no prodrome, supine syncope, family history of sudden cardiac death, or syncope with a loud noise (evaluate for long QT syndrome). Avoid excessive testing — unless cardiac features are present, pediatric syncope can be managed conservatively with ECG and PCP follow-up.

Elderly syncope is particularly challenging — many present with unexplained falls rather than syncope, and 25-50% of unexplained falls in elderly may be syncope-related. Neurally mediated syncope is still common in this age group. QTc prolongation occurs in 25% of elderly patients; QTc >500 ms predicts 30-day and 1-year mortality. For unexplained syncope WITHOUT a serious ED diagnosis, hospitalization does not improve 30-day adverse outcomes — mortality relates primarily to underlying comorbidities, not the syncope itself.
