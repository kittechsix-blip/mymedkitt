Syncope: ECG First, Then Risk-Stratify

Adults ≥18 presenting to the ED after transient loss of consciousness. Excludes pediatrics, pregnancy, and non-syncopal loss of consciousness (primary seizure, hypoglycemia, intoxication, head trauma).

⚠ Exclude life-threatening causes before diagnosing simple syncope. TLOC with persistent abnormal vitals, neuro deficit, chest pain, or signs of hemorrhage is NOT simple syncope until serious diagnoses are ruled out.
⚠ ECG is mandatory for ALL syncope patients — even suspected vasovagal. Abnormal ECG is a high-risk feature in every syncope risk tool. QTc >460 ms is concerning; QTc >500 ms is high-risk.
⚠ Normal orthostatic vitals do NOT exclude serious causes and do not predict 30-day outcomes in the elderly — never rely on them alone.
⚠ Head CT is NOT indicated for asymptomatic syncope with a normal neuro exam; routine D-dimer is NOT indicated unless PE is suspected by PERC/Wells.

1 · First: is this actually dangerous?

TLOC is not syncope until you exclude killers
Transient loss of consciousness with persistent abnormal vitals, a neuro deficit, chest pain, dyspnea, abdominal pain, or signs of hemorrhage is a life-threatening presentation — not simple syncope.[1] Map the red flag to the killer: hypotension+tachycardia → hemorrhage / PE / sepsis / AAA · chest pain → ACS / dissection / PE · headache+deficit → SAH / stroke · abdominal pain → ruptured AAA / ectopic · persistent bradycardia → high-grade AV block.

For everyone else, the single non-negotiable first test is the ECG. ECG is mandatory for ALL syncope patients — even those who look classically vasovagal — because an abnormal tracing is a high-risk feature in every validated tool.[1][2]

Resist reflex imaging
Head CT is NOT indicated for asymptomatic syncope with a normal neuro exam.[18] Routine D-dimer is NOT indicated unless PE is suspected by PERC/Wells.[19]

2 · ECG — mandatory for every patient

Place the patient on continuous telemetry. In low-risk patients, 50% of arrhythmic causes are detected within 2 hours; extend to 6 hours for medium/high-risk.[6]

Read the QTc carefully: >460 ms is concerning and >500 ms is high-risk for a malignant arrhythmia.[16] Enter the measured QTc to plot it on the risk scale:

4 · Risk & disposition — what to do now

Set the toggles for this patient. Exactly one instruction below activates, in strict precedence: life-threat → high-risk → cardiac → abnormal ECG → low-risk. Risk tools (CSRS is best validated; SFSR is a simple binary screen) support — but do not replace — clinical judgment.[14][15]

Do now — resuscitate the killer
Life-threatening features are present. This is not simple syncope. Resuscitate and pursue the specific diagnosis (ACS, dissection, PE, SAH, ruptured AAA, high-grade AV block) with targeted ECG, labs, and imaging. Do not risk-stratify as syncope until the killer is excluded.[1]
Do now — admit / observation unit
High-risk feature present → hospital admission or observation with continuous telemetry and cardiology involvement. Triggers: sustained/symptomatic VT, high-grade AV block, symptomatic bradycardia or pauses, severe aortic stenosis, HCM, or an inherited arrhythmia syndrome.[1][2]
Do now — cardiac workup
Structural heart disease + syncope = HIGH RISK. Order hs-cTnT and BNP/NT-proBNP (each independently predicts 30-day mortality) and echocardiography when HF, CAD, an abnormal ECG, or elevated biomarkers are present. Continuous telemetry for a minimum of 6 hours for medium/high-risk.[8][12]
Do now — treat as intermediate, monitor
The ECG is abnormal but there is no overt structural or high-risk feature yet. Keep on telemetry (50% of arrhythmic causes are detected within 2 hours), obtain biomarkers, and arrange early ambulatory monitoring — abnormal ECG never discharges as low-risk on the tracing alone.[6][13]
Do now — discharge with counseling
No high-risk feature, normal ECG, normal vitals, typical reflex trigger → discharge, even when the cause is unexplained.[2] Counsel on trigger avoidance, counterpressure maneuvers, hydration/salt, and fall prevention; arrange PCP follow-up.
ESC high-risk vs low-risk featuresrisk cheat-sheet
High-risk (ESC)
Major structural or coronary disease, abnormal ECG, syncope during exertion or while supine, palpitations before syncope, or a family history of sudden cardiac death.[2]
Low-risk (ESC)
Young, typical reflex features, identifiable trigger, syncope only when standing, normal ECG, and normal vitals.[2]

Intermediate (age ≥50, prior cardiac disease, device without dysfunction, family history of early SCD) → shared decision-making; a benign ED-identified cause such as dehydration may be discharged safely.[17]
