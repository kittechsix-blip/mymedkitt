Bronchiolitis: Suction, Score, Support — Not Salbutamol

Previously healthy infants 28 days–24 months in the ED with first-episode bronchiolitis. Excludes chronic lung disease/cystic fibrosis, congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, neuromuscular disease, abnormal airway, and recurrent (>3 episodes) wheezing — each needs individualized management off this pathway.

⚠ NOT recommended: CXR, viral testing, albuterol, steroids, antibiotics — none have a role in standard bronchiolitis and each adds cost, radiation, or harm without benefit.
⚠ Use the BAS Calculator for objective severity scoring before AND after every intervention — clinician gestalt drifts; the score anchors the disposition.
⚠ Target SpO₂ ≥90% awake or ≥88% asleep — do NOT over-oxygenate. Chasing a normal saturation drives needless admissions and monitor dependence.
⚠ HFNC starting flow: <7 kg = 4 LPM, ≥7 kg = 6 LPM (start FiO₂ 21%, titrate to SpO₂ ≥90%). Watch ≥30 minutes in the ED after starting.
⚠ On HFNC, monitor BAS every 15 minutes; if BAS ≥9, suction FIRST, then reassess. Escalation triggers: worsening after 60 minutes, FiO₂ >50%, flow above max, or apnea.
⚠ PICU/critical-care for worsening >60 min on HFNC, witnessed apnea, need for PPV, FiO₂ >50%, or severe dehydration/shock. Prepare for intubation and ensure IV access.

1 · Recognize & scope

Who is on this pathway
Age >28 days and <24 months with a first episode of wheezing plus increased work of breathing, persistent cough, and feeding difficulty (± fever). This is a clinical diagnosis — CXR and albuterol are not indicated.[1]
Not recommendedWhy
Albuterol / salbutamolNo effect on admission or length of stay[5]
Nebulized hypertonic saline (ED)No benefit in the emergency setting[6]
Systemic steroidsNo benefit in first-episode bronchiolitis[1]
AntibioticsViral illness — reserve for proven bacterial coinfection[7]
CXR / viral testingDoes not change management; adds cost and radiation[1]
Chest physiotherapyNo benefit; may distress the infant[8]
Exclusion criteria — leave this pathway if ANY are present8 items
  • Chronic lung disease or cystic fibrosis
  • Congenital heart disease
  • Immunodeficiency
  • Toxic appearance or shock
  • Neuromuscular disease
  • Artificial or abnormal airway
  • Recurrent wheezing (>3 episodes)
  • Respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation
Comorbid infants need individualized evaluation and management — subspecialty consult, alternative diagnoses (reactive airway disease, pertussis, foreign body), and management guided by the underlying condition.[2]

Mild (BAS 0–3) — supportive care
Gentle superficial nasal suction with an aspirator (NOT deep beyond the nasopharynx), reposition for comfort, encourage PO fluids. No medications. Observe 1–2 hours, then reassess.[2]
Moderate (BAS 4–8) — notify provider
Notify provider. Nasal suction, rehydrate (IV if not tolerating PO), supplemental O₂ via nasal cannula or mask. Target SpO₂ ≥90% awake or ≥88% asleep.[1]
Severe (BAS 9–12) — escalate now
Notify provider immediately. Nasal suction, place PIV, IV fluid resuscitation if dehydrated, continuous monitoring — and proceed directly to HFNC initiation (Section 3).[3]

3 · HFNC — dose, monitor, escalate

Enter weight above. Start FiO₂ 21% and titrate to SpO₂ ≥90%. Watch at least 30 minutes in the ED after starting; continuous pulse oximetry required; NPO ~1 hour to assess response.[3]

—Starting flow — 4 LPM if <7 kg, 6 LPM if ≥7 kg
—Maximum flow — 2 LPM/kg for ≤7 kg, else 14 LPM
On HFNC: BAS every 15 minutes
If BAS ≥9: suction first, then reassess. If still ≥9 after suction, contact the primary team. Must be stable ≥4 hours on maintenance settings before any weaning attempt.[3]
Escalate flow / call critical care
If not at max flow, increase by 2 LPM (cap: 2 LPM/kg for ≤7 kg, 14 LPM for >7 kg). Critical-care consult for: worsening after 60 minutes, FiO₂ >50%, flow above max, apnea, or severe dehydration/shock.[3]

4 · Disposition

ED discharge — ALL must be met
SpO₂ ≥90% on room air · RR <60/min with minimal/no increased WOB · tolerating PO to maintain hydration · parents comfortable with home care and suction technique · education complete.[2]
Return precautions (teach the parents)
Feeding decreases >50% · worsening breathing · signs of dehydration (fewer wet diapers, no tears, dry mouth) · fever ≥100.4°F in infants <3 months.[9]
  • Acute-care admit: routine management, FiO₂ <50% to keep SaO₂ ≥90%, HFNC at standard flow
  • Orders: BAS Q4h, nasal suction PRN, continuous/intermittent pulse oximetry, assess hydration & feeding
  • Inpatient discharge: SpO₂ ≥90% on room air for ≥2 continuous hours, RR <60, minimal WOB, tolerating PO, parents demonstrate suction
PICU / critical care
Worsening after 60 minutes of HFNC · need for PPV · witnessed apnea · flow above max · FiO₂ >50% · severe dehydration or shock. Prepare for potential intubation and ensure IV access. NICU if <44 weeks corrected gestational age.[3]
