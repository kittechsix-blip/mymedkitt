Sodium Disorders: Hyponatremia & Hypernatremia

Adults ≥18 in the ED or inpatient setting with hyponatremia (Na+ <135 mEq/L) or hypernatremia (Na+ >145 mEq/L). Excludes pediatrics and dialysis-dependent ESRD — renal replacement changes correction dynamics and needs a modified approach.

⚠ Symptom severity — not the sodium number — determines urgency. Seizures, coma, or respiratory arrest require immediate 3% saline; do not wait for labs.
⚠ Never exceed 10 mEq/L correction in 24 hours (8 mEq/L if high-risk) — overcorrection causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), which is often irreversible.
⚠ Give thiamine 100 mg IV empirically before or with sodium correction — thiamine deficiency dramatically increases ODS risk.
⚠ Hypovolemic, water-excess, and diuretic-induced hyponatremia all carry HIGH overcorrection risk once the underlying driver is removed — check Na+ q2h and have D5W + DDAVP rescue at the bedside.
⚠ Do NOT use vaptans (tolvaptan, conivaptan) for SIAD — unpredictable overcorrection, hepatotoxicity, no mortality benefit.
⚠ Correct hypernatremia slowly — max 10-12 mEq/L per 24 hours if chronic; rapid correction risks cerebral edema.

2 · Hyponatremia — severe/moderate symptoms: act now

Give 3% saline now — do not wait for labs
Hypertonic Saline 100-150 mL IV bolus over 10-20 minutes. Repeat up to 2 more times (3 boluses total) if severe symptoms persist. Peripheral IV access is fine for boluses — do not delay for a central line. Target: raise Na+ 4-6 mEq/L in the first 1-2 hours — enough to reverse cerebral edema and stop seizures.[12]

Recheck Na+ at 1 hour. Check Na+ every 2 hours during active correction. Concurrent K+ repletion counts toward Na+ correction — 40 mEq KCl raises Na+ roughly 1 mEq/L.

3 · Mild/asymptomatic hyponatremia — workup

Order the diagnostic triad simultaneously: serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium. Check potassium and correct concurrently.[2]

Serum osmolality interpretation
Hypotonic (<275 mOsm/kg) = true hyponatremia → continue to etiology. Non-hypotonic (≥275) = pseudohyponatremia or translocational hyponatremia (hyperglycemia, mannitol, IV contrast).[3]
Not true hyponatremia
Hyperglycemia: corrected Na+ = measured Na+ + 1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 (use 2.4 if glucose >400). Pseudohyponatremia (triglycerides >1500, paraproteinemia): confirm with point-of-care sodium (direct ISE). Treat the underlying cause — no hypertonic saline needed.[3]
—Sodium correction to add (glucose above 100)

Urine osm <100 = ADH suppressed (water excess). Urine osm >100 with urine Na <30 = kidney retaining sodium (hypovolemic/hypervolemic). Urine Na >30 = kidney wasting (SIAD, diuretics, adrenal insufficiency).[2][5]

5 · Hypernatremia — assessment & treatment

Hypernatremia is almost always a free water deficit — inadequate intake, excessive loss, or (rarely) sodium gain. Assess volume status and urine output.[9]

Free water deficit
FWD (L) = TBW × (Na/140 − 1), where TBW = weight × 0.6 (male) or × 0.5 (female/elderly). Underestimates true deficit — add ~1-1.5 L/day for ongoing losses. Replace over 48-72h via enteral free water (preferred), D5W IV, or 0.45% NaCl.[9]
—Free water deficit
Correction rate
Acute (<48h): can correct 1 mEq/L/hr. Chronic (>48h or unknown, most hospitalized patients): maximum 10-12 mEq/L per 24 hours — rapid correction risks cerebral edema.[9][11]

Polyuria with urine osm <300 suggests DI. DDAVP trial: urine osm rises >50% at 2h = central DI (kidney responds to exogenous ADH); minimal rise = nephrogenic DI (kidney resistant).[9][10]

Central DI: Desmopressin (DDAVP) 2 mcg IV q8-12h (acute inpatient) OR 10 mcg intranasal BID (stable/outpatient).[9]

Post-neurosurgical triphasic response
DI (days 1-5) → SIAD (days 5-10, uncontrolled ADH release from dying neurons) → permanent DI (if >80% of neurons destroyed). Do NOT continue DDAVP blindly into the SIAD phase — causes dangerous hyponatremia. Check urine output and Na+ q4-6h in the first 2 weeks.[9]

Nephrogenic DI: DDAVP is ineffective. Remove the offending agent — lithium (most common; discuss with psychiatry first), hypercalcemia, hypokalemia. Correct K+ to >3.5 mEq/L.[10]

Hydrochlorothiazide + Amiloride (thiazide paradox)
HCTZ 25 mg + Amiloride 5 mg
PO, HCTZ daily / amiloride BID
Mild volume depletion → increased proximal reabsorption → less water to the diluting segment → less urine output. Amiloride also blocks lithium entry into collecting duct cells. Combine with a low-sodium diet.

Indomethacin 50 mg PO TID (short-term only) can reduce urine output 25-50%, but GI/renal toxicity limits long-term use. Patients may need 10-20 L/day of free water intake to keep up with losses.[10]

Iatrogenic hypernatremia from excessive sodium administration (NS boluses, NaHCO3, high-sodium medications) or hypertonic saline overcorrection.[11]

Furosemide
20-40 mg
IV, daily or BID
Produces hypotonic urine (Na+ ~75 mEq/L), removing more sodium than water. Replace urine volume with D5W or enteral free water. Switch maintenance IV fluids from NS to D5W.

Additional pitfalls & nuances4 items
Concurrent K+ repletion counts toward Na+ correction — 40 mEq KCl raises Na+ roughly 1 mEq/L as K+ enters cells and Na+ exits.[6]
Elderly hypernatremia is almost always chronic and related to impaired thirst — correct slowly, ensure caregiver-driven water access on a schedule, and review diuretics on discharge.[11]
Hospital-acquired hypernatremia carries 40-60% ICU mortality — but this reflects underlying illness severity, not sodium toxicity per se. Prevention (scheduled free water) beats treatment.[11]
Overcorrection is comparatively rare in hypernatremia (unlike hyponatremia) — reported mainly in aggressive pediatric correction.[9]
