Push-Dose Pressors: Bridge the Gap, Not the Destination

Adults in the ED or periprocedural setting who need immediate, temporary blood pressure support via IV bolus vasopressor while a continuous infusion is prepared. Excludes pediatric push-dose use (not established) and cardiac arrest — these are NOT arrest doses.

⚠ NEVER give the full cardiac arrest dose (1 mg IV epinephrine) to a patient with a pulse — a 61-fold overdose vs IM dosing that can cause malignant hypertension, VT/VF, intracranial hemorrhage, and death.
⚠ Do NOT use pre-filled saline flush syringes to reconstitute push-dose epinephrine — FDA prohibits this due to device-classification dosing-error risk.
⚠ Avoid phenylephrine in bradycardia, suspected low cardiac output, or impaired contractility — pure alpha-1 agonism may worsen these via reflex bradycardia and reduced stroke volume.
⚠ LABEL every syringe and bag clearly before handoff — medication errors occurred in 19% of patients receiving push-dose pressors in one study, including a reported 1000-fold phenylephrine overdose (50 mg given instead of 50 mcg).
⚠ Push-dose pressors are a temporary bridge (5-15 minutes), not definitive therapy — prepare a continuous infusion simultaneously and transition promptly.

1 · Indications — when to reach for push-dose

What this is (and isn't)
Push-dose pressors are IV bolus vasopressor doses for immediate blood pressure support, bridging to a continuous infusion. They provide hemodynamic support when a continuous infusion cannot be established within 5-8 minutes. Epinephrine has won: it is now preferred over phenylephrine for most situations because it combines inotropic and vasopressor effects.[1][2][6]

Set the clinical scenario above — the callout below and the flow map focus on that pathway. Then set the agent in section 3 to see live dosing.

Peri-Intubation Hypotension
Hypotension affects ~40% of critically ill patients undergoing emergency intubation and is independently associated with increased mortality. Epinephrine preferred — provides both alpha and beta effects; phenylephrine may decrease cardiac output via pure vasoconstriction. Optimize preload with IV fluids before induction and have the pressor drawn up at bedside.[4][7]
Septic Shock — Bridge to Infusion
Pharmacy-mixed vasopressor drips take 10-15 minutes minimum. Push-dose bridges that gap while continuing aggressive volume resuscitation and targeting MAP ≥65 mmHg. Diastolic BP <40 mmHg strongly suggests profound vasoplegia — a vasopressor is needed immediately. Epinephrine preferred if cardiac output may be impaired; phenylephrine for pure vasodilatory shock with adequate cardiac output and tachycardia.[1][2][3][9]
Anaphylaxis — Refractory to IM Epinephrine
Refractory anaphylaxis (persistent symptoms despite 2-3 IM epi doses) occurs in <1% of cases. Peri-arrest: push-dose epinephrine 20-50 mcg IV push, repeat every 1-2 minutes. Sustained support: IV infusion 5-15 mcg/min — Brown et al 2004: 19 patients with bee sting anaphylaxis treated at 5-15 mcg/min, all responded within 5 minutes. Do NOT give the cardiac arrest dose (1 mg IV) to a patient with a pulse — a 61-fold dosing error vs IM.[5][10]
Bradycardia with Hypotension
Push-dose epinephrine provides immediate chronotropic and inotropic support via beta-1 agonism. Phenylephrine is NOT appropriate here — its pure alpha agonism may worsen bradycardia through a reflex parasympathetic response.[1][2][3]
Procedural Hypotension
Post-propofol, post-cardioversion, or sedation-related hypotension is often anticipated and transient — a single push-dose can avoid the need for continuous infusion. Pre-hydrate with 250-500 mL crystalloid and have the pressor drawn up before the procedure.[1][2][3]

4 · Safety — the 1 mg error

The 1 mg cardiac arrest dose error
NEVER give the full cardiac arrest dose (1 mg = 1000 mcg) of IV epinephrine to a patient with a pulse. This is a 61-fold overdose vs IM dosing and can cause malignant hypertension, tachyarrhythmias (VT/VF), myocardial ischemia, intracranial hemorrhage, and death. ALWAYS dilute the cardiac amp, ALWAYS label the syringe clearly, and double-check concentration before every dose.
Medication-error rate
Medication errors occurred in 19% of patients receiving push-dose pressors in one study — including a reported case of 50 mg phenylephrine given instead of 50 mcg (a 1000-fold overdose). Use pre-mixed pharmacy syringes when available; never use pre-filled saline flush syringes to reconstitute medications.

5 · Troubleshooting & transition

If push-dose pressors are not achieving adequate MAP, reassess systematically. Set the troubleshooting scenario above.

Suspected hypovolemia — volume first
Pressors work poorly in an empty tank. POCUS: IVC collapse on inspiration, hyperdynamic/empty LV. Give 500-1000 mL crystalloid bolus and reassess; blood products if hemorrhagic. Initial sepsis resuscitation targets 30 mL/kg (SSC 2021), then be restrictive.
Phenylephrine failing — switch agents
Phenylephrine failure suggests low cardiac output, bradycardia, or septic cardiomyopathy. Switch to epinephrine — response rate 71.8% vs 55.9% for phenylephrine.
Refractory shock — escalation ladder
1. Maximize push-dose pressor (more frequent dosing)
2. Start continuous norepinephrine infusion (can be peripheral)
3. Add vasopressin 0.03-0.04 units/min (non-titratable)
4. Stress-dose hydrocortisone 50 mg IV q6h
5. Consider methylene blue 1-2 mg/kg IV for NO-mediated vasodilation
6. VA-ECMO for refractory cardiogenic/mixed shock
Ready to transition — continuous infusion
Push-dose is a 5-15 minute bridge — prepare the continuous infusion simultaneously. Norepinephrine infusion: start 0.05 mcg/kg/min (4-6 mcg/min), titrate by 0.02-0.05 mcg/kg/min to MAP ≥65 mmHg; can start peripherally for up to 6 hours (SSC 2021).
