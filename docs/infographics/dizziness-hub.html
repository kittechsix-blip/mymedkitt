Dizziness Hub: Sick Check First, Then Sort by Timing and Triggers

ED presentations of dizziness, vertigo, lightheadedness, presyncope, imbalance, wooziness, feeling "off," or unclear acute gait complaints — dizziness is a chief complaint, not a diagnosis, and this hub sorts it into the correct dangerous or benign lane.

⚠ Dizziness is a chief complaint, not a diagnosis. Glucose, vitals + O2 sat, ECG, gait, ocular-motor exam, and a focused neuro/stroke screen come before labeling anything benign. Posterior circulation stroke, dysrhythmia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, bleeding, and tox can all present with vague wording.
⚠ Focal neuro deficit, severe gait/truncal ataxia, diplopia/dysarthria/dysphagia, new severe headache, neck pain, or a Horner sign = neurovascular emergency lane — open Stroke, ICH, or Cervical Artery Dissection and treat that physiology first.
⚠ Syncope, near-syncope, chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, hypotension, brady/tachycardia, or melena/pallor = cardiac/perfusion/bleeding lane — open Syncope, Chest Pain Hub, A-Fib RVR, or GI Bleed Hub before treating this as vestibular.
⚠ Altered mental status, seizure, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, fever/shock, CO exposure, overdose, or a dangerous ECG/lab = metabolic/sepsis/tox lane — correct reversible physiology now; these can recur after an initially reassuring exam.
⚠ HINTS/HINTS+ is valid ONLY in acute vestibular syndrome — continuous symptoms lasting hours to days with spontaneous nystagmus, performed by a trained examiner, with no immediate central red flag already present. A normal head CT does NOT clear a dangerous posterior-circulation presentation.
⚠ Inability to walk unaided or severe truncal ataxia is an objective red flag, never routine discharge. Isolated gait failure can be posterior circulation stroke — reassess gait after treatment and document it.
⚠ Do NOT discharge inability to walk, central ocular signs, new focal neuro findings, high-risk syncope features, or recurrent metabolic/toxic symptoms. Admit, observe, or activate the specialty pathway instead.

1 · Sick check first, then differential

Set the lane toggles above as you triage — the single active pathway highlights below and in the algorithm.

First 60 seconds: fingerstick glucose now; vitals including O2 sat and temperature, orthostatics only when safe; ECG early if lightheaded, presyncopal, weak, elderly, cardiac history, medication change, or unclear syndrome; screen for stroke/ICH (face/arm/leg asymmetry, dysarthria, aphasia, diplopia, dysphagia, dysmetria, gaze deviation, visual field cut, severe headache, neck pain, new inability to walk); observe gait and truncal stability before sedating medications when safe; eye exam for spontaneous nystagmus only when the patient meets AVS criteria; medication/toxin scan (sedatives, alcohol, antihypertensives, anticonvulsants, aminoglycosides, insulin/sulfonylureas, CO exposure).

Neurovascular dizziness
Open Acute Ischemic Stroke, ICH, or Cervical Artery Dissection. Immediate actions: glucose confirmed/corrected, last-known-well, NIHSS-style focused exam, CT head, CTA head/neck when stroke/dissection/LVO concern exists, MRI when posterior stroke remains plausible after CT/CTA and the patient is not otherwise explained. High-risk findings: inability to walk unaided, severe truncal ataxia, direction-changing or vertical nystagmus, skew deviation, new diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, limb ataxia, sensory/motor asymmetry, Horner syndrome, severe occipital headache, neck pain after trauma/manipulation. Pitfall: CT head is insensitive for early posterior fossa ischemia — a normal CT does not clear a dangerous posterior circulation presentation. Treat as neurovascular until central signs, gait failure, and dissection/stroke features are resolved or explained.
Presyncope / cardiac / perfusion dizziness
Open Syncope, Chest Pain Hub, A-Fib RVR, or GI Bleed Hub as clinically indicated. Immediate actions: ECG, monitor, IV access if unstable, glucose, CBC/CMP/troponin when indicated, pregnancy test when relevant, orthostatics only when safe, bedside echo/IVC/lung/aorta POCUS if shock or unclear perfusion. High-risk features: exertional syncope/presyncope, chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, abnormal ECG, known structural heart disease, persistent hypotension, severe anemia/bleeding, family history of sudden death, older/frail patient with injury or recurrent episodes. Lightheaded dizziness with perfusion features follows the syncope/cardiac/bleeding pathway, not the vestibular pathway.
Metabolic / sepsis / toxicologic dizziness
Open Hypoglycemia, Sepsis, CO Toxicity, Unknown Ingestion Hub, Potassium Disorders, Adrenal Insufficiency, or Thyroid Disorders. Immediate actions: correct glucose, oxygen if hypoxic or CO possible, ECG, medication reconciliation, temperature, VBG/chemistry when ill, lactate/cultures/antibiotics if sepsis physiology, co-oximetry if exposure cluster or headache/nausea/flu-like symptoms with normal pulse ox. Pitfall: dizziness from hypoglycemia, CO, sedatives, or antihypertensive over-treatment can recur after an initially reassuring exam. Treat reversible physiology now, then determine recurrence risk before disposition.

Acute Vestibular Syndrome (AVS)
Open Vertigo for the full HINTS+/BPPV/Mimics workflow. AVS definition: continuous dizziness/vertigo lasting hours to days, usually with nausea/vomiting, head-motion intolerance, gait unsteadiness, and spontaneous nystagmus. HINTS/HINTS+ is appropriate only when: continuous AVS is present, spontaneous nystagmus is present, the examiner is trained and comfortable interpreting head impulse/nystagmus direction/skew/hearing, and there is no immediate central red flag already requiring the stroke pathway. Central signs: normal head impulse in AVS, direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus, vertical/torsional nystagmus, skew deviation, new unilateral hearing loss with vascular concern, severe gait/truncal ataxia, or any focal neurologic deficit. If central signs or exam uncertainty: open Acute Ischemic Stroke and obtain stroke imaging/neurology input.
Triggered Positional Dizziness
Open Vertigo for Dix-Hallpike, supine roll, BPPV nuance, canalith repositioning, and discharge plan. Classic BPPV pattern: brief episodes, seconds to <1 minute, triggered by head position change, no spontaneous nystagmus at rest, no focal neurologic findings, able to ambulate at baseline between spells. Do NOT do Dix-Hallpike yet if: spontaneous nystagmus at rest, a new focal neurologic sign, severe headache/neck pain, severe truncal ataxia, inability to walk, or symptoms are continuous rather than brief triggered episodes. Imaging: avoid routine CT/MRI when diagnostic criteria for typical BPPV are met and no inconsistent features exist. Classic BPPV is a bedside diagnosis and treatment pathway, not a reflex imaging pathway.
Spontaneous Episodic Dizziness
Common lanes: Migraine or Vertigo for vestibular migraine phenotype; Acute Ischemic Stroke for TIA/posterior circulation concern; Syncope for dysrhythmia/perfusion symptoms; Chest Pain Hub when ACS/PE physiology coexists. Key discriminators: duration (minutes-hours), frequency, complete recovery between episodes, headache/photophobia/phonophobia, auditory symptoms, vascular risk, diplopia/dysarthria/ataxia, palpitations, exertional trigger, orthostasis, medication timing. Pitfall: transient posterior circulation ischemia may have a normal exam after symptoms resolve — do not let resolution erase high-risk history.
Lightheaded / Presyncopal Dizziness
Open Syncope for the full risk pathway. Core ED evaluation: history of position/exertion/palpitations/chest pain/dyspnea/bleeding, ECG, glucose, volume status, medication list, CBC/CMP/troponin when clinically indicated, pregnancy test when relevant. Orthostatic dizziness: confirm safely only after dangerous cardiac/bleeding features are screened; treat volume depletion or medication effect, then reassess standing and walking. Do NOT call it vertigo when the physiology is near-faint, exertional, palpitations, hypotension, anemia, or medication-induced perfusion failure.
Disequilibrium / Unsafe Gait
Open Acute Ischemic Stroke, Vertigo, Weakness Hub, or Cervical Artery Dissection based on the exam. High-risk gait findings: cannot sit upright, cannot stand, cannot walk unaided, severe veering/falling, new limb ataxia, new diplopia/dysarthria/dysphagia, new focal weakness/sensory loss. Lower-risk patterns: chronic stable neuropathy, baseline gait disorder, medication sedation that fully clears, visual impairment, deconditioning with no acute neurologic or perfusion findings. Pitfall: isolated gait failure can be posterior circulation stroke — if the patient cannot safely ambulate at baseline, disposition should not be routine discharge.
Post-Exposure / Medication Dizziness
Open CO Toxicity, Unknown Ingestion Hub, or targeted tox/electrolyte consults. Ask specifically: new antihypertensive/diuretic, sedative, opioid, alcohol/cannabis, anticonvulsant, lithium, aminoglycoside, chemotherapy, salicylate, carbon monoxide source, multiple symptomatic people, recent dose change, renal failure, dehydration, missed meals. ED workup: ECG, glucose, orthostatics when safe, BMP/Mg, co-oximetry if CO possible, salicylate/acetaminophen levels when ingestion possible, medication reconciliation with dosing time. Disposition depends on recurrence risk: long-acting sedatives, insulin/sulfonylureas, CO exposure, abnormal ECG, persistent gait impairment, or unsafe medication access usually need observation/admission.

5 · Reassess after treatment, then disposition

Repeat the exam after glucose correction, fluids, antiemetic, Epley, medication hold, or observation. The repeat gait and ocular motor exam often decides disposition.

Admit / ICU / Transfer
New/worse neuro finding, central ocular sign, cannot walk, recurrent hypoglycemia/tox symptoms, or abnormal ECG/vitals/labs.
  • Stroke/ICH/dissection concern or central ocular motor signs.
  • Inability to walk at baseline, severe truncal ataxia, or unsafe gait after treatment.
  • Syncope high-risk features, abnormal ECG, persistent hypotension, ACS/PE concern.
  • Recurrent hypoglycemia, insulin/sulfonylurea risk, CO/tox exposure, dangerous electrolyte abnormality.
  • Sepsis/shock, hypoxia, significant bleeding/anemia, persistent vomiting/dehydration.
  • Unclear diagnosis with persistent objective abnormality or unsafe social situation.
Observation / Serial Exam
Improving but not baseline, unclear diagnosis, needs serial exam or repeat test. Observation is appropriate when symptoms improve but diagnosis remains uncertain, gait is not clearly baseline, AVS has uncertain central/peripheral findings, presyncope needs rhythm/orthostatic/lab trend, or metabolic/toxic dizziness could recur. Observation endpoints: stable vitals, reassuring ECG/rhythm, no evolving neurologic signs, safe ambulation, tolerating PO, glucose stable when relevant, treatment response maintained, follow-up plan clear. Use observation to prove trajectory, not to delay a needed stroke/cardiac/tox pathway.
Discharge Checklist
Back to baseline, objective low-risk peripheral/reversible diagnosis, safe ambulation/support. Discharge only when: vitals stable and glucose/ECG reassuring for the phenotype; no focal neuro deficit, central ocular sign, severe headache/neck pain, or high-risk syncope feature; walking safely at baseline; a low-risk diagnosis is documented (classic BPPV treated/referred, vestibular migraine with safe neuro exam, orthostasis/medication effect corrected, or benign/reversible cause with recurrence risk addressed); patient has safe support, follow-up, and return precautions. Return precautions: new weakness/numbness, trouble speaking/swallowing, double vision, severe headache/neck pain, inability to walk, syncope, chest pain, dyspnea, persistent vomiting, recurrent low glucose symptoms, fever/confusion, or worsening dizziness.

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls10 items
Do NOT label dizziness benign without glucose, vitals+O2 sat, ECG, gait, and a focused neuro/ocular-motor screen first — dizziness is a chief complaint, not a diagnosis.
Do NOT sort dizziness by the patient's word ("vertigo" vs. "dizzy") alone — use timing, triggers, and targeted exam.
Do NOT use HINTS/HINTS+ outside acute vestibular syndrome (continuous symptoms + spontaneous nystagmus) or without a trained examiner.
Do NOT perform Dix-Hallpike when spontaneous nystagmus at rest, a new focal neurologic sign, severe headache/neck pain, severe truncal ataxia, inability to walk, or continuous (not brief triggered) symptoms are present.
Do NOT let a normal head CT reassure you in a posterior-circulation-concerning presentation — CT is insensitive for early posterior fossa ischemia.
Do NOT let symptom resolution erase a high-risk history — transient posterior circulation ischemia can have a normal exam after symptoms resolve.
Do NOT forget dizziness from hypoglycemia, CO, sedatives, or antihypertensive over-treatment can recur after an initially reassuring exam.
Do NOT treat isolated unsafe gait as benign — it can be posterior circulation stroke; if the patient cannot safely ambulate at baseline, do not route to routine discharge.
Do NOT use CT head as a generic "dizziness screen" — image the specific dangerous diagnosis you are pursuing, not the chief complaint.
Do NOT discharge inability to walk, central ocular signs, new focal neuro findings, high-risk syncope features, or recurrent metabolic/toxic symptoms.

Use observation to prove trajectory, not to delay a needed stroke/cardiac/tox pathway.

Discharge only when: vitals stable and glucose/ECG reassuring for the phenotype; no focal neuro deficit, central ocular sign, severe headache/neck pain, or high-risk syncope feature; walking safely at baseline; a low-risk diagnosis is documented (classic BPPV treated/referred, vestibular migraine with safe neuro exam, orthostasis/medication effect corrected, or benign/reversible cause with recurrence risk addressed); patient has safe support, follow-up, and return precautions. Return precautions: new weakness/numbness, trouble speaking/swallowing, double vision, severe headache/neck pain, inability to walk, syncope, chest pain, dyspnea, persistent vomiting, recurrent low glucose symptoms, fever/confusion, or worsening dizziness.

6 · One-glance algorithm

Sick check first: glucose, vitals+O2, ECG, gait, ocular-motor, neuro exam — Screen for stroke/ICH, dysrhythmia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, bleeding, and tox before labeling anything benign.

Early differential: pick the dangerous bucket — Vascular/neuro, vestibular, cardiac/perfusion, metabolic/endocrine, toxicologic/medication, other.

Time-critical exclusion present? — Neurovascular, cardiac/perfusion/bleeding, or metabolic/sepsis/tox exclusions come before vestibular labeling.

Neurovascular dizziness → Stroke/ICH/Dissection pathway — CT insensitive for early posterior fossa ischemia — treat as neurovascular until resolved or explained.

Presyncope/cardiac/perfusion dizziness → Syncope pathway — Lightheaded dizziness with perfusion features follows the syncope/cardiac/bleeding pathway.

Metabolic/sepsis/tox dizziness → correct physiology now — Correct reversible physiology now, then determine recurrence risk before disposition.

No exclusion — timing + triggers: sort the syndrome — AVS, triggered positional, spontaneous episodic, presyncope, disequilibrium, or post-exposure.

Acute Vestibular Syndrome (AVS) → Vertigo consult, HINTS if criteria met — Continuous hours-to-days, spontaneous nystagmus. HINTS only if trained examiner + no central red flag.

Triggered positional (BPPV pattern) → Vertigo consult — Seconds to <1 minute, position-triggered, no spontaneous nystagmus at rest.

Spontaneous episodic → migraine/TIA/Meniere/dysrhythmia work-up — Minutes-hours with between-episode recovery — do not let resolution erase high-risk history.

Lightheaded/presyncopal → Syncope pathway — Cardiovascular/perfusion presentation until proven otherwise.

Disequilibrium/unsafe gait → objective red flag — Isolated gait failure can be posterior circulation stroke — never routine discharge.

Post-exposure/medication dizziness → tox/metabolic work-up — Disposition depends on recurrence risk (sedatives, insulin/sulfonylureas, CO exposure, abnormal ECG).

Imaging & testing strategy — image the diagnosis, not the word "dizzy" — CT head, CTA head/neck, MRI DWI, or no routine imaging, based on syndrome and objective risk.

Reassess after treatment/first pass — Repeat gait and ocular-motor exam often decides disposition.

Admit / observe / activate specialty pathway — New/worse neuro finding, cannot walk, recurrent hypoglycemia/tox, abnormal ECG/vitals/labs.

Observation / serial exam — Improving but not baseline, unclear diagnosis, needs serial exam or repeat test.

Discharge checklist — Back to baseline, low-risk diagnosis documented, safe follow-up and return precautions.

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT label dizziness benign without glucose, vitals+O2 sat, ECG, gait, and a focused neuro/ocular-motor screen first — dizziness is a chief complaint, not a diagnosis.

Do NOT sort dizziness by the patient's word ("vertigo" vs. "dizzy") alone — use timing, triggers, and targeted exam.

Do NOT use HINTS/HINTS+ outside acute vestibular syndrome (continuous symptoms + spontaneous nystagmus) or without a trained examiner.

Do NOT perform Dix-Hallpike when spontaneous nystagmus at rest, a new focal neurologic sign, severe headache/neck pain, severe truncal ataxia, inability to walk, or continuous (not brief triggered) symptoms are present.

Do NOT let a normal head CT reassure you in a posterior-circulation-concerning presentation — CT is insensitive for early posterior fossa ischemia.

Do NOT let symptom resolution erase a high-risk history — transient posterior circulation ischemia can have a normal exam after symptoms resolve.

Do NOT forget dizziness from hypoglycemia, CO, sedatives, or antihypertensive over-treatment can recur after an initially reassuring exam.

Do NOT treat isolated unsafe gait as benign — it can be posterior circulation stroke; if the patient cannot safely ambulate at baseline, do not route to routine discharge.

Do NOT use CT head as a generic "dizziness screen" — image the specific dangerous diagnosis you are pursuing, not the chief complaint.

Do NOT discharge inability to walk, central ocular signs, new focal neuro findings, high-risk syncope features, or recurrent metabolic/toxic symptoms.
