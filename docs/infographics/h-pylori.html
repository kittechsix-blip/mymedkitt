H. pylori — ED Decision Support

Adults in the ED with suspected or known Helicobacter pylori infection: dyspepsia, peptic ulcer disease, or GI cancer-risk indications for testing. Excludes pediatrics. Alarm features (GI bleeding, weight loss, dysphagia, iron-deficiency anemia, new dyspepsia at age >=60) route to the GI/EGD pathway, not routine ED test-and-treat.

⚠ Do not use clarithromycin or levofloxacin regimens empirically unless susceptibility is known — resistance has made empiric macrolide/quinolone therapy unreliable.
⚠ Every treated H. pylori patient needs proof of eradication (test-of-cure) — symptom improvement alone does not confirm cure.
⚠ Hold PPI/PCAB for 2 weeks and antibiotics/bismuth for 4 weeks before any active-infection test or test-of-cure, or the result may be falsely negative.
⚠ Alarm features — GI bleeding, weight loss, progressive dysphagia, persistent vomiting, a mass, or new dyspepsia at age >=60 — require the GI/EGD pathway. Do not let an H. pylori plan delay resuscitation, imaging, EGD, or surgical evaluation.
⚠ A positive serology does not prove active infection or cure — never use serology as the test-of-cure.

1 · Overview

H. pylori is a treatable cause of peptic ulcer disease and a recognized gastric cancer risk factor. The ED's job: identify who needs urgent GI workup, who needs outpatient active-infection testing, and what current eradication regimen should be documented or started when treatment is appropriate. This is not a generic abdominal-pain tool — use the toggles below to pick the lane.

2 · What to do now

Alarm / complicated presentation
This is not a simple outpatient H. pylori pathway. Stabilize and risk-stratify the acute presentation. Use the Upper GI Bleed pathway if bleeding is present. Arrange urgent GI evaluation / EGD when alarm features are present. H. pylori testing may be done by biopsy at EGD, or noninvasively later if no endoscopy occurs. Do not let an H. pylori plan delay resuscitation, imaging, EGD, or surgical evaluation.
No EGD needed — outpatient active-infection testing
No alarm features today. Arrange stool antigen or urea breath testing after holding PPI/PCAB x2 weeks and antibiotics/bismuth x4 weeks; treat if positive and confirm eradication.
EGD already indicated
Ask GI to biopsy-test for H. pylori during endoscopy and plan eradication + test-of-cure if positive. The ED usually does not need to choose the biopsy method — document the indication.
Start optimized bismuth quadruple therapy
Start optimized bismuth quadruple therapy x14 days (PPI BID + bismuth QID + tetracycline 500 mg QID + metronidazole 500 mg TID/QID). Do not use empiric clarithromycin or levofloxacin. Schedule test-of-cure before discharge.
Penicillin allergy — BQT is still the main option
True penicillin allergy: optimized BQT is the main empiric option (no amoxicillin exposure). Do not reflexively use clarithromycin triple just because of reported penicillin allergy. Consider penicillin allergy evaluation when feasible — amoxicillin resistance is uncommon and matters for salvage therapy.
Susceptibility known or BQT impractical — use an alternative
Use a susceptibility-guided or formulary alternative: vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual, rifabutin-amoxicillin-PPI triple, or susceptible clarithromycin/levofloxacin regimen. Route complex first-regimen decisions to GI/PCP rather than guessing from memory.
Failed clarithromycin-based regimen
Move to optimized bismuth quadruple therapy x14 days; do not repeat the failed macrolide.
Failed optimized BQT
Do not repeat the same regimen unless nonadherence was the clear cause and GI agrees. Route to GI for culture or molecular susceptibility testing; consider rifabutin triple or vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual if no true penicillin allergy.
Multiple failures or unclear history
High-risk for resistance. Arrange GI-directed susceptibility-guided salvage (culture/molecular, preferably at EGD). Avoid empiric clarithromycin or levofloxacin without susceptibility.

4 · Alarm features

  • Hematemesis, melena, hemodynamic instability, or iron-deficiency anemia
  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Progressive dysphagia or odynophagia
  • Persistent vomiting
  • Palpable abdominal mass or lymphadenopathy
  • Family history of gastric cancer or high gastric cancer risk background
  • New dyspepsia at age >=60
  • Severe focal abdominal pain, peritonitis, or obstruction physiology

5 · Testing

Reasonable indications to test: current or prior peptic ulcer disease; dyspepsia age <60 without alarm features; dyspepsia age <50 with elevated gastric cancer risk; unexplained iron-deficiency anemia after appropriate evaluation; immune thrombocytopenic purpura; gastric MALT lymphoma or post-endoscopic resection of early gastric cancer; atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, autoimmune gastritis, or gastric epithelial polyps; adult household members of a person with confirmed H. pylori; chronic NSAID use or starting daily aspirin therapy. Do not order a test unless there is a plan to treat if positive.

Best practical tests: urea breath test or stool antigen test. Hold PPI or PCAB for 2 weeks before testing. Hold antibiotics and bismuth for 4 weeks before testing. Bridge symptoms with H2 blocker or antacids if needed. Avoid serology for testing active infection — it does not prove active infection or cure, so it should never be used as the test-of-cure. Do not start antibiotics before collecting a valid test unless there is a clear specialist-driven reason.

Biopsy-based testing (histology, rapid urease, culture, or molecular susceptibility) is appropriate when EGD is already indicated: UGIB with suspected ulcer source, gastric ulcer, complicated ulcer or malignancy concern, alarm-feature dyspepsia, or need for culture/molecular susceptibility after repeated treatment failure. All confirmed positives still need eradication therapy and test-of-cure.

7 · Test-of-cure

All treated patients need proof of eradication. Acceptable tests: urea breath test, stool antigen test, or biopsy-based testing if EGD is being done. Timing: at least 4 weeks after antibiotics are completed; off PPI/PCAB for 2 weeks; off bismuth and antibiotics for 4 weeks. H2 blocker or antacids may bridge symptoms. Document before discharge: "H. pylori eradication requires test-of-cure; symptom improvement alone does not confirm cure."

8 · Persistent / failed treatment

First confirm this is true persistence: was test-of-cure done at the correct time? Was the patient off PPI/PCAB, antibiotics, and bismuth long enough? Was the test stool antigen, urea breath, or biopsy-based — not serology? What exact regimen and adherence?

After optimized BQT failure, avoid repeating the same regimen unless nonadherence was the clear cause and GI agrees. Review prior macrolide, quinolone, metronidazole, tetracycline, and rifamycin exposure. Consider rifabutin triple therapy or vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual therapy if no true penicillin allergy. If levofloxacin is considered, use only with documented susceptibility. Arrange GI for culture or molecular susceptibility testing when available. Do not guess a second-line regimen from memory.

High-risk for resistance or bad history. Best next move: GI follow-up for susceptibility-guided therapy, preferably with culture or molecular testing if EGD is performed. Document the missing details: prior antibiotics and durations, whether treatment was completed, prior test-of-cure type and date, PPI/PCAB/bismuth/antibiotic holds before testing, and true drug allergies vs intolerance. Avoid empiric clarithromycin or levofloxacin regimens without susceptibility.

10 · Pearls & source notes

Why the regimen changed, and where the sourcing gaps areClarithromycin resistance, drug-store coverage gaps

The 2024 ACG guideline moved away from clarithromycin triple therapy as the empiric default because resistance rates have made empiric macrolide/quinolone therapy unreliable. Optimized bismuth quadruple therapy x14 days is now the default when susceptibility is unknown.

Sourcing note: myMedKitt's drug-store has no H. pylori indication for bismuth (subcitrate has no entry at all; subsalicylate's only indication is diarrhea, dosed very differently), tetracycline, amoxicillin, or levofloxacin, and no entry at all for rifabutin or vonoprazan. Only omeprazole carries an explicit H. pylori eradication indication. Every dose above is therefore sourced from the consult text itself, not the drug-store, except the PPI/omeprazole entry.
