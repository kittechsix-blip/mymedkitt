1 · Recognize

An orthopedic emergency
Bacterial infection of a joint space causes irreversible cartilage destruction within 8 hours through proteolytic enzyme release from neutrophils. Knee is most common (~50%); bimodal age distribution (young children and adults >55). Incidence 2-10 per 100,000 person-years.[1][2]

Risk factors: age >80, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, recent joint surgery or prosthesis, IVDU, immunocompromise (HIV, transplant, biologics), crystal arthropathy (gout, pseudogout).[1][3]

Classic: joint pain (most sensitive symptom), swelling/effusion, warmth, erythema, limited active AND passive range of motion. Fever present in 44-97% — may be absent in elderly or immunocompromised patients. Monoarticular in >80% of cases.[1][4]

Atypical presentations
Polyarticular (10-20%): think gonococcal, S. aureus bacteremia, or endocarditis. Afebrile: elderly, immunocompromised, chronic infection. Minimal swelling: deep joints (hip, shoulder, sacroiliac).[1]

Modified Kocher (Caird) adds CRP >2.0 mg/dL (>20 mg/L) as a 5th criterion — CRP is the strongest independent risk factor. All 5 factors present: 98% predictive.[5]

Adults: Kocher does NOT apply
In adults, PMN percentage, ESR, serum WBC, fever, and weight-bearing status are not significant predictors of septic arthritis. Newman criteria for adults: organisms on synovial microscopy/culture, OR grossly purulent joint fluid, OR compatible clinical features plus a positive blood culture. Rule of thumb: all acute monoarticular arthritis is septic until proven otherwise. Crystal arthropathy present does NOT exclude infection — dual diagnosis in 1-2% of cases.[6][7][8]

2 · Workup — arthrocentesis is the gold standard

Do not delay arthrocentesis
Do NOT delay for labs to return, imaging, coagulopathy (a relative, not absolute contraindication), or overlying cellulitis (approach from an unaffected area). Send CBC, CMP, ESR, CRP, and blood cultures (positive in up to 66% of bacterial septic arthritis). Serum markers are NOT reliable for diagnosis — CRP, ESR, and WBC have poor discriminatory value; mean synovial WBC is 70,581 in septic vs 26,758 in non-septic joints. Synovial fluid analysis is the diagnostic cornerstone.[1][4][9][10]

Collect: cell count with differential, Gram stain, culture (aerobic + anaerobic), crystal analysis (polarized microscopy), and synovial lactate/glucose where available.[1][10]

4 · Source control

Antibiotics alone are insufficient
Source control is essential. Options: serial arthrocentesis (repeatable, minimally invasive, effective for accessible joints), arthroscopic irrigation and debridement (breaks up loculations, 91% cure rate with systemic antibiotics), and arthrotomy (open surgical drainage). Multiple studies show no significant outcome difference between serial aspiration and surgical lavage for uncomplicated native-joint disease.[1][18]
  • Aspirate the joint daily, or twice daily if rapidly re-accumulating.
  • Document volume removed; use ultrasound guidance for optimal drainage.
  • Send the initial fluid for full analysis; subsequent taps for culture only if deteriorating.
  • Success indicators: decreasing effusion volume, improving synovial WBC, clinical improvement.
  • Escalate to surgery if effusion persists beyond 5-7 days, WBC is not improving, or loculations are found on imaging.
  • Amenable joints: knee (most common), ankle, elbow, wrist, small joints of hands/feet.

IV to oral transition: clinical improvement, decreasing CRP/ESR, organism susceptible to an oral agent with good bioavailability, tolerating oral intake. Oral options: fluoroquinolones, TMP-SMX (MRSA), doxycycline, rifampin (adjunct only, not monotherapy).[15]

CRP is the most useful trend marker
CRP rises within hours and normalizes within 1 week with effective treatment; ESR is slower (may stay elevated for weeks). Expected timeline: fever resolves 48-72h, joint pain improves 3-5 days, full ROM takes weeks to months. Poor response: wrong antibiotic, inadequate drainage, abscess, osteomyelitis, drug fever, or a secondary infection site.[1]
All patients require admission
All patients with suspected or confirmed septic arthritis require admission. Determine floor vs ICU by hemodynamic status.[1]

Medical floor: orthopedic surgery consult (all cases), ID consult recommended, continue empiric IV antibiotics and de-escalate by culture, daily joint exam, serial CRP, non-weight-bearing initially with splinting for comfort. Typical stay 3-7 days, transition to OPAT when stable.[1]

ICU admission criteria
Sepsis/septic shock, polyarticular disease with bacteremia, suspected endocarditis (S. aureus bacteremia — get TTE/TEE), necrotizing fasciitis, respiratory failure, or hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors. Aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, urgent surgical consult for source control. Overall mortality 5-15%, higher with delayed treatment, virulent organisms, or comorbidities.[1]

Serial arthrocentesis (daily) — Escalate to surgery if effusion persists beyond 5-7 days or WBC not improving.

Surgical I&D — consult ortho EARLY — Absolute: hip, shoulder, SI, PJI, osteomyelitis, failed serial aspiration. 91% cure rate with arthroscopy + antibiotics.

Antibiotic duration + CRP trend — S. aureus 3-4wk, Strep 2-3wk, GC 7-14d, Pseudomonas 4-6wk. CRP normalizes within 1 week with effective treatment.

Floor or ICU? — All suspected septic arthritis requires admission — this only decides level of care.

Medical floor — ortho + ID, serial CRP

ICU — sepsis mgmt, urgent source control

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT delay arthrocentesis for blood tests, imaging, or coagulopathy correction — cartilage destruction begins within 8 hours.

Do NOT let a synovial WBC below 50,000 reassure you — sensitivity is only 56%, and 39% of culture-positive cases fall below that cutoff.

Do NOT let a negative Gram stain rule out infection — sensitivity is only 29-50%.

Do NOT let crystals on synovial fluid rule out infection — dual diagnosis (crystal arthropathy plus septic arthritis) occurs in 1-2% of cases.

Do NOT attempt aspiration alone for hip, shoulder, or sacroiliac joint infection — these require surgical drainage.

Do NOT treat with antibiotics alone — source control (serial aspiration or surgical drainage) is essential; antibiotics alone are insufficient.
