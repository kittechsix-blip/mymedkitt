Excited Delirium: Resuscitation Syndrome, Sedation Strategy, Disposition

Adults with severe hyperactive delirium and dangerous agitation in the ED or prehospital handoff — especially with hyperthermia, acidosis, stimulant toxidrome, prolonged struggle/restraint, or physiologic collapse. 'Excited delirium' is used for searchability, not as a final diagnosis. Excludes routine psychiatric agitation without physiologic danger and pediatrics; alcohol/sedative withdrawal and heat stroke have dedicated pathways referenced inline.

⚠ Extreme agitation with hyperthermia, acidosis, or prolonged struggle is a resuscitation syndrome — stop the struggle and sedate early; this is not primarily a psychiatric hold.
⚠ Immediate violent threat or physiologic crash: chemical control now — do not delay lifesaving sedation for ECG or labs.
⚠ Stimulant or hyperthermic agitation: benzodiazepines plus rapid cooling are core therapy. Avoid antipsychotic-only sedation, and do NOT use antipyretics for stimulant or heat-stroke hyperthermia.
⚠ Ketamine 4-5 mg/kg IM (or 1-2 mg/kg IV/IO) is for immediate violent threat only — have airway-ready monitoring at bedside; post-ketamine intubation risk is higher in profoundly intoxicated or acidotic patients.
⚠ Midazolam 5 mg IM (or 2-5 mg IV), repeated q5-10 min, is core therapy for stimulant, withdrawal, and seizure-risk agitation — do not underdose the crashing stimulant patient.
⚠ Do NOT give IM olanzapine close to parenteral benzodiazepines — combined respiratory/cardiopulmonary depression risk.
⚠ After sedating for immediate threat, obtain ECG once safe and correct potassium/magnesium if the QT interval is prolonged; if QTc > 500 ms persists, avoid further QT-prolonging drugs when possible.
⚠ Failed sedation needs a second drug CLASS, not repeated tiny doses. Do not keep piling physical restraint onto an actively fighting, hyperthermic, acidotic patient.
⚠ Do not use bicarbonate routinely for lactic acidosis from struggle; treat hyperkalemia immediately if present.
⚠ Before intubating profound metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, or suspected hyperkalemia: avoid succinylcholine (use rocuronium) and match or exceed pre-intubation minute ventilation immediately after paralysis — post-intubation hypoventilation can precipitate arrest.
⚠ Screen early for rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, AKI, DIC, liver injury, dysrhythmia, and occult trauma — more than one complication may be present at once.
⚠ Physical restraint is a bridge to medication, not definitive treatment. Never leave a patient prone or hog-tied, and never stack body weight on the torso.
⚠ Psychiatric pathway is appropriate only after dangerous medical physiology and complications are resolved or excluded — do not stop the diagnostic workup by labeling the event 'excited delirium.'

1 · Recognize & secure

Severe hyperactive delirium with dangerous agitation — especially with hyperthermia, acidosis, stimulant toxidrome, prolonged struggle, restraint, or physiologic collapse. Use 'excited delirium' for searchability, not as a final diagnosis — treat the physiology first, then find the cause.

Danger path
Stop the struggle. Heat, catecholamines, lactate, hypoxia, rhabdomyolysis, and positional asphyxia are the killers. Chemical control early — physical restraint is a bridge to medication, not definitive treatment.
  • Call security, nursing, respiratory therapy, and a physician team lead.
  • Assign roles before contact: medication, airway, monitor, IV/IO/labs, cooling, restraints.
  • Remove weapons and reduce noise/stimulation if this does not delay control.
  • Avoid prone, hog-tie, or compressive restraint — move to supine or lateral as soon as safely possible.
  • Put on continuous pulse oximetry, cardiac monitor, BP cycling, and capnography as soon as safe.
  • Obtain rectal/core temperature when feasible.
Assessment-first path
If the patient can be engaged safely, use verbal de-escalation while another clinician screens for reversible danger: glucose, oxygen saturation/ETCO2, temperature, pulse/BP/respiratory rate, trauma, toxidrome, meningismus, focal neuro deficit, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, sepsis, withdrawal, pregnancy, and medication causes. Escalate immediately to the danger path if agitation worsens, temperature is high, the patient becomes exhausted, or staff cannot safely assess.

2 · What to do now — sedation strategy

Set Sedation strategy needed above to the patient in front of you — the single correct next action and its weight-based dose appear below.

Immediate threat → Ketamine
Ketamine 4-5 mg/kg IM (or 1-2 mg/kg IV/IO slow push when access and team control allow) is fastest for an immediate violent threat when stepwise sedation is unsafe. Have airway-ready monitoring at bedside — post-ketamine intubation risk is higher in profoundly intoxicated, acidotic, or repeatedly sedated patients.
Stimulant / withdrawal / seizure risk → Benzodiazepine
Midazolam 5 mg IM (or 2-5 mg IV), repeat q5-10 min to control dangerous agitation. Lorazepam 2-4 mg (drug-store canonical dose 1-2 mg), repeat q10-15 min, is an alternative with a longer effect. Do not underdose the crashing stimulant patient; plan respiratory support in older, intoxicated, or multi-sedative patients.
Undifferentiated agitation → Droperidol + midazolam
Droperidol 5-10 mg + midazolam 5 mg IM or IV once, reassess in 10-15 min (drug-store canonical single-dose range 2.5-5 mg). Olanzapine 5-10 mg is an alternative — but do NOT give IM olanzapine close to parenteral benzodiazepines (respiratory depression risk). Sedate first; obtain ECG as soon as safe rather than delaying for it.
Older / frail / high resp-risk → lower doses
Start 25-50% lower than standard adult doses and reassess. Avoid benzodiazepines for routine geriatric delirium unless withdrawal, stimulant toxidrome, seizure risk, or immediate safety need. Avoid anticholinergics, and avoid stacking IM olanzapine with parenteral benzodiazepines.
Known QT risk → QT-conscious pathway
Prefer benzodiazepines for tox/withdrawal/stimulant physiology, or ketamine for immediate uncontrolled threat. Avoid or minimize additional haloperidol/droperidol when QTc > 500 ms unless benefit clearly outweighs risk with active monitoring and electrolyte correction.
Severe acidosis / shock / airway failure
Sedation and ventilation planning must happen together. Stop muscle activity with sedation, oxygenate/ventilate, cool if hyperthermic, and treat shock. Do not use bicarbonate routinely for lactic acidosis from struggle.

5 · QT / ECG considerations

Should the ECG change medication timing? Immediate threat: do not delay lifesaving sedation for ECG. High QT risk but controllable: get ECG and correct electrolytes before butyrophenones when practical. After haloperidol or droperidol: obtain ECG once safe if risk factors, higher/repeated dosing, electrolyte abnormality, or a concerning rhythm.

Immediate threat / unsafe to obtain ECG
Sedate with the medication most likely to control the threat; place a monitor as soon as possible. Obtain ECG after chemical control; check K, Mg, Ca, pH, temperature, renal function, and co-ingestions. Correct K to high-normal and Mg if QT prolonged or torsades risk. If QTc > 500 ms after control, avoid further QT-prolonging drugs when possible.
High QT risk
Known long QT, QTc > 500 ms, torsades history, multiple QT-prolonging medications, hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia/hypocalcemia, bradycardia, structural heart disease, or severe renal/hepatic dysfunction. Prefer benzodiazepines or ketamine; avoid/minimize additional haloperidol or droperidol.
Standard QT risk
Give needed sedation and monitor clinically; place ECG/cardiac monitor as soon as feasible. Obtain ECG earlier with repeated dosing, older/frail patient, polypharmacy, electrolyte risk, renal failure, chest pain, syncope, stimulant intoxication, or dysrhythmia. Droperidol's boxed QT warning corresponds to low absolute real-world risk at typical agitation doses with appropriate monitoring.

6 · Physiology rescue

Reassess within minutes. Controlled means: monitorable without prolonged struggle, acceptable oxygenation/ventilation, temperature measured with cooling underway if elevated, acceptable or actively resuscitated BP/perfusion, and the team can obtain labs, ECG, and trauma/tox/infection evaluation.

Refractory agitation
Confirm dose, route, time since administration, and whether medication actually entered muscle/IV. Add a second drug CLASS rather than repeating tiny ineffective doses — ketamine if benzodiazepines failed and immediate threat persists; benzodiazepine if antipsychotic failed and stimulant/withdrawal physiology is possible. Move to airway-ready resuscitation and ICU-level sedation planning if repeated sedation is required. Recheck the diagnosis. Do not keep piling physical restraint onto an actively fighting, hyperthermic, acidotic patient.
  • Stop muscle activity with sedation.
  • Oxygenate and ventilate — use ETCO2 and blood gas trends.
  • Cool if hyperthermic.
  • Treat shock and hypovolemia with crystalloid and vasopressors as needed.
  • Check glucose, lactate/pH, K, Ca, Mg, CK, creatinine, troponin, ECG, tox labs when indicated.
  • Treat hyperkalemia immediately if present.
Airway / ICU sedation now
Preoxygenate aggressively; treat hyperkalemia if suspected; prepare vasopressor support for shock. Avoid succinylcholine if hyperkalemia, severe rhabdomyolysis, crush, burn, neuromuscular disease, or prolonged immobilization is suspected — use rocuronium. Immediately match or exceed pre-intubation minute ventilation after paralysis if severe metabolic acidosis is present; post-intubation hypoventilation can precipitate arrest.
  • Remove clothing and external heat sources.
  • Evaporative cooling: spray water plus fans.
  • Ice packs to neck, axillae, groin.
  • Consider ice-water immersion or aggressive external cooling for life-threatening hyperthermia when operationally feasible.
  • Do not use acetaminophen/NSAIDs as primary therapy.

Restore perfusion with balanced crystalloid or normal saline; reassess BP, lung exam, urine output, lactate/pH, CK, creatinine, K, Ca after each bolus. Place a Foley if rhabdomyolysis, AKI, shock, or critical illness. Score rhabdomyolysis risk with the McMahon Rhabdo Risk score when labs are available.

  • POC glucose, core temperature, ECG, and continuous monitoring.
  • VBG/ABG with lactate if severe, hyperthermic, hypoxic, shocked, or exhausted.
  • CMP, Mg, Phos, Ca; CK now and repeat if rising.
  • UA for blood/myoglobin and urine output; CBC and cultures if infection possible.
  • Troponin if stimulant, chest pain, dysrhythmia, shock, older patient, or concerning ECG.
  • Coags, fibrinogen, LFTs if hyperthermia, DIC, liver injury, or severe illness.
  • Acetaminophen, salicylate, ethanol, pregnancy test, and targeted tox testing when indicated.
  • CT head/trauma imaging only after stabilization unless neuro catastrophe is driving the presentation.

Restraint safety after medication
Physical restraints should be brief, paired with chemical control and monitoring, and removed as soon as safe. Do NOT leave a patient prone or hog-tied, stack body weight on the torso, continue physical struggle when medication has failed, or treat quietness as safety without checking ventilation, glucose, temperature, and perfusion.

7 · Disposition

Disposition is driven by physiology, complication burden, repeated sedation, and ability to identify and reverse the cause. Set Disposition above to see the matching criteria.

ICU
Temperature ≥40 C or persistent hyperthermia; pH <7.20 or rising lactate after sedation; intubation/respiratory failure/repeated deep sedation; shock/vasopressors; rhabdomyolysis with AKI or McMahon high risk; dysrhythmia/QTc >500 with instability; DIC, liver injury/failure, seizure, coma; or unclear cause with persistent delirium.
Medical admission / observation
Agitation is controlled, but the delirium cause is not fully corrected; labs are abnormal but not ICU-level; CK/Cr/K/lactate/temperature need trending; an older/frail patient has new delirium; or infection, withdrawal, intoxication, or occult trauma remains possible.
Psychiatric pathway after medical exclusion
Move to psychiatric evaluation only after normal/explained vitals, no hyperthermia/hypoxia/hypoglycemia/severe intoxication or withdrawal/sepsis/CNS emergency/occult trauma, and no clinically important acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, electrolyte threat, dysrhythmia, or organ injury. Document the medical reasoning — do not label the event 'excited delirium' and stop the diagnostic workup.
Rare discharge
Discharge only with a clear reversible cause corrected, return to baseline mental status, normal oxygenation/ventilation/temperature/perfusion, no significant acidosis/rhabdomyolysis/AKI/hyperkalemia/dysrhythmia/ischemia/DIC/trauma/infection/ongoing tox risk, sedating medication sufficiently worn off, and reliable observation with return precautions. When uncertain, observe or admit.
Other pitfalls5 items
Do NOT delay lifesaving sedation for ECG or labs in an immediate violent threat or physiologic crash.
Do NOT use antipyretics as primary therapy for heat stroke or stimulant hyperthermia — they do not fix the mechanism.
Do NOT give IM olanzapine close to parenteral benzodiazepines — case reports of respiratory arrest and death.
Do NOT use succinylcholine when hyperkalemia, severe rhabdomyolysis, crush/burn injury, or prolonged immobilization is suspected — use rocuronium.
Do NOT label the event 'excited delirium' and stop the diagnostic workup before psychiatric disposition — medical danger must be excluded first.

Give needed sedation and monitor clinically; place ECG/cardiac monitor as soon as feasible. Obtain ECG earlier with repeated dosing, older/frail patient, polypharmacy, electrolyte risk, renal failure, chest pain, syncope, stimulant intoxication, or dysrhythmia. Droperidol's boxed QT warning corresponds to low absolute real-world risk at typical agitation doses with appropriate monitoring.

6 · Physiology rescue

Reassess within minutes. Controlled means: monitorable without prolonged struggle, acceptable oxygenation/ventilation, temperature measured with cooling underway if elevated, acceptable or actively resuscitated BP/perfusion, and the team can obtain labs, ECG, and trauma/tox/infection evaluation.

Confirm dose, route, time since administration, and whether medication actually entered muscle/IV. Add a second drug CLASS rather than repeating tiny ineffective doses — ketamine if benzodiazepines failed and immediate threat persists; benzodiazepine if antipsychotic failed and stimulant/withdrawal physiology is possible. Move to airway-ready resuscitation and ICU-level sedation planning if repeated sedation is required. Recheck the diagnosis. Do not keep piling physical restraint onto an actively fighting, hyperthermic, acidotic patient.

  • Stop muscle activity with sedation.
  • Oxygenate and ventilate — use ETCO2 and blood gas trends.
  • Cool if hyperthermic.
  • Treat shock and hypovolemia with crystalloid and vasopressors as needed.
  • Check glucose, lactate/pH, K, Ca, Mg, CK, creatinine, troponin, ECG, tox labs when indicated.
  • Treat hyperkalemia immediately if present.

Preoxygenate aggressively; treat hyperkalemia if suspected; prepare vasopressor support for shock. Avoid succinylcholine if hyperkalemia, severe rhabdomyolysis, crush, burn, neuromuscular disease, or prolonged immobilization is suspected — use rocuronium. Immediately match or exceed pre-intubation minute ventilation after paralysis if severe metabolic acidosis is present; post-intubation hypoventilation can precipitate arrest.

  • Remove clothing and external heat sources.
  • Evaporative cooling: spray water plus fans.
  • Ice packs to neck, axillae, groin.
  • Consider ice-water immersion or aggressive external cooling for life-threatening hyperthermia when operationally feasible.
  • Do not use acetaminophen/NSAIDs as primary therapy.

Restore perfusion with balanced crystalloid or normal saline; reassess BP, lung exam, urine output, lactate/pH, CK, creatinine, K, Ca after each bolus. Place a Foley if rhabdomyolysis, AKI, shock, or critical illness. Score rhabdomyolysis risk with the McMahon Rhabdo Risk score when labs are available.

  • POC glucose, core temperature, ECG, and continuous monitoring.
  • VBG/ABG with lactate if severe, hyperthermic, hypoxic, shocked, or exhausted.
  • CMP, Mg, Phos, Ca; CK now and repeat if rising.
  • UA for blood/myoglobin and urine output; CBC and cultures if infection possible.
  • Troponin if stimulant, chest pain, dysrhythmia, shock, older patient, or concerning ECG.
  • Coags, fibrinogen, LFTs if hyperthermia, DIC, liver injury, or severe illness.
  • Acetaminophen, salicylate, ethanol, pregnancy test, and targeted tox testing when indicated.
  • CT head/trauma imaging only after stabilization unless neuro catastrophe is driving the presentation.

Complication | Key actions

Rhabdomyolysis / AKI | IV crystalloid + urine output; repeat CK/Cr/K/Ca/Phos/Mg/lactate; treat hyperkalemia urgently; avoid routine bicarbonate for CK alone; dialysis for standard indications. Full pathway: [Rhabdomyolysis](#/tree/rhabdomyolysis).

Hyperkalemia / electrolyte threat | Calcium for membrane stabilization; insulin/dextrose + albuterol + bicarbonate if severe acidemia; dialysis if refractory. Full pathway: [Potassium Disorders](#/tree/potassium).

Dysrhythmia / QT / ischemia | ECG now; correct hypoxia, hyperthermia, acidosis, K/Mg/Ca, shock; magnesium for torsades; avoid additional QT-prolonging sedatives if QTc >500 ms.

Hyperthermia organ injury | Continue rapid active cooling; ICU early; trend temp, lactate/pH, CK, Cr, K, Ca, Mg, LFTs, INR/PTT, fibrinogen, platelets, troponin, urine output.

Physical restraints should be brief, paired with chemical control and monitoring, and removed as soon as safe. Do NOT leave a patient prone or hog-tied, stack body weight on the torso, continue physical struggle when medication has failed, or treat quietness as safety without checking ventilation, glucose, temperature, and perfusion.

7 · Disposition

Disposition is driven by physiology, complication burden, repeated sedation, and ability to identify and reverse the cause. Set Disposition above to see the matching criteria.

Temperature ≥40 C or persistent hyperthermia; pH <7.20 or rising lactate after sedation; intubation/respiratory failure/repeated deep sedation; shock/vasopressors; rhabdomyolysis with AKI or McMahon high risk; dysrhythmia/QTc >500 with instability; DIC, liver injury/failure, seizure, coma; or unclear cause with persistent delirium.

Agitation is controlled, but the delirium cause is not fully corrected; labs are abnormal but not ICU-level; CK/Cr/K/lactate/temperature need trending; an older/frail patient has new delirium; or infection, withdrawal, intoxication, or occult trauma remains possible.

Move to psychiatric evaluation only after normal/explained vitals, no hyperthermia/hypoxia/hypoglycemia/severe intoxication or withdrawal/sepsis/CNS emergency/occult trauma, and no clinically important acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, electrolyte threat, dysrhythmia, or organ injury. Document the medical reasoning — do not label the event 'excited delirium' and stop the diagnostic workup.

Discharge only with a clear reversible cause corrected, return to baseline mental status, normal oxygenation/ventilation/temperature/perfusion, no significant acidosis/rhabdomyolysis/AKI/hyperkalemia/dysrhythmia/ischemia/DIC/trauma/infection/ongoing tox risk, sedating medication sufficiently worn off, and reliable observation with return precautions. When uncertain, observe or admit.

Other pitfalls

Do NOT delay lifesaving sedation for ECG or labs in an immediate violent threat or physiologic crash.

Do NOT use antipyretics as primary therapy for heat stroke or stimulant hyperthermia — they do not fix the mechanism.

Do NOT give IM olanzapine close to parenteral benzodiazepines — case reports of respiratory arrest and death.

Do NOT use succinylcholine when hyperkalemia, severe rhabdomyolysis, crush/burn injury, or prolonged immobilization is suspected — use rocuronium.

Do NOT label the event 'excited delirium' and stop the diagnostic workup before psychiatric disposition — medical danger must be excluded first.
