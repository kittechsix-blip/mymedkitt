Laryngeal Trauma: Don't Paralyze — Airway Decision to Grade-Based Management

Adults with suspected blunt or penetrating laryngeal trauma in the ED — hard/soft-sign triage, the awake-airway decision, Schaefer-Fuhrman classification, imaging, grade-based management, and disposition including associated-injury screening.

⚠ RSI is CONTRAINDICATED in suspected laryngeal trauma — do NOT paralyze. Paralysis removes spontaneous ventilation; if intubation fails there is no backup (can't mask, can't wake), and laryngeal manipulation can create a false passage or complete obstruction — risk of CICO (can't intubate, can't oxygenate).
⚠ Awake tracheostomy is the preferred surgical airway for significant laryngeal trauma — it avoids passing an instrument through the injury site. Call anesthesia, ENT, and trauma surgery immediately and prepare a surgical airway kit at bedside.
⚠ Cricothyrotomy is CONTRAINDICATED if there is a laryngeal fracture, laryngotracheal disruption, or tracheal transection — proceed directly to tracheostomy BELOW the level of injury instead.
⚠ Even when not contraindicated, cricothyrotomy is a last-resort technique in laryngeal trauma — reserve it for cannot-ventilate emergencies when tracheostomy is not immediately available; it can still worsen the underlying injury.
⚠ Grade V (complete laryngotracheal separation) requires immediate tracheostomy below the injury and emergent surgical repair in the OR — this is the highest-mortality category; may need to retrieve the distal trachea from the mediastinum.

4 · Imaging & classification

CT neck with IV contrast (gold standard) — only perform once the airway is stable or secured. Identifies soft tissue edema, hematoma, cartilage fractures, cricoarytenoid dislocation. Low threshold for imaging — subtle CT findings may indicate significant injury.[1]

CT angiography — add for penetrating trauma or suspected vascular injury; assesses carotid/vertebral dissection.[1]

Direct laryngoscopy + esophagoscopy — performed in the OR after the airway is secured, for complete assessment of endolaryngeal and esophageal injuries.[1][2]

Grade I: 52% of cases. Grade II: 37-45%. Grades III-V: minority but highest morbidity/mortality.[1][2]

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT paralyze / perform RSI in suspected laryngeal trauma — if intubation fails there is no backup, and laryngeal manipulation can create a false passage or complete obstruction.

Do NOT attempt standard cricothyrotomy if laryngeal fracture, laryngotracheal disruption, or tracheal transection is suspected — proceed directly to tracheostomy below the injury.

Do NOT delay calling anesthesia, ENT, and trauma surgery or prepping the surgical airway kit while attempting a risky intubation attempt in an unstable airway.

Do NOT trust a single normal flexible laryngoscopy in a high-mechanism patient as definitive — edema can mask injury; repeat after swelling resolves.

Do NOT obtain CT neck before the airway is stable or secured — imaging never precedes airway control in suspected laryngeal trauma.

Do NOT discharge an asymptomatic patient with a concerning mechanism before a minimum of 6-12 hours observation and a normal flexible laryngoscopy — initial mild symptoms can accompany severe injury.

Do NOT skip the associated-injury workup — 8% of patients have associated C-spine fractures; maintain c-spine precautions until cleared.

Do NOT delay definitive OR repair beyond 24-48 hours for Grade III-V injury — delayed repair drops good-voice outcome from 58% to 22% and good-airway outcome from 87% to 55%.
