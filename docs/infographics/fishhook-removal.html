Fishhook Removal — ED Management

Patients presenting to the ED with an embedded fishhook. Covers triage screening, technique selection, removal, and post-removal wound care/disposition. Eye/eyelid involvement and vital-structure involvement (carotid, radial/ulnar/femoral vessels, peritoneum, testicle/urethra, trachea/larynx) are excluded from ED removal — these route to emergent specialty consultation instead of a removal technique.

⚠ Eye or eyelid involvement = STOP — do NOT attempt removal; cover with rigid shield, emergent ophthalmology consult, never attempt ED removal for ocular fishhook injuries
⚠ Vital structure involvement (carotid/jugular, radial/ulnar/femoral vessels, peritoneum, testicle/urethra, trachea/larynx) = do not attempt removal; imaging + surgical consultation
⚠ String-yank technique requires a FIXED body part — never on earlobe, nose, or lip (free-floating body parts)
⚠ Limit removal attempts to 2 maximum — each attempt causes more edema and bleeding
⚠ Antibiotics are NOT routinely indicated — reserve for immunocompromised patients, diabetes/PVD, deep wounds involving tendon/cartilage/bone, saltwater/brackish exposure, heavy contamination, or delayed presentation >24 hours
⚠ Do not delay removal for imaging if hook type is known and location is superficial

Anatomy & triage

Most fishhook injuries can be managed in the ED with proper technique selection. The barb is the key challenge — it prevents simple retrograde removal. Hook anatomy: eye (loop for line attachment), shank (straight portion), bend (curved portion), point (sharp tip), barb (angled projection preventing easy removal), gap/gape (distance from point to shank). Match technique to hook type, location, and depth.

  • Single-barbed — most common recreational hook
  • Treble hook — three hooks joined at eye (lures)
  • Multiple barbs — commercial fishing hooks
  • Barbless — catch-and-release hooks (easy removal)

Screen for conditions requiring immediate specialty consultation before attempting removal.

  • Eye or eyelid involvement — hook in globe, cornea, or eyelid
  • Vital structure involvement — near carotid, radial artery, testicle, peritoneum
  • Deep penetration near joint/tendon — large hook, suspected joint space or tendon involvement
  • Superficial soft tissue only — hand, finger, extremity, scalp, trunk, no vital structures

Triage STOP branches

Eye involvement — STOP
Do NOT attempt removal. Cover eye with rigid shield (metal cup/patch); do NOT apply pressure; keep patient calm, minimize eye movement; emergent ophthalmology consult; consider CT orbit if deep penetration suspected. Disposition: operating room for removal under controlled conditions — never attempt ED removal for ocular fishhook injuries. Uncontrolled removal risks globe perforation, lens damage, vitreous loss, and retinal detachment.
Vital structure involvement
Structures requiring consultation: carotid artery/jugular vein, radial/ulnar artery, femoral vessels, peritoneum/abdominal wall, testicle or urethra, trachea or larynx. Do not attempt removal; obtain imaging (CT or ultrasound) to assess depth; appropriate surgical consultation; stabilize hook to prevent further injury. Disposition: operating room or procedural suite with surgical backup.

Concerning features: large commercial hooks (>2cm gap), pain with passive ROM of adjacent joint, hook near flexor tendon sheath, penetration depth >1.5-2cm, unable to assess depth clinically.

Next steps: X-ray to assess hook position and size; consider ultrasound for soft tissue assessment; if joint space or tendon involvement suspected, hand surgery or orthopedic consult.

If superficial on imaging: proceed to technique selection — advance-and-cut technique is usually successful for large hooks.

Assessment, imaging & prep

  • History: hook type (single, treble, barbed, barbless), time of injury, how it occurred, water exposure (fresh, salt, contaminated), tetanus status
  • Exam: entry point location, direction of barb (critical for technique selection), depth of penetration, proximity to vital structures, neurovascular status distal to injury, associated injuries
  • Have patient draw/describe the hook if not visible — helps plan technique
  • Remove all attached fishing line, bait, and lure components first; tape/cut uninvolved hooks on treble hooks

X-ray is not required for all fishhook injuries but is helpful in specific situations: deep penetration with uncertain depth, large hook size, near bone or joint, multiple hooks (treble) with unclear positions, unknown hook type, or failed removal attempt. Findings show hook type, size, orientation, proximity to bone/joint, and barb direction. Ultrasound may help visualize the hook in soft tissue and assess proximity to tendons/vessels.

Do not delay removal
Do not delay removal for imaging if hook type is known and location is superficial.
  • Needle driver or hemostat
  • Wire cutters (must cut through hook metal)
  • 18-gauge needle
  • Antiseptic (povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine)
  • String/suture material (0-silk or fishing line)
  • Local anesthetic (lidocaine without epinephrine), 25-27 gauge needle for infiltration
  • Eye protection for patient AND provider
  1. Remove all attached line, bait, lures
  2. Clean area with antiseptic
  3. Tape or cut uninvolved hooks on treble hooks
  4. Position patient comfortably with good lighting
  5. Put on eye protection (hook can fly during string-yank)

Anesthesia: String-yank often needs no anesthesia (fastest, least painful). All other techniques: local infiltration around hook. Digital block for finger/toe involvement.

Removal techniques

Choose technique based on hook characteristics and location. Start with the least traumatic option.

TechniqueBest forSuccess rateKey steps
RetrogradeBarbless or very superficially embedded hooks~30-40%Press shank down to disengage barb; back out along entry path; stop if resistance felt
String-yankSmall-medium hook on a FIXED body part (not earlobe/nose/lip)65-90%Wrap string at bend; press shank down; jerk parallel to shank in one quick motion
Advance-and-cutLarge hooks or barb near skin surface>95%Advance hook until barb exits skin; cut barb with wire cutters; back remainder out through entry wound
Needle-coverDeeply embedded or sensitive area (face, ear, nose, joint)60-70%Insert 18G needle along entry tract, bevel toward hook curve, to sheath the barb; withdraw hook and needle together

Indications: barbless hooks, very superficially embedded hooks, barb not yet engaged in tissue.

Technique: apply downward pressure on hook shank to disengage barb; while maintaining pressure, back hook out along entry path using smooth, steady motion.

Success rate: ~30-40%. If resistance is felt, stop immediately — the barb is engaged, switch to another technique, do not force.

Advantage: no additional tissue trauma if successful. Disadvantage: rarely successful for barbed hooks.

Contraindication: free-floating body parts (earlobe, nose, lip) — must have a stable surface to press against.

Equipment: 0-silk suture, fishing line, or umbilical tape (~18 inches); eye protection for all personnel.

Technique: wrap string around the bend of the hook at midpoint; wind string around index finger for a secure grip; stabilize the skin surface against a flat surface; apply firm downward pressure on the hook shank (to disengage barb); while maintaining shank pressure, jerk the string parallel to the shank in one quick motion. The hook will fly — ensure clear trajectory.

Success rate: 65-90%. Often needs no anesthesia, creates no new wound, can be done in the field — least painful technique.

Indications: large hooks, barb visible near skin surface, string-yank failed or contraindicated, multiple-barbed hooks. Anesthesia is always required (local infiltration at the exit point).

Single-barbed hooks: anesthetize skin where the barb will exit; grasp shank with needle driver/hemostat; advance hook forward through tissue until barb exits skin; cut barb off with wire cutters; back remaining hook out through entry wound.

Treble/multiple hooks: advance hook until barb exits; cut shank at entry point (not the barb); pull entire hook through in direction of advancement — avoids backing the barb through tissue.

Success rate: >95%. Disadvantage: creates a second wound (exit point).

Indications: face, ear, nose (where a new wound is undesirable); large hooks with prominent barbs; single-barbed hooks only. Local anesthesia required.

Technique: anesthetize thoroughly around hook; insert an 18-gauge needle (or larger for big hooks) along the entry tract, bevel facing the hook curve; advance needle to cover the barb; once sheathed, advance the hook slightly to engage the barb in the needle lumen; back out hook and needle together as a unit, rotating/twisting to help engage the barb.

Success rate: 60-70%. Requires dexterity — difficult to engage the barb blind; works best with superficially embedded hooks. Some authors use a #11 scalpel blade to create a small nick for needle entry.

Removal outcome

After the removal attempt, classify the outcome: successful removal, failed (hook still embedded), or a complication (bleeding, suspected deeper injury, broken hook).

Failed removal — reassess before retrying
Limit attempts to 2 maximum — each attempt causes more edema and bleeding. After a failed attempt: reassess hook position and depth, consider imaging if not already done, and switch to a different technique.

Escalation: string-yank failed → try advance-and-cut; needle-cover failed → try advance-and-cut; advance-and-cut failed → rarely fails, if it does, consult surgery.

Refer when: two failed attempts, significant bleeding or edema, patient intolerance/distress, technically difficult location, or concern for deeper structure involvement. Disposition: hand surgery or plastic surgery consultation for difficult cases.
Complications during removal
Broken hook: may occur with corroded or old hooks — X-ray to confirm fragment location, remove if accessible (may require surgical extraction), document retained foreign body.

Significant bleeding: apply direct pressure — usually venous and responds to pressure; if arterial (pulsatile), consider vascular consultation; tourniquet rarely needed.

Suspected tendon/nerve injury: reassess motor and sensory function, compare to pre-procedure exam — new deficit = consultation (may have been present pre-removal).

Embedded hook fragment: small fragments without symptoms may be observed — tetanus prophylaxis, antibiotics if deep or contaminated, close follow-up.

Wound care & tetanus prophylaxis

  1. Explore wound for retained foreign body (bait, debris)
  2. Irrigate thoroughly with saline or tap water
  3. Leave wound open — do not suture
  4. Apply triple antibiotic ointment and a simple dressing

Tetanus prophylaxis: administer Td/Tdap if >5 years since last booster. If never immunized or unknown, give Td/Tdap plus tetanus immune globulin (TIG) — in different extremities, never mixed in the same syringe.

  • Change dressing daily or if wet/dirty
  • Watch for infection: increasing redness/warmth/swelling, purulent drainage, fever, red streaking (lymphangitis)
