Urinary Sphincter Emergencies — ED Management

Adults presenting to the ED with acute urinary retention (obstructive, neurogenic, infectious, medication-induced, or post-operative) or new-onset urinary incontinence (overflow, urge, or neurogenic). Covers ED evaluation, neurologic screening for cauda equina syndrome, catheterization technique, alpha-blocker/5-ARI therapy, and disposition. Stress incontinence from pelvic floor weakness is addressed only as a differential — the source consult states it is not typically an ED emergency.

⚠ PVR >300 mL = definite retention requiring catheterization; PVR >1000 mL = high-volume retention with post-obstructive diuresis risk

⚠ Do NOT replace 100% of post-obstructive diuresis urine output — replace only 50-75%, over-replacement perpetuates the diuresis

Key principle

Normal voiding requires an intact detrusor contraction (parasympathetic S2-S4), coordinated sphincter relaxation (somatic S2-S4), a patent urethra, and intact cortical inhibition. Presentations split into retention (cannot empty) and incontinence (cannot control voiding) — both can signal a life-threatening neurologic emergency.

Bladder dysfunction + back pain + leg symptoms = cauda equina until proven otherwise.

  • Saddle anesthesia
  • Bladder dysfunction (retention > incontinence)
  • Bilateral leg weakness / sciatica
  • Severe low back pain, decreased anal tone, fecal incontinence, sexual dysfunction

Urge incontinence without neurologic signs

Post-operative urinary retention (POUR)
5-70% incidence depending on procedure. Risk factors: age >60, male sex, pre-existing BPH/LUTS, pelvic/anorectal or spinal-anesthesia surgery, opioid use, large-volume IV fluids, surgery >2 hours. Single in-out catheterization is often sufficient; if persistent, Foley x 24-48h and start tamsulosin. Worry if persistent >48h or neurologic symptoms appear (rule out epidural hematoma after spinal surgery).

ED evaluation

  • History: duration/onset, associated pain/fever/neuro symptoms, medication list, surgical history, prior retention episodes
  • Exam: palpable bladder, suprapubic tenderness, urethral meatus, DRE (prostate size/tenderness/nodularity/tone)
  • Bedside: bladder ultrasound (PVR) is the most important test; urinalysis
  • Labs if indicated: BMP (high-volume retention/AKI concern), PSA (non-emergent, discuss with urology), CBC/blood cultures if septic
  • Sensory: perineal/saddle sensation (S2-S4), perianal sensation, LE dermatomes
  • Motor: bilateral leg strength, toe/heel walking
  • Reflexes: patellar (L3-L4), Achilles (S1-S2), bulbocavernosus (S2-S4), anal wink (S2-S4)
  • Rectal: anal tone (decreased in CES), voluntary squeeze, prostate assessment
Sacral nerve dysfunction
Absent bulbocavernosus reflex + absent anal wink = sacral nerve dysfunction. Any neurologic finding triggers an emergent MRI lumbar spine (post-traumatic: CT first then MRI; malignancy history: MRI whole spine).

Post-void residual interpretation

If unable to void at all, PVR = total bladder volume. In overflow incontinence, a patient may void small amounts frequently but PVR remains elevated (>200 mL).

Clamping myth debunked
Rapid, complete drainage is safe. No evidence supports clamping at intervals to prevent complications — hematuria ex vacuo is usually self-limited and does NOT require clamping.

Key principle

Normal voiding requires an intact detrusor contraction (parasympathetic S2-S4), coordinated sphincter relaxation (somatic S2-S4), a patent urethra, and intact cortical inhibition. Presentations split into retention (cannot empty) and incontinence (cannot control voiding) — both can signal a life-threatening neurologic emergency.

Bladder dysfunction + back pain + leg symptoms = cauda equina until proven otherwise.

Management algorithm

Urinary Sphincter Emergency — Retention (cannot void) vs incontinence (cannot control voiding) vs mixed retention-with-overflow.

Chief complaint? — Acute retention / new-onset incontinence / mixed-overflow.

Classify the incontinence — Overflow (high PVR) vs urge (detrusor overactivity, no neuro signs) vs neurogenic (back pain, weakness, bowel changes).

Neurologic red flags? — Saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness/numbness, new bowel incontinence or constipation, severe back pain, bilateral sciatica.

Cauda equina protocol — Foley for decompression, emergent MRI lumbar spine, neurosurgery STAT, dexamethasone 10 mg IV if malignancy/abscess suspected, NPO for likely OR. Decompression <24h = best chance of recovery; bladder function has the worst recovery of all deficits.

Emergent MRI → neurosurgery, ICU — MRI lumbar spine without contrast (unless infection/tumor suspected). If unavailable: CT myelogram or transfer. Do NOT delay imaging for any other workup.

Non-neurogenic etiology? — BPH, medication-induced, infectious, or post-operative.

UA/culture, treat UTI, PCP follow-up — Most common cause is UTI. Bladder scan to rule out overflow. Red flags requiring imaging: hematuria, pelvic mass, GU malignancy history, failed empiric treatment.

Infectious cause: treat + catheterize — Acute bacterial prostatitis: exquisitely tender/boggy prostate, do NOT massage (bacteremia risk). Antibiotics + catheterize. Severe UTI: catheterize AND treat. HSV sacral radiculitis can cause retention in young patients.

Post-op retention (POUR) — 5-70% incidence depending on procedure. Bladder scan to confirm >300-400 mL. Single in-out catheterization often sufficient; Foley x 24-48h if persistent. Worry if >48h despite catheter or neurologic symptoms.

ED evaluation — History: onset, meds (anticholinergics/opioids/alpha-agonists), surgical history, prior retention. Exam: palpable bladder, urethral meatus, DRE (prostate size/tenderness/nodularity, tone). Bladder ultrasound (PVR) is the most important bedside test.

Focused neuro exam — Perineal/saddle sensation, LE strength, bulbocavernosus reflex, anal wink. Absent bulbocavernosus + absent anal wink = sacral nerve dysfunction. Any neurologic finding → emergent MRI.

Post-void residual? — <50 mL normal; 50-100 mL equivocal; 100-200 mL elevated; 200-300 mL significant; >300 mL definite retention; >1000 mL high-volume (POD risk).

PVR <100 mL: reassess, treat UTI/OAB — Adequate emptying — consider UTI, overactive bladder, interstitial cystitis. No catheter needed. Urology referral if symptoms persist.

PVR >1000 mL: BMP, monitor for POD — Obtain BMP + UA/culture. Monitor for post-obstructive diuresis (>200 mL/hr x2h or >3L/24h). Hematuria ex vacuo is usually self-limited. Complete/rapid drainage is safe — clamping myth debunked, no evidence supports interval clamping.

Catheterize — Standard 16-18 Fr; hematuria with clots 20-22 Fr; suspected stricture 14 Fr. Coude tip (curve UP) first-line in older men with suspected BPH. Lidocaine jelly 2%, 10-20 mL, wait 5 min.

Catheterization successful? — Urine draining immediately with relief vs resistance at prostatic urethra / false passage / complete obstruction.

Failed: Coude → urology STAT — Try Coude, then more lubrication or Seldinger technique. Urology for cystoscopy with guidewire or suprapubic catheter. Do NOT make repeated blind attempts (false passage risk). Start tamsulosin now while awaiting urology.

Start alpha-blocker at time of catheterization — Tamsulosin 0.4 mg PO daily — improves TWOC success from 26% to 48% (OR 2.47). Alternatives: alfuzosin, silodosin, doxazosin.

5-ARI: not helpful acutely — Finasteride or dutasteride — effect takes 3-6 months, typically started by urology, not in the ED. Bethanechol is a rarely-used cholinergic option for atonic bladder.

Disposition? — Neurologic emergency vs POD/AKI vs stable for discharge with catheter.

Admit: neurologic emergency — ICU/step-down, neurosurgery primary, serial neuro exams q2-4h, NPO for likely surgery. Bladder function has the worst prognosis for recovery.

Admit: POD / AKI — I/O q2h, replace 50-75% of hourly urine output with 0.45% NS (do NOT replace 100%), BMP q12-24h, nephrology if refractory >48h.

Discharge with Foley + tamsulosin — Tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, catheter care instructions, urology follow-up in 3-7 days for TWOC, strict return precautions.
