Rectal Prolapse Reduction — Bedside Procedure

ED/bedside patients (adult and pediatric) with rectal tissue protruding through the anus. Covers distinguishing full-thickness prolapse from mucosal prolapse/prolapsing hemorrhoids and a separate pediatric pathway. Excludes attempted bedside reduction of necrotic, strangulated, perforated/ruptured, or peritonitic prolapse — these require emergent surgical consultation, not manual reduction. Reduction of full-thickness prolapse is temporizing, not definitive; definitive treatment is operative.

⚠ Distinguish full-thickness prolapse (concentric rings + sulcus) from mucosal/hemorrhoidal prolapse (radial folds) before deciding management.
⚠ Do NOT reduce a necrotic, strangulated, perforated/ruptured, or peritonitic prolapse — call surgery emergently.
⚠ Relieve edema with liberal granulated sugar (10-15 min dwell) before attempting manual reduction.
⚠ Use gentle steady circumferential pressure, invaginating the apex first — do not force reduction.
⚠ Discharge with a bowel regimen and colorectal referral; colonoscopy for new-onset prolapse or age >50.

Overview

Use this for the patient with rectal tissue protruding through the anus. Most acute prolapse can be reduced at the bedside — the job is to confirm it is reducible, relieve edema, and reduce gently before tissue becomes nonviable.

Core sequence: confirm it is prolapse and full-thickness vs mucosal → screen viability/red flags → analgesia +/- sedation → granulated sugar to relieve edema → gentle reduction → confirm and disposition.

  • Distinguish full-thickness prolapse (concentric rings + sulcus) from mucosal/hemorrhoidal prolapse (radial folds).
  • Do NOT reduce a necrotic, strangulated, perforated/ruptured, or peritonitic prolapse — call surgery.
  • Relieve edema with liberal granulated sugar (10-15 min dwell) before attempting reduction.
  • Use gentle steady circumferential pressure, invaginating the apex first; do not force.
  • Discharge with a bowel regimen and colorectal referral; colonoscopy for new-onset or age >50.

Recognition

Inspect the protruding tissue. Full-thickness rectal prolapse shows concentric (circular) mucosal rings and a palpable sulcus between the prolapse and the anal verge. Mucosal prolapse / prolapsing internal hemorrhoids show radial folds and no sulcus.

FeatureFull-thickness prolapseMucosal / hemorrhoid prolapse
Fold patternConcentric (circular) ringsRadial folds
SulcusPalpable groove between mass and anal vergeAbsent
Typical exam clueCircular folds all the way aroundEngorged hemorrhoidal columns often visible
Pediatric pitfall — intussusception
In a child, a circumferential protrusion is usually mucosal prolapse, but a finger that passes alongside the mass into a sulcus suggests intussusception presenting at the anus rather than simple prolapse.

Mucosal prolapse / prolapsing hemorrhoids

Reduce gently, treat the driver
Mucosal prolapse and prolapsing internal hemorrhoids are usually reducible with gentle pressure and rarely strangulate, but the same edema-reduction principles apply. Reduce gently after analgesia; the granulated-sugar technique works here too if the tissue is edematous. Treat the underlying driver: constipation, straining, prolonged toileting. A thrombosed or strangulated hemorrhoid that will not reduce, or one with necrosis, warrants surgical/colorectal evaluation rather than forced reduction.

Viability screen

Red flags against bedside reduction
Before reducing, assess for tissue compromise. Reducing dead bowel or a perforation back into the abdomen is harmful. Red flags: frank necrosis or black/gangrenous mucosa, deep ulceration, peritonitis, hemodynamic instability, evisceration of small bowel through a rupture, or an irreducible incarcerated prolapse despite adequate edema reduction and analgesia.

Surgical emergency pathway

Do NOT reduce — surgical emergency
Stop. Do not attempt bedside reduction. Nonviable (necrotic/gangrenous) prolapse, strangulation, peritonitis, instability, or a ruptured prolapse with eviscerated bowel needs emergent colorectal/general surgery consultation. A rare but reported complication is rupture of the prolapse with bowel evisceration — treat as a surgical abdomen.
  1. Resuscitate: IV access, fluids, analgesia, blood work.
  2. Broad-spectrum antibiotics if perforation/peritonitis is suspected.
  3. Keep eviscerated bowel moist with saline-soaked gauze; do not push it back in.
  4. Emergent colorectal/general surgery consultation.

Analgesia, positioning, sedation

Make the patient comfortable and relaxed — a tense patient and a painful prolapse defeat reduction.

Positioning: knee-chest (prone jackknife) or left lateral decubitus with hips flexed; Trendelenburg can help gravity assist a large prolapse.

Analgesia / relaxation (titrate; see Pharmacy for full dosing):
- Local perianal field block with lidocaine (or bupivacaine for longer duration) to relax the sphincter and reduce pain.
- IV analgesia: fentanyl or morphine / hydromorphone.
- Procedural sedation (ketamine, propofol, or midazolam) for a large, painful, or anxious prolapse.

Sphincter relaxation is often the limiting step; a good block plus light sedation frequently allows reduction that pressure alone could not.

Granulated sugar (sucrose) technique

Edema is what blocks reduction. Granulated table sugar is hyperosmotic and osmotically draws fluid out of the prolapsed mucosa, shrinking it. This is a low-cost, well-described ED maneuver for edematous prolapse; it buys you a reducible mass, it is not a substitute for the viability check.

  1. Liberally pour/pack ordinary granulated table sugar over the entire exposed mucosal surface.
  2. Let it dwell 10-15 minutes (a cold compress or wrapping the mass can add osmotic/contraction effect).
  3. As the prolapse shrinks, it often begins to reduce partially on its own.
  4. If still edematous, reapply sugar and wait again — the cycle may be repeated.

Manual reduction

  1. Apply gentle, steady, circumferential pressure with the thumbs/palms over the apex of the prolapse.
  2. Invaginate the leading edge (apex) first, rolling it inward through the anal canal like reversing a sock.
  3. Maintain firm, patient pressure for 1-2 minutes rather than short forceful pushes.
  4. Once the apex passes the sphincter, the remainder usually follows.
  5. A gloved finger can guide the last segment in and confirm it has fully reduced.
If it will not reduce
After adequate edema reduction, analgesia, and sphincter relaxation, if it still will not reduce — stop forcing it. Treat as incarcerated: re-screen viability and consult surgery.

Confirming reduction

After the attempt, confirm with a digital rectal exam that the rectum is fully reduced and the tone/canal are normal.

Pediatric rectal prolapse

Pediatric rectal prolapse is usually mucosal, benign, and self-limited, often reducing spontaneously or with gentle pressure. Reduce gently with steady pressure (the sugar technique can help if edematous); sedation is rarely needed.

  • Constipation/straining — the most common driver; treat aggressively.
  • Cystic fibrosis — prolapse can be the presenting sign; have a low threshold for a sweat chloride test.
  • Chronic diarrhea / parasites.
  • Malnutrition.
  • Pertussis.
  • Neuromuscular disease.
  • A pathologic lead point (polyp) suggesting intussusception.

Disposition & follow-up detail

Most patients with a successfully reduced prolapse go home. Bowel regimen (prevent recurrence and straining): stool softener / osmotic laxative (polyethylene glycol or equivalent) and dietary fiber; avoid straining and prolonged toileting; treat constipation or diarrhea. Refer all full-thickness prolapse to colorectal surgery — definitive treatment is operative (e.g., rectopexy or perineal repair); reduction is temporizing.

ScenarioDisposition
Reduced, viable, uncomplicatedHome with bowel regimen + colorectal surgery referral
New-onset prolapse, age >50, or alarm features (bleeding, weight loss, bowel habit change)Colonoscopy to exclude a lead point or malignancy
Difficult reduction, borderline viability, significant comorbidity, or unsafe discharge (pain control/social factors)Admit / observe
Return precautions
Recurrence that will not reduce, severe pain, bleeding, fever, or signs of strangulation.
