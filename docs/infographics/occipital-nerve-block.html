Greater Occipital Nerve Block — ED Procedure

Adults in the ED with a confirmed benign headache phenotype: cluster headache (acute attack rescue or bridge therapy), occipital neuralgia, cervicogenic or post-traumatic headache, hemicrania continua, or status migrainosus refractory to the parenteral cocktail and DHE. Secondary causes (SAH, dissection, CVST, AACG, hypertensive emergency, meningitis) must be excluded first. Not for use when diagnosis is unclear — return to the Headache Hub red-flag screener first.

⚠ Exclude SAH, dissection, CVST, AACG, meningitis before performing ONB for any headache complaint.
⚠ No skin infection, no LA allergy; document anticoagulation risk/benefit.
⚠ GON lies MEDIAL to the occipital artery — palpate the pulse and aim medial.
⚠ ASPIRATE before every injection — intra-arterial → vertebral collateral → seizure risk.
⚠ Observe for 15 min post-procedure for vasovagal syncope.
⚠ Avoid steroid adjunct in pregnancy; limit repeated steroid blocks to ≤4 per year.

Contraindications & Pre-procedure Checklist

Absolute contraindications
Overlying skin infection at injection site. Known allergy to local anesthetic class (amide cross-reactivity is rare; ester allergy more common). Patient refusal.

Relative — escalate / consult:

- Therapeutic anticoagulation (INR >3, new DOAC dose <24 h, unfractionated heparin gtt) — per ASRA 2018, ONB is superficial and bleeding risk is low, but document risk/benefit
- Prior skull defect / craniectomy at injection site (palpate carefully; ultrasound if available)
- Active occipital scalp pathology (alopecia areata in the injection field, cellulitis, prior infection)
- Pregnancy: LA is generally safe; AVOID steroid adjunct (use LA-only)

  1. Informed verbal/written consent (vasovagal, transient numbness, alopecia at site if steroid used, intra-arterial injection risk)
  2. Allergies (LA, latex, antiseptic)
  3. Anticoagulation status + last dose
  4. IV access if vasovagal-prone or anxious patient
  5. Monitor in place for 15 min post-procedure

Anatomy — GON, LON, Occipital Artery

Greater Occipital Nerve (GON): medial branch of C2 dorsal ramus; emerges through trapezius/semispinalis capitis ~2-3 cm lateral to midline at the superior nuchal line. Landmark: ~1/3 of the distance from the inion to the mastoid process. Lies MEDIAL to the occipital artery — palpate the artery to avoid intra-arterial injection.

Lesser Occipital Nerve (LON): C2-C3 (cervical plexus, sensory); located lateral to GON, approximately 2/3 of the way from inion to mastoid.

Clinical pearl: for cluster headache, blocking GON alone is often sufficient (~78% acute response). For occipital neuralgia or post-traumatic HA, consider a combined GON + LON block on the symptomatic side.

Steroid adjunct — evidence note
RCTs (Ashkenazi 2008, Cuadrado 2017) show steroid does NOT add benefit beyond LA-alone for cluster headache. Steroid adjunct is reasonable for cervicogenic headache and hemicrania continua only. Avoid steroid in pregnancy and in repeated blocks (alopecia, Cushingoid features); limit repeated steroid blocks to ≤4 per year.

Technique — Mark & Inject

  1. Palpate the external occipital protuberance (inion) — bony midline prominence at the back of the head.
  2. Palpate the mastoid process — bony prominence behind the ear.
  3. Draw an imaginary line between them along the superior nuchal line.
  4. Mark a point ~1/3 of the distance from inion to mastoid — this is the GON target.
  5. Palpate for the occipital artery pulse at this point. The GON lies MEDIAL to the artery. Mark the GON injection point 1-2 cm medial to the pulse.
  6. If blocking LON: mark a second point ~2/3 of the inion-to-mastoid distance.
  7. Prep skin with chlorhexidine or alcohol; let dry.
  1. Hold the syringe perpendicular to the skull.
  2. Advance the needle slowly until you contact bone (skull is the safety stop — prevents intracranial penetration).
  3. Withdraw 1-2 mm off bone.
  4. ASPIRATE before injecting. If blood returns → withdraw, redirect 1-2 mm medially, re-aspirate.
  5. Inject 2-3 mL of LA (± steroid adjunct) slowly over ~30 seconds.
  6. Withdraw needle, apply pressure with gauze for 1-2 min, place bandage.
  7. Repeat on contralateral side ONLY if bilateral indication (cluster is unilateral — only block the symptomatic side).
Intra-arterial injection risk
Intra-arterial injection into the occipital artery (which feeds into the vertebral system via collaterals) can cause seizure or transient neurologic symptoms. ASPIRATE before every injection.
—Bupivacaine max safe dose (2 mg/kg, no epi)
—Lidocaine max safe dose (4.5 mg/kg, no epi)

Blood on Aspiration — What Now

Do not inject at this position
You aspirated before injecting (good) and got blood return. The occipital artery runs near the GON target. Do NOT inject at this position.
  • Withdraw and redirect medially 1-2 mm. GON lies medial to the artery. Re-aspirate. Inject only if dry.
  • If unable to redirect to a dry pocket after 2 attempts, abort the procedure — do not proceed. Discuss alternative (sphenopalatine block, pharmacologic) with the patient.

Post-procedure Care & Discharge

Observe for 15 min for vasovagal syncope (1-3% incidence). Document baseline + post-procedure neuro exam (GON sensory distribution — occiput, vertex, posterior scalp). Onset of relief: 5-15 minutes for cluster. LA-only duration: 4-6 hours (bupivacaine) or 1-2 hours (lidocaine). LA + steroid duration: weeks to months (cervicogenic / hemicrania continua).

  • Numbness in occipital distribution is expected for 4-6 h (or longer with steroid)
  • Mild local soreness for 1-2 days is normal
  • Return precautions: severe new headache, fever, expanding hematoma, neurologic deficit beyond expected numbness, signs of infection at injection site
  • Follow-up with neurology / headache specialist within 1-2 weeks for outpatient prophylaxis planning
  • If this was a cluster acute block: also discharge with a verapamil-bridge plan
