Brief Resolved Unexplained Event (BRUE) — Recognition, Risk Stratification & Disposition

Infants <1 year in the ED with a sudden, brief (<1 minute), NOW RESOLVED episode of cyanosis/pallor, breathing change, tone change, or altered responsiveness that remains unexplained after history and exam. Excludes any event where fever, respiratory infection, witnessed GER/vomiting, choking, or witnessed seizure explains the episode — those are not BRUE and route to the identified cause instead.

⚠ BRUE = <1 year, sudden, brief (<1 minute) episode, NOW RESOLVED, unexplained after H&P — if a cause is identified, it is NOT a BRUE.
⚠ Lower-risk BRUE (ALL of: >60 days old, not markedly preterm, first event, <1 minute, no CPR by a trained provider, normal exam) → minimal workup, discharge appropriate.
⚠ >90% of BRUEs are higher-risk — admit for a tiered, hypothesis-driven workup prioritizing non-accidental trauma, cardiac arrhythmia, and serious infection.
⚠ Obtain a 12-lead ECG in ALL higher-risk BRUE — screens for long QT (QTc >460 ms) and WPW, either of which can cause apnea/BRUE and sudden death.
⚠ NAT screening is essential in every BRUE — bruising in a non-mobile infant is a red flag; apply the TEN-4 FACES rule and report to CPS if suspected.
⚠ Test for pertussis if underimmunized or exposed — young infants may present with apnea/cyanosis, not the classic 'whoop'; admit infants <3 months given apnea risk.

Recognition

BRUE replaced ALTE in 2016 — more precise definition, less alarming terminology. Infant <1 year with a sudden, brief (<1 minute), NOW RESOLVED episode of ≥1 of the features below. Key distinction: the event must be UNEXPLAINED after H&P — if a cause is identified, it is not a BRUE.

FeatureDescription
Cyanosis or pallorCentral (not acrocyanosis)
Breathing changeAbsent, decreased, or irregular
Tone changeHypertonia or hypotonia
ResponsivenessAltered level of consciousness
NOT a BRUE if:
Fever present, respiratory infection symptoms (bronchiolitis, URI), GER with witnessed vomiting/regurgitation, choking on feeding or foreign body, witnessed seizure activity, event not resolved (still symptomatic), or age ≥1 year. If any exclusion is present, diagnose and treat the underlying cause instead.

Higher-risk is the majority
>90% of BRUEs are classified as higher-risk — the low-risk category is intentionally narrow.
  • AAP strong recommendation AGAINST: routine CBC, BMP, blood gas
  • AGAINST: chest X-ray, echocardiogram, EEG, neuroimaging (CT/MRI)
  • AGAINST: upper GI series, LP/CSF analysis, empiric acid suppression (PPI/H2)
  • AGAINST: admission solely for monitoring

MAY consider (weak recommendations): 12-lead ECG (screen long QT/WPW), pertussis testing if underimmunized or outbreak area, and brief observation (1-4 hours) with pulse oximetry. Education is key — reassure parents, teach infant CPR, safe-sleep counseling, and when to return.

Observation: 1-4 hours with continuous pulse oximetry, serial exams watching for recurrence, and documentation of normal vital signs and exam at discharge. Parent education: infant CPR training, safe sleep (supine, no co-sleeping, firm mattress), and return precautions.

  • Normal observation period (1-4 hours) with no recurrent events
  • Normal vital signs and exam
  • Parent education completed (infant CPR, safe sleep)
  • Follow-up arranged with PCP within 24-48 hours
  • Prognosis: recurrence ~5%, serious underlying condition ~1% in true low-risk BRUE

Most common identifiable causes
1. GER-related laryngospasm. 2. Lower respiratory infection. 3. Seizure. 4. Child maltreatment. Serious underlying condition: ~1% of low-risk, ~5% of high-risk.

NAT, pertussis, and cardiac causes

NAT red flags
Recurrent events with same caregiver, inconsistent or changing history, delay in seeking care, unexplained injuries, prior CPS involvement, domestic violence in home, substance abuse, young/isolated/stressed caregivers. TEN-4 FACES rule: bruising on Torso, Ear, Neck in any child <4 years, OR Face, Arm, Cheek, Eyes, Soft tissue in infant <4 months → highly suspicious for abuse. If suspected: head CT/MRI, skeletal survey, dilated fundoscopic exam, social work consult, mandated CPS report, admit for safety.

Infants <3 months may not have the classic 'whoop' — presenting instead with apnea, cyanosis, paroxysmal cough, post-tussive emesis, poor feeding. Test (nasopharyngeal PCR) if underimmunized (0-2 DTaP doses), pertussis exposure, outbreak in community, paroxysmal cough, or any respiratory symptoms.

BRUE algorithm

BRUE: sudden, brief (<1 min), resolved event — Infant <1 year, ≥1 of cyanosis/pallor, breathing change, tone change, or altered responsiveness — unexplained after H&P.

Meets BRUE criteria (unexplained after H&P)? — Fever, respiratory infection, witnessed GER/vomiting, choking, or witnessed seizure explain the event → not a BRUE.

Not a BRUE — manage identified cause — Document the identified cause and manage per the appropriate pathway (fever, bronchiolitis, GER, choking, seizure, pertussis).

Lower-risk or higher-risk? — ALL lower-risk criteria must be met (age, prematurity, event count, duration, CPR, exam); any deviation = higher-risk.

Lower-risk: avoid routine testing — AAP strongly recommends against routine labs/imaging/EEG/LP/acid suppression/admission-for-monitoring. May consider ECG, pertussis testing, brief observation.

Observation complete, no concerns? — 1-4 hours continuous pulse oximetry, serial exams, parent education (CPR, safe sleep).

Discharge home — Close PCP follow-up 24-48 hours, infant CPR resources, written return precautions. Recurrence ~5%, serious condition ~1%.

Higher-risk: tiered, hypothesis-driven evaluation — Prioritize time-sensitive conditions: NAT, feeding/aspiration, cardiac arrhythmia, serious infection, congenital anomaly. Avoid indiscriminate testing.

Tier-1 testing: pulse ox, ECG, glucose, BMP/CBC, pertussis — Continuous pulse ox 4+ hours, 12-lead ECG, blood glucose, VBG/BMP, CBC, pertussis PCR if indicated; add NAT/cardiac/seizure/GI workup per concern.

NAT screening — TEN-4 FACES — Bruising on torso/ear/neck (<4 years) or face/arm/cheek/eyes/soft tissue (<4 months) is highly suspicious for abuse. Head CT/MRI, skeletal survey, dilated fundoscopy, social work, mandated CPS report.

Pertussis: apnea without classic whoop — Test NP PCR if underimmunized, exposed, or outbreak. Treat with azithromycin 10 mg/kg/day × 5 days; admit infants <3 months (apnea risk).

Cardiac: ECG for all — screen long QT, WPW — QTc >460 ms (>480 concerning) = prolonged. WPW delta wave/short PR. Cardiology consult if abnormal.

Admission criteria & orders — Continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring, pulse oximetry, apnea monitoring, observed feedings, social work if NAT concern.

Disposition: discharge, admit, or transfer? — Low-risk + normal observation = discharge; higher-risk or recurrent = admit; needs subspecialty/PICU = transfer.

Discharge instructions — Infant CPR, safe sleep, return precautions, PCP follow-up 24-48 hours. Home apnea monitoring NOT routinely recommended.

Admission orders — higher-risk BRUE — Continuous monitor, apnea alarm, tier-2 testing guided by clinical suspicion, subspecialty consults, social work if NAT concern.

Transfer to higher level of care — Recurrent apnea, respiratory failure, hemodynamic instability, suspected NAT needing child-abuse team, or need for subspecialty expertise not available locally.
