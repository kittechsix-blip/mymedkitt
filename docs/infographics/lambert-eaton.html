Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome: Recognize, Avoid, Hunt the Cancer

Adults presenting with new proximal weakness, hyporeflexia, or suspected Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) in the ED or on the inpatient neurology service. Excludes established myasthenia gravis, pediatric NMJ disorders, and other paraneoplastic syndromes without VGCC antibodies — each needs a different pathway.

⚠ AVOID neuromuscular blockers (depolarizing AND non-depolarizing) — LEMS causes profound, prolonged paralysis and is frequently first discovered via unexpected prolonged blockade after anesthesia. If RSI is unavoidable, anticipate marked dose sensitivity and favor a sugammadex-reversible agent.
⚠ AVOID aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones — both impair presynaptic neuromuscular junction transmission.
⚠ AVOID IV magnesium — competes with presynaptic calcium influx and can precipitate severe weakness.
⚠ AVOID calcium-channel blockers, especially verapamil and diltiazem — documented precipitants of respiratory failure in LEMS.
⚠ AVOID iodinated IV contrast — reported to exacerbate weakness.
⚠ Pulse oximetry is a poor, late indicator of neuromuscular respiratory strength — do not be reassured by a normal SpO2; trend FVC, NIF, and single-breath count instead.

1 · Recognition & mechanism

The mirror image of myasthenia gravis
LEMS is a presynaptic autoimmune disorder: antibodies against P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCC) on the motor nerve terminal reduce calcium influx and acetylcholine release. Rapid repetitive activity lets calcium build up, transiently boosting ACh release — strength and reflexes improve with use (facilitation), the mirror image of MG fatigability.[1]

~50-60% is paraneoplastic, almost always tied to small cell lung cancer (SCLC) — LEMS often precedes the cancer diagnosis by months to years. The rest is autoimmune/idiopathic. Classic patient: an older smoker with proximal leg weakness, dry mouth, and hard-to-elicit reflexes.

2 · What to do now

Toggle MG-like presentation above if the picture is ocular/bulbar-onset and fatigable rather than proximal and improving with use.

Triad present — confirm LEMS and hunt for cancer
Proximal leg weakness + dry mouth + reflexes that augment with exercise: send anti-P/Q VGCC antibodies, proceed to electrodiagnostics, and start the SCLC hunt with CT chest → PET.
MG-like presentation — reassess the diagnosis
Ocular/bulbar onset, fatigable, normal reflexes points toward myasthenia gravis, not LEMS — work it up on that track (anti-AChR/MuSK, RNS decrement, thymoma screen).

3 · The LEMS triad

  1. Proximal muscle weakness — legs > arms, symmetric; difficulty rising from a chair or climbing stairs; atrophy mild relative to weakness.
  2. Autonomic dysfunction (80-96%) — dry mouth is the most common and often earliest symptom; also constipation, erectile dysfunction, orthostatic hypotension, reduced sweating.
  3. Hypo/areflexia that AUGMENTS after exercise — post-exercise facilitation, the bedside hallmark and the opposite of MG fatigability.

Ocular/bulbar involvement is present but milder and later than MG — bulbar/ocular onset should prompt reassessment for MG.

4 · Diagnosis

Three pillars: clinical triad + serology + electrodiagnostics.

Serology
Anti-P/Q-type VGCC antibodies positive in 85-95% (titers >~30 pmol/L support the diagnosis in context); ~10-15% seronegative. N-type VGCC antibodies are NOT useful in isolation — do not diagnose LEMS off an isolated N-type result. SOX1 antibodies (~64% in SCLC-LEMS, ~95% specific) flag a paraneoplastic process.[4]
Electrodiagnostics (repetitive nerve stimulation)
Low resting CMAP amplitude; decrement at low-frequency (2-5 Hz) stimulation; >60% (often >100%) INCREMENT at high-frequency (20-50 Hz) or after 10 s of maximal contraction — the electrodiagnostic signature of post-exercise facilitation.

5 · Hunt the small cell lung cancer

Every new LEMS diagnosis triggers a malignancy hunt
Treating the SCLC often improves the neurologic syndrome, and LEMS may be the first sign of an otherwise-occult tumor. CT chest first-line; if negative, FDG-PET/CT. If initial imaging is negative, repeat screening every 3-6 months for at least 2 years (high-risk: every 3 months) — confirm the surveillance interval against current institutional guidance.

  • Current smoking

7 · Respiratory monitoring

Frank respiratory failure is less common than in MG but does occur — and is often iatrogenic (see drugs to avoid). It is the only truly emergent LEMS complication. Monitor FVC and NIF, bedside single-breath count (cannot reach 20 = concerning), and bulbar safety (cough strength, secretion handling, swallow).

Thresholds are extrapolated from myasthenia gravis
LEMS-specific FVC/NIF cutoffs are not well established — use the MG-derived thresholds below (FVC <20 mL/kg or NIF <30 cmH2O → intubate) and trend the patient rather than anchoring on a single value.
