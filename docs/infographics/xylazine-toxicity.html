Xylazine Toxicity ("Tranq Dope") — OA2A Toxidrome, Wounds & Withdrawal

Adults ≥18, ED management of xylazine/medetomidine-adulterated opioid exposure — acute OA2A (opioid + alpha-2 agonist) overdose, xylazine-associated wounds (XAW), and xylazine withdrawal. Excludes pediatric dosing and non-ED outpatient addiction-medicine continuity care.

⚠ Xylazine and medetomidine are alpha-2 adrenergic agonists, NOT opioids — naloxone does NOT reverse their sedation. STILL give naloxone: ~95% of xylazine overdoses involve fentanyl co-ingestion.
⚠ Titrate naloxone to respiratory rate >12, NOT to wakefulness — over-reversal in a patient who stays sedated from alpha-2 agonism can precipitate opioid withdrawal with vomiting and aspiration risk.
⚠ Do NOT give atropine as first-line for xylazine-associated bradycardia — peripheral alpha-2 vasoconstriction is still present, so unopposed muscarinic blockade can precipitate a hypertensive crisis. Dobutamine is preferred if treatment is needed.
⚠ Do NOT aggressively debride xylazine-associated wounds — islands of healthy tissue represent healing potential; autolytic debridement is preferred.
⚠ Stage 3A xylazine wound (exposed bone, function preserved): surgical consult for serial debridement; amputation ONLY if conservative management fails.
⚠ Stage 3B xylazine wound (exposed bone, osteomyelitis, no meaningful function): amputation is the recommendation — discuss goals of care and document shared decision-making before proceeding.
⚠ No antidote exists for xylazine or medetomidine — atipamezole (veterinary alpha-2 antagonist) is not available for human use.

Overview — Recognition

Alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, same class as clonidine/dexmedetomidine — FDA-approved for veterinary use ONLY. Street names: Tranq, Tranq dope, Sleep cut, Zombie drug. Xylazine plasma half-life (IV): 23-50 minutes, longer with IM/SC. NOT detected by standard urine drug screen (requires GC-MS/LC-MS confirmatory testing — 7-8 day turnaround — or lateral-flow strips). No antidote — atipamezole (veterinary alpha-2 antagonist) is not available for human use.[1][2]

2025 update: medetomidine now dominant in some markets
Medetomidine — 10x more potent than xylazine — reached 72% of Philadelphia drug-supply samples (Oct-Dec 2024) while xylazine dropped from 98% to 31%. Same alpha-2 agonist management principles apply, but expect more severe sedation and bradycardia. Xylazine and medetomidine are NOT opioids and are NOT reversed by naloxone — but still give naloxone: 95% of xylazine overdoses involve fentanyl co-ingestion.[2]

Withdrawal Management

Xylazine withdrawal symptoms begin 4-6 hours after last use (faster than opioid withdrawal): anxiety, irritability, restlessness, dysphoria, tachycardia, diaphoresis, hypertension. No GI symptoms, unlike opioid withdrawal. Buprenorphine and methadone do NOT treat the xylazine component.[1][5]

Algorithm

Xylazine Toxicity — Initial Presentation — Alpha-2 agonist NOT reversed by naloxone. Still give naloxone — 95% of xylazine overdoses involve fentanyl co-ingestion.

Respiratory status? — Xylazine + opioid = synergistic respiratory depression. Xylazine alone rarely causes apnea; combined with fentanyl it can cause profound respiratory failure.

Xylazine-Associated Wound (XAW) present? — XAW not always at injection sites — systemic vasoconstriction + direct cytotoxicity. 56% MRSA, 37% Group A Strep on culture. Do NOT misdiagnose as necrotizing fasciitis.

Withdrawal symptoms present — Onset 4-6 hours after last use (faster than opioid withdrawal). No GI symptoms (unlike opioid withdrawal). Buprenorphine/methadone do NOT treat the xylazine component.

Give naloxone — titrate to RR, not wakefulness — 0.4-2 mg IV/IM q2-3min PRN (4 mg intranasal), goal RR >12 and SpO2 >92% — NOT full arousal. Excess dosing risks precipitating withdrawal with aspiration.

Stage 1-2 XAW — conservative wound care — Superficial (Stage 1) or exposed muscle/tendon with preserved function (Stage 2). Avoid aggressive debridement; autolytic debridement preferred. See antibiotic selection below.

Stage 3A XAW — exposed bone, function preserved — Surgical consult for serial debridement. IV vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam. Amputation ONLY if conservative management fails.

Withdrawal treatment: clonidine first-line — Clonidine 0.1-0.3 mg PO q4-6h (hold if SBP <90 or HR <50). Dexmedetomidine ICU infusion for severe withdrawal. Combined opioid+xylazine withdrawal: start buprenorphine and clonidine simultaneously; xylazine does not block buprenorphine — MOUD still works for the opioid component.

Response to naloxone? — Pure opioid OD: respirations improve AND patient wakes. OA2A toxidrome: respirations improve BUT patient stays sedated. No response after 10mg total naloxone: reconsider non-opioid cause; airway criteria include persistent apnea, GCS <8 with aspiration risk, copious secretions, or refractory hypoxia — recovery position/BVM/O2 first, intubate if criteria met.

Stage 3B XAW — exposed bone, no function — Osteomyelitis present with no meaningful function preserved. Amputation is the recommendation — discuss goals of care and document shared decision-making.

Withdrawal-track disposition? — Discharge criteria: hemodynamically stable (HR>50, SBP>90), RR>12, SpO2>92%, ambulatory, tolerating PO, 4-6h post-naloxone without re-sedation.

Cardiovascular findings? — Mechanism: initial peripheral alpha-2B vasoconstriction (transient hypertension) then central alpha-2A sympatholysis (bradycardia + hypotension). Expect HR 40-60, SBP 80-100, possible QTc prolongation.

Wound-track disposition? — Discharge: Stage 1 or mild non-infected Stage 2 with outpatient antibiotics. Admit: infected Stage 2 or Stage 3 for IV antibiotics, surgical consult, serial debridement.

ED observation 4-6h post-naloxone — Continuous pulse oximetry, vitals q1h, LOC checks, aspiration precautions (elevate HOB). Re-dose naloxone if RR<12, SpO2<92%, or increasing sedation.

SBP <90 — volume, then norepinephrine — NS/LR 500-1000mL bolus first-line, often sufficient. Refractory: norepinephrine 0.1-0.3 mcg/kg/min IV, start low. NE preferred over phenylephrine — pure alpha agonists may worsen tissue perfusion.

Symptomatic bradycardia — avoid atropine — Often does not require treatment if asymptomatic. If symptomatic: dobutamine 2.5-10 mcg/kg/min or isoproterenol 1-4 mcg/min preferred; atropine 0.5-1mg IV is last resort only.

Monitor — no antidote exists — Continuous pulse ox, telemetry, temperature, glucose, ECG. Minimum 4-6 hours post-naloxone. No antidote — atipamezole is not available for human use.

Discharge — harm reduction kit — Narcan nasal spray, fentanyl/xylazine test strips, sterile injection supplies, wound care if applicable. Never Use Alone, SAMHSA helpline, MOUD referral, return precautions.

Admit — telemetry / observation — Infected wounds needing IV antibiotics, withdrawal management with clonidine, or persistent sedation >6 hours. Telemetry, vitals q2-4h, addiction medicine + wound care/surgery + social work consults.

Admit — ICU — Intubated, vasopressor requirement, dexmedetomidine infusion for severe withdrawal, or Stage 3 wounds with sepsis. Medetomidine withdrawal: 77.5% require ICU admission.

Naloxone: 0.4-2 mg IV/IM (or 4 mg intranasal) IV, IM, or IN — Repeat every 2-3 minutes PRN, up to 10 mg total. Titrate to RR >12 and SpO2 >92% — NOT full arousal. If no response after 10 mg total, reconsider a non-opioid cause.

No response to naloxone — airway management criteria

  • Bag-valve mask — rescue breaths are especially helpful.
  • Supplemental O2 via nasal cannula or non-rebreather.
  • Intubate if: persistent apnea despite naloxone, GCS <8 with aspiration risk, copious secretions/emesis, or refractory hypoxia.

Post-intubation: sedation may be difficult to titrate (patient already sedated from the alpha-2 agonist) — plan for prolonged intubation if a severe xylazine component is suspected.
