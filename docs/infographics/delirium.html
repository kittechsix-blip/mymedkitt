Delirium — ED Recognition & Management

Adults presenting with acute or fluctuating confusion/altered mental status in the ED. Excludes chronic dementia without acute change and primary psychiatric disorders without medical precipitant. Delirium is a symptom requiring urgent etiology search, not just behavioral management.

⚠ Do NOT sedate hypoactive delirium — antipsychotics are not beneficial; treat the underlying cause instead.
⚠ Do NOT combine olanzapine with parenteral (IM/IV) benzodiazepines — risk of fatal respiratory depression and hypotension.
⚠ AVOID benzodiazepines in elderly delirium — independent risk factor for delirium, falls, paradoxical agitation, and respiratory depression.
⚠ AVOID typical antipsychotics (haloperidol, droperidol) in Parkinson disease / Lewy body dementia — risk of severe neuroleptic sensitivity and worsened motor symptoms.
⚠ AVOID antipsychotics in acute stimulant (sympathomimetic) intoxication — lowers seizure threshold and impairs thermoregulation; use benzodiazepines instead.
⚠ QTc monitoring required after haloperidol/droperidol — QTc >500 ms or increase >60 ms from baseline: discontinue the agent and correct K⁺/Mg²⁺.
⚠ NEVER prone positioning during physical restraint — risk of positional asphyxia. Restraints are a temporizing measure only and can worsen agitation.
⚠ Give thiamine BEFORE dextrose in suspected alcohol use disorder to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy.

Initial assessment

Delirium is acute brain dysfunction — DSM-5 defines it as an acute/fluctuating disturbance in attention and cognition. 7-24% of elderly ED patients have delirium; up to 80% of ICU patients. Missed diagnosis rate 54-89%. Mortality is comparable to sepsis and MI. Delirium is a symptom requiring urgent etiology search, not just behavioral management.

  • Remove sharps/equipment from reach; minimum 5 staff for physical restraint; activate security if needed
  • Approach calmly, non-threateningly; one person speaks; position near exit, do not corner the patient
  • Verbal de-escalation (AAEP Project BETA) avoided sedation in 27% of cases in one study

Primary survey: ABCs, vitals, fingerstick glucose. Fingerstick glucose — D50W 25g IV if <60 mg/dL. SpO2 — supplement O2 if hypoxic. Temperature — fever suggests infection; >104°F (40°C) raises concern for excited delirium or CNS infection. Naloxone 0.4-2 mg IV/IN if opioid features present. Thiamine before dextrose in suspected alcohol use disorder to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy.

Reversible cause found — correct it
Hypoglycemia: D50W 25g IV (adults) or D10W 2-5 mL/kg (peds), recheck glucose in 15 min. Hypoxia: supplemental O2 to SpO2 >94%. Opioid toxicity: Naloxone 0.4-2 mg IV/IN, may repeat q2-3 min; consider infusion (2/3 of effective dose per hour) if recurrent. Hypothermia: active warming. Hypotension: IV fluid bolus. If mental status does not improve after correction, proceed with full delirium evaluation — multiple precipitants often coexist.

CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) — delirium present when ALL of: (1) acute onset or fluctuating course, (2) inattention, PLUS EITHER (3) altered level of consciousness (RASS ≠ 0) or (4) disorganized thinking. Obtain baseline mental status from family/caregivers/prior records — without a baseline, diagnosis is unreliable.

Hypoactive delirium — the most commonly missed subtype
32-67% undiagnosed in the ED. Higher mortality than hyperactive. Often dismissed as "tired" or baseline dementia — verify against family/prior documentation. Antipsychotics are NOT beneficial — focus on treating the underlying cause.

Agitation, combativeness, hallucinations. Mixed delirium fluctuates between subtypes — most persistent course. Distinguish from primary psychosis: delirium is acute/fluctuating with inattention and altered consciousness; new-onset "psychiatric" symptoms without psychiatric history should be assumed medical until proven otherwise.

Hyperactive delirium with hyperthermia — medical emergency
Severe agitation plus hyperthermia, acidosis, or prolonged physical struggle can rapidly progress to dysrhythmia, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, or arrest. Temperature >104°F is a major danger sign. Immediate: remove from prone position → aggressive cooling → IV access → rapid sedation and monitoring.

Etiology-directed workup

Multiple causes often coexist; in 13% of cases no precipitant is identified. All categories converge on the same next step — non-pharmacological management — regardless of which is suspected.

  • Remove unnecessary lines/leads/catheters (Foley is a known precipitant); allow mobility within safety bounds

Never prone; monitor closely
Indicated only for imminent danger despite verbal de-escalation. Minimum 5-person team, soft restraints preferred, head of bed elevated, secure to bed frame (not side rails). NEVER prone positioning — risk of positional asphyxia. Reassess q15 min (neurovascular checks); re-evaluate need q1-2h. Concurrent pharmacological management should be initiated to facilitate removal as quickly as possible.

▶ What to do now

Excited delirium syndrome → sedate, cool, monitor for arrest
Midazolam 5 mg IM (repeat q5-10 min) or, for immediate violent threat/failed benzodiazepine, Ketamine 4 mg/kg IM (prepare airway). Aggressive cooling to target <101°F. Large-bore IV ×2. Continuous telemetry — anticipate PEA/asystole. Labs: CK, K⁺, VBG/lactate, creatinine. ICU disposition mandatory.
Hypoactive → do NOT sedate
Treat the underlying cause — antipsychotics are not beneficial for hypoactive delirium and can worsen outcomes.
Non-pharmacologic control adequate
Continue reorientation, environmental modification, and etiology treatment. Reassess if agitation escalates or safety becomes a concern.
Young adult / undifferentiated
Haloperidol 5-10 mg IM + Midazolam 2.5-5 mg IM ("B52") — combination achieves faster resolution than either alone; QTc monitoring required. OR Droperidol 5 mg IM monotherapy — faster onset (3-10 min). OR Olanzapine 10 mg IM — no QTc effect, no dystonia, but do NOT combine with parenteral benzodiazepines. Wait ≥20 min before redosing.
Elderly (≥65) — reduce all doses 50%, avoid benzodiazepines
Haloperidol 0.5-2 mg IM/IV OR Olanzapine 2.5-5 mg IM. Mild agitation, cooperative patient: Quetiapine 25-50 mg PO or Risperidone 0.5-1 mg PO (less sedating). Black box: 1.6-1.7× mortality risk with antipsychotics in dementia-related psychosis — document risk-benefit discussion.
Parkinson's / Lewy body dementia — avoid typical antipsychotics
First-line: Quetiapine 12.5-25 mg PO (lowest D2 affinity). If severe agitation and PO not feasible: low-dose Midazolam 1-2 mg IM (avoid high doses) or low-dose Olanzapine 2.5 mg IM. Consult neurology early.
Intoxication / withdrawal — benzodiazepines are the drug of choice
Alcohol/sedative withdrawal: Lorazepam 1-2 mg IV or Midazolam 2.5-5 mg IM. Sympathomimetic intoxication: benzodiazepines preferred — AVOID antipsychotics (lowers seizure threshold, impairs thermoregulation). Active cooling and aggressive IV fluids if hyperthermic.

Treatment escalation

Inadequate response — escalate
Redose at same/higher dose (≥20 min for antipsychotics, ≥10 min for benzodiazepines). Switch routes — IV if IM not effective. Add a second class — combination (antipsychotic + benzodiazepine) is superior to monotherapy; if olanzapine was used, switch to haloperidol first, THEN add a benzodiazepine. Ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV or 4 mg/kg IM for refractory agitation — prepare for intubation; avoid in elderly, heart disease, or schizophrenia. If cumulative haloperidol >20 mg or midazolam >15 mg, reconsider the diagnosis — is there an untreated underlying cause?

Delirium is an independent risk factor for morbidity and mortality
