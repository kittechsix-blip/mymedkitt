Shoulder Dislocation: Assess, Reduce, Immobilize, Dispo

Adults and adolescents in the ED with a suspected or confirmed anterior, posterior, or inferior (luxatio erecta) glenohumeral dislocation. Excludes open dislocations and pre-hospital reduction.

⚠ MANDATORY pre-reduction neurovascular exam — document axillary nerve sensation (regimental badge area), radial pulse, and capillary refill before attempting any reduction technique. Neurovascular deficits occur in 5-35% of anterior dislocations and must be established as pre-existing vs. iatrogenic.
⚠ Vascular compromise (expanding hematoma, absent pulse, bruit) — do NOT attempt reduction. Emergent vascular surgery consult; may require OR reduction with vascular repair.
⚠ Greater tuberosity fracture and humeral neck fracture-dislocation: single reduction attempt only — call orthopedics early for failed attempts, multi-part fractures, or surgical-neck involvement.
⚠ Avoid the Hippocratic (foot-in-axilla) and Kocher (forceful lever-arm torque) reduction techniques — both carry high neurovascular injury and fracture risk.

1 · Initial assessment

Shoulder dislocation is the most common large-joint dislocation seen in the ED. Anterior: 95-97% (abduction + external rotation + extension; arm slightly abducted, externally rotated, loss of deltoid contour). Posterior: 2-4% — easily missed (seizure, electrocution, direct anterior force; arm adducted, internally rotated). Inferior (luxatio erecta): <1% — arm locked overhead, high neurovascular injury association.

MANDATORY pre-reduction neurovascular exam — axillary nerve is the most commonly injured (5-35%): test "regimental badge" sensation over the lateral deltoid. Also check musculocutaneous sensation (lateral forearm), radial pulse vs. unaffected side, and capillary refill. Document ANY deficit before reduction — it must be established as pre-existing vs. iatrogenic.

First-time dislocation carries higher risk of associated injury (Bankart, Hill-Sachs, GT fracture) and higher recurrence risk in young patients — pre-reduction imaging matters more. Recurrent dislocation often needs less analgesia and may reduce easily with a known mechanism — but red flags (new neurovascular deficit, unusual limb position, significantly more pain than prior episodes) route back through full imaging workup just like a first-time or uncertain-history presentation.

FARES — steps89-95% success, VAS 1.6
  1. Patient supine, arm at side; grasp wrist with both hands
  2. Apply gentle longitudinal traction along the axis of the arm
  3. Begin vertical oscillations (±5 cm up/down) at 2-3 cycles/second
  4. Slowly abduct the arm while maintaining traction and oscillations
  5. At ~90° abduction, add external rotation
  6. Continue oscillations until reduction (~120° abduction) — average 2-3 minutes
Scapular manipulation — steps79-97% success, 65% <1 min
  1. Patient prone, affected arm hanging off the stretcher edge (or seated variant)
  2. Apply 5-10 lbs downward traction on the arm
  3. Push the inferior tip of the scapula medially (toward spine)
  4. Simultaneously push the superior scapula laterally
  5. This rotates the glenoid to meet the humeral head
External rotation — steps78-90% success
  1. Flex elbow to 90°, arm adducted at side
  2. Slowly externally rotate the forearm at ~1°/second — slow is key
  3. Continue to maximum external rotation (~90°)
  4. If not reduced, abduct the arm while maintaining external rotation, then internally rotate
Contraindicated with an uncooperative patient or a surgical neck fracture.
Cunningham — steps35-77% success, no force
  1. Patient seated upright, relaxes shoulders back
  2. Flex elbow to 90°, support wrist on examiner's forearm
  3. Patient shrugs shoulders then relaxes them down
  4. Massage biceps with firm strokes from mid-arm to elbow
  5. Massage trapezius and deltoid similarly — humeral head self-reduces via relaxation
Stimson (prone) — steps28-97% success, no assistant needed
  1. Patient prone, affected arm hanging freely off the stretcher
  2. Apply 5-10 lbs weight to the wrist (or gentle manual traction)
  3. Wait 15-30 minutes for muscle fatigue and relaxation
  4. May gently externally rotate the arm if needed
Traction-countertraction — steps~80% success, backup method
  1. Wrap a sheet around the patient's chest, under the axilla on the affected side, for countertraction
  2. Operator applies inline traction on the arm (slight abduction)
  3. Gradually externally rotate while maintaining traction
  4. May add gentle lateral traction on the proximal humerus
Usually requires procedural sedation. AVOID Hippocratic (foot in axilla) and Kocher (forceful lever-arm torque) — neurovascular injury and fracture risk.

7 · Post-reduction & immobilization

  1. Palpate deltoid contour — should be restored; assess ROM (improved); patient reports relief
  2. Repeat neurovascular exam — axillary nerve sensation, radial pulse — compare to pre-reduction and document any change
  3. Post-reduction radiographs — confirm anatomic reduction, assess for iatrogenic fracture, identify Hill-Sachs/Bankart
  4. Sling and swathe initially; decide internal vs. external rotation positioning

2016 meta-analysis: no overall significant difference between internal (sling) and external rotation immobilization. External rotation brace shows benefit mainly in patients ≤30 years (better Bankart coaptation on MRI). Duration is 3-6 weeks regardless of position.

Best candidates: age 20-30, first-time dislocation, Bankart lesion, GT fracture, motivated/compliant patient. 46% relative risk reduction in recurrence (age ≤30). Prescription: 10-15° ER, worn 24/7 except bathing, duration 3-4 weeks, followed by physical therapy.
Arm at side, elbow flexed 90°, swathe optional for first few days. Age <40: 3 weeks; age >40: 1-2 weeks (prioritize early ROM to prevent stiffness). Remove sling 3x daily for elbow/wrist ROM; pendulum exercises after 1 week; ice for pain/swelling; no lifting, pushing, or pulling with the affected arm.

8 · Disposition

Urgent orthopedic consult (consider admission): failed reduction, neurovascular compromise, open dislocation, fracture-dislocation requiring surgery, bilateral dislocations. Outpatient follow-up: successful reduction, all first-time dislocations, recurrent dislocations (surgical evaluation), associated fractures.

Best candidates: age 20-30, first-time dislocation, Bankart lesion, GT fracture, motivated/compliant patient. 46% relative risk reduction in recurrence (age ≤30). Prescription: 10-15° ER, worn 24/7 except bathing, duration 3-4 weeks, followed by physical therapy.

Arm at side, elbow flexed 90°, swathe optional for first few days. Age <40: 3 weeks; age >40: 1-2 weeks (prioritize early ROM to prevent stiffness). Remove sling 3x daily for elbow/wrist ROM; pendulum exercises after 1 week; ice for pain/swelling; no lifting, pushing, or pulling with the affected arm.

8 · Disposition

Urgent orthopedic consult (consider admission): failed reduction, neurovascular compromise, open dislocation, fracture-dislocation requiring surgery, bilateral dislocations. Outpatient follow-up: successful reduction, all first-time dislocations, recurrent dislocations (surgical evaluation), associated fractures.

Ibuprofen: 600-800mg TID with food — As stated in the shoulder-discharge node. Consider a short opioid course for severe pain.

Cyclobenzaprine: 10mg PO TID (optional) — Muscle relaxant option for muscle spasm.

  • Sling immobilization as prescribed; no lifting >5 lbs with affected arm; no overhead activities; no driving until out of sling
  • Return precautions: worsening numbness/tingling, new weakness, uncontrolled pain, signs of re-dislocation
  • Orthopedics follow-up within 1-2 weeks; physical therapy referral; first-time young patients — discuss surgical options

15-20 years | 86.6%

21-25 years | 73.8%

>40 years | 10-15%

Additional risk factors: male sex (OR 1.92), contact/collision sports, hyperlaxity, manual occupation. Age <25: surgery significantly reduces re-dislocation (RR 0.14-0.15). ISIS score ≥4: augmented repair (Latarjet) may be needed.

Reasons for failure: soft tissue interposition (capsule, labrum, biceps tendon), associated fracture blocking reduction, locked (chronic) dislocation, inadequate muscle relaxation. Next steps: deeper sedation/general anesthesia, OR reduction with fluoroscopy, arthroscopic or open reduction. While awaiting OR: sling, pain control, ice, neurovascular monitoring, NPO.

Vascular injury (rare but catastrophic): expanding hematoma, absent pulse, bruit — immediate vascular surgery consult, CTA if stable, OR if unstable. Do NOT attempt reduction. Nerve injury (5-35%): axillary most common, most are neurapraxia resolving in weeks to months; EMG at 3-4 weeks if no improvement; no improvement at 3 months — consider exploration.

In-season quick return possible in 2-3 weeks with protective bracing — recurrence risk 37-90% if returning to contact sports. Return-to-play criteria: pain-free, symmetric shoulder strength, bilateral scapular strength, functional ROM for sport-specific activities. First-time dislocation in a young athlete: discuss early surgery. Post-Latarjet: 88% return to sport, 72.6% at prior level.

Rotator cuff tear risk: age >60 70%, age >80 80%. Red flags: persistent weakness, inability to actively abduct, positive drop arm test, pain out of proportion. MRI preferred (ultrasound if skilled operator). Early surgical repair (within 4 weeks) — 84% excellent/good results vs 50% non-operative. Shorter immobilization (1-2 weeks) with early ROM.

Other pitfalls

Do NOT skip the pre-reduction neurovascular exam — deficits are common (5-35%) and must be documented before any reduction attempt to distinguish pre-existing from iatrogenic injury.

Do NOT use the Hippocratic (foot-in-axilla) or Kocher (forceful lever-arm torque) techniques — both carry high neurovascular injury and fracture risk.

Do NOT make more than one reduction attempt on a greater tuberosity or humeral neck fracture-dislocation without calling orthopedics.

Do NOT attempt reduction with vascular compromise present (expanding hematoma, absent pulse, bruit) — this requires emergent vascular surgery, not a reduction attempt.
