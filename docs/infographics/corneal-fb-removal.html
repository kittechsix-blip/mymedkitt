1 · Pre-Procedure Screen — Rule Out Open Globe First

Scope of this consult: removal of a superficial corneal or conjunctival foreign body in the ED. Excludes intraocular FB (penetrating), open globe, and central visual-axis material requiring ophthalmology.

Mechanism risk-stratification:
• Low velocity / blunt (dust, debris under lid) — usually superficial
• High velocity / metal-on-metal (grinder without eye protection, hammering metal) — assume intraocular FB until proven otherwise; CT orbit usually indicated
• Vegetative material (plant, wood) — higher fungal infection risk, lower threshold for ophthalmology referral

Seidel test: apply a fluorescein strip, examine at slit lamp with cobalt blue. Aqueous leaking from a perforation dilutes the orange fluorescein into a bright green stream (the "Seidel positive" waterfall).

Mechanism-based imaging: high-velocity metal-on-metal or any suspicion of intraocular FB → CT orbit (thin cuts) before any manipulation. Avoid MRI if metal FB is possible.

Also screen for: hyphema, irregular pupil, severe pain out of proportion, decreased VA, blood in the anterior chamber.

3 · Eyelid Eversion — Exclude Tarsal FB

Always evert the upper lid even if the FB looks corneal. A tarsal FB is invisible until you flip the lid, and it keeps scratching the cornea with every blink.

  1. Anesthetize the eye
  2. Ask the patient to look down (NOT close)
  3. Grasp the upper lashes; place a cotton-tip applicator horizontally across the upper lid crease
  4. Roll the lid up and over the cotton-tip — exposes the tarsal conjunctiva
  5. Wipe any FB off the tarsal surface with a moistened cotton-tip
  6. Release; the lid returns to position naturally
  7. Also check the lower fornix — pull down lower lid, ask patient to look up

4 · Removal Ladder — Start Gentle

Escalate from least to most invasive. Most superficial FB come off with irrigation or a cotton-tip; needle removal at the slit lamp is reserved for embedded particles.

Indication: loose surface FB, conjunctival sac debris, dust, eyelash, particulate material.

  1. Patient supine, head turned to affected side, towel under cheek
  2. 20-50 mL normal saline via syringe with no needle, or IV tubing + bag held high
  3. Direct stream medial to lateral canthus, flushing across the surface and into the fornices
  4. Have patient look in all directions to expose fornices
  5. Examine again at slit lamp — if FB still present, advance to cotton-tip or needle

Indication: FB sitting on the cornea surface, not embedded — typically loose dust, fiber, or small particles.

  1. Moisten cotton-tip applicator with sterile saline (dry cotton can scrape epithelium)
  2. At slit lamp with patient stabilized
  3. Gentle dabbing motion — never rub. Touch the FB only, lift it off
  4. If it sticks to the cotton, dispose of it; if it stays on the cornea, FB is embedded → move to needle technique
Pitfall
Dragging a cotton-tip across the cornea creates a large abrasion. Always dab, do not drag.

Indication: embedded corneal FB, small, peripheral (NOT central visual axis).

  1. Patient seated at slit lamp, both eyes open (uninvolved eye fixates on target)
  2. Anesthetize affected eye
  3. 25 or 27 gauge needle on a tuberculin syringe (the syringe gives you a handle; the needle does the work)
  4. Approach tangentially — bevel parallel to the cornea, NOT pointing at the cornea
  5. Brace your hand on the slit lamp — any patient movement plus a perpendicular needle = corneal perforation
  6. Sweeping motion, not stabbing
  7. Remove the FB in one or two attempts
Pitfall
If the FB is central (over the pupil), an inexperienced operator should refer rather than risk a scar in the visual axis.

Metallic FB (most often steel from grinding) oxidizes within hours and leaves a rust ring even after the FB itself is removed. The rust is corneal injury and must come out — but timing matters.

  • Same-day removal with ophthalmic burr (Algerbrush) — preferred when available. Light touch under slit lamp, mechanical curettage of softened rust. Stop before reaching Bowman's layer.
  • Defer 24-48h to ophthalmology — many EDs do this; the rust ring softens with time and is easier to remove. Patient on prophylactic antibiotics in the meantime.
  • Leave a tiny residual ring if peripheral and small — it often migrates to the surface and sloughs off; ophthalmology will recheck.
Do not chase the rust
Do not chase the rust into the stroma. Stop when the surface is clean even if a faint stain remains. Ophthalmology will finish the job at follow-up.

  • Topical antibiotic per post-care guide — Pseudomonas coverage for contact lens users

  • Oral analgesia
