Methemoglobinemia — Recognition & Treatment

Adults and children in the ED/ICU with suspected or confirmed acquired methemoglobinemia (drug/toxin-induced). Excludes congenital methemoglobinemia (NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency, HbM disease) and sulfhemoglobinemia, which mimics MetHb but does not respond to methylene blue. Infant and pregnancy considerations noted separately.

⚠ Methylene blue CONTRAINDICATED in G6PD deficiency — acts as an oxidant and causes severe hemolysis (FDA 2024 warning); use ascorbic acid IV or exchange transfusion instead.
⚠ MetHb >40%: seizures, coma, arrhythmias, cardiovascular collapse — give methylene blue empirically on high clinical suspicion; do NOT wait for co-oximetry confirmation.
⚠ Methylene blue maximum total dose 7 mg/kg — higher doses can paradoxically CAUSE methemoglobinemia.
⚠ Standard pulse oximetry and calculated ABG/VBG O2 saturation CANNOT detect methemoglobinemia — co-oximetry (venous or arterial) is required.
⚠ Treat if MetHb >30% or symptomatic at any level; do not delay treatment for a G6PD result in a critically ill patient.
⚠ Dapsone and other long-half-life agents cause rebound MetHb — minimum 24-48h monitoring; do not discharge same day.

Recognition

Methemoglobinemia occurs when hemoglobin iron is oxidized from ferrous (Fe2+) to ferric (Fe3+), which cannot bind oxygen. Normal MetHb is <2% in non-smokers (up to 3-4% in smokers). Classic presentation: cyanosis unresponsive to supplemental O2, SpO2 reads 82-86% despite high FiO2, and chocolate-brown blood that does not turn red on air exposure.

MetHb levelPresentation
<3%Normal
3-15%Asymptomatic to mild cyanosis
15-30%Fatigue, headache, dyspnea, tachycardia
30-50%Confusion, syncope, chest pain, severe dyspnea
50-70%Arrhythmias, seizures, coma
>70%Death

MetHb level does not always correlate with symptoms — patients with baseline anemia or cardiopulmonary disease tolerate MetHb poorly. Functional Hb = Total Hb x (1 - MetHb%) determines true oxygen-carrying capacity.

—Functional Hb

Etiology & diagnosis

  • Benzocaine (most common single cause — Hurricaine spray, Cetacaine)
  • Dapsone (most frequent overall in retrospective studies)
  • Nitrates/nitrites — nitroglycerin, sodium nitrite, well-water contamination
  • Phenazopyridine, metoclopramide, aniline dyes, naphthalene (mothballs)
Co-oximetry required
Standard pulse oximetry and calculated ABG/VBG O2 saturation CANNOT detect methemoglobinemia — creates a classic "saturation gap" (normal PaO2 + low SpO2). Co-oximetry (venous or arterial — both accurate) is the gold standard.

▶ What to do now

G6PD normal / unknown → methylene blue
Proceed with methylene blue 1-2 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes (see dosing below). Repeat 1 mg/kg in 30-60 minutes if inadequate response; max total 7 mg/kg.
Known G6PD deficiency → methylene blue CONTRAINDICATED
Do NOT give methylene blue — it acts as an oxidant and causes hemolysis in G6PD deficiency. Use ascorbic acid IV or exchange transfusion instead.
Uncertain G6PD status, high-risk ethnicity
Favor a trial of methylene blue only if severe/life-threatening; otherwise favor ascorbic acid or exchange transfusion. Consult Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222.

G6PD deficiency & alternative therapies

G6PD deficiency — methylene blue contraindicated
Methylene blue requires NADPH (produced via G6PD) to reduce MetHb. In G6PD deficiency it becomes an oxidant instead. FDA (2024): treatment with methylene blue may result in severe hemolysis and severe anemia in G6PD-deficient patients.

Goldfrank's Toxicology: "Judicious use of methylene blue is recommended in most patients with G6PD deficiency and symptomatic methemoglobinemia." Favor a trial of methylene blue if severe/life-threatening (MetHb >50%, cardiovascular collapse); favor alternatives if moderate with stable vitals. Consult Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222.

Ascorbic Acid (Vitamin C)
1.5-3g IV q6h (up to 10g/dose reported)

Slow onset (hours-days); safe in G6PD deficiency. Tree-stated adult range; drug-store lists a narrower 1.5-2g IV q6h for the same indication (see source notes / conflicts).

Exchange transfusion

Apheresis / manual exchange
Definitive therapy — effective regardless of G6PD status. Indicated: MetHb >70%, refractory to medications, or G6PD + severe MetHb.

Hyperbaric oxygen increases dissolved plasma O2 while arranging definitive therapy — it does not directly reduce MetHb and is slow-acting (hours). Limited availability.

Special populations

Infants <6 months are high risk: NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase activity is only 50% of adult levels, fetal hemoglobin oxidizes more easily, and higher gastric pH allows bacterial nitrate-to-nitrite conversion. Common causes: well water contaminated with nitrates, homemade vegetable purees, diarrheal illness. Same methylene blue dosing applies: 1-2 mg/kg IV.

Pregnancy: fetal hemoglobin is more susceptible to oxidation; fetal MetHb may exceed maternal levels. IBCC: "In life-threatening acquired methemoglobinemia, methylene blue should probably be used" — IV administration risk is less defined than the (contraindicated) intra-amniotic route, and theoretical risks are likely outweighed by maternal/fetal death from severe MetHb. Obtain OB consultation for fetal monitoring.

Disposition

ICU admission if MetHb >30% at presentation, methylene blue was required, severe symptoms occurred, rebound risk (dapsone), G6PD deficiency with MetHb, or hemodynamic instability. Observation if mild-moderate and responding. Discharge only if asymptomatic, MetHb <5% and trending down, causative agent removed, no rebound-risk agent, and reliable follow-up.

  • Continuous cardiac monitoring
  • Serial co-oximetry q2-4h initially, then q4-6h
  • Hemolysis markers: LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count
  • Dapsone: minimum 24-48 hour observation with MDAC + cimetidine

Serial co-oximetry q4h until MetHb <5%, telemetry, symptom checks. Escalate to ICU if MetHb rises, new symptoms develop, or hemolysis is evident.

Safe for discharge only if asymptomatic, MetHb <5% on most recent lab and trending down, causative agent identified and removed, no rebound-risk agent (avoid same-day discharge after dapsone), and reliable follow-up within 3-5 days.

Management algorithm

Methemoglobinemia — assess severity — Cyanosis unresponsive to O2 + SpO2 plateau 82-86% + chocolate-brown blood that stays brown on air exposure. Normal MetHb <2% (non-smoker).

Mild (10-20%): cyanosis, headache, fatigue — Cyanosis not improving with O2; SpO2 falsely reads 82-86%. Brown-blood test: blood stays chocolate brown on gauze.

Moderate (20-40%): confusion, dyspnea, tachycardia — Central cyanosis, tachycardia, tachypnea. Calculate functional Hb — level doesn't always correlate with symptoms.

Severe (>40%): seizures, coma, arrhythmias — treat empirically — High-flow O2, IV access, cardiac monitoring, co-oximetry STAT. Give methylene blue empirically on high suspicion — do NOT wait for lab confirmation.

Asymptomatic / incidental elevated MetHb — Confirm no subtle symptoms; remove cause; high-flow O2; serial MetHb q2-4h. Treat if >30% (strongly consider) or 20-30% (consider, especially with comorbidities).

Identify & remove causative agent — Benzocaine (most common single agent), dapsone (most frequent overall), nitrates/nitrites, phenazopyridine, metoclopramide, aniline dyes, naphthalene.

Co-oximetry (venous or arterial) — gold standard — Standard pulse ox and calculated ABG/VBG O2 sat CANNOT detect MetHb — 'saturation gap' (normal PaO2 + low SpO2). Bedside brown-blood test on gauze.

Treat? — check G6PD status (do not delay) — Definite: MetHb >30%, symptomatic at any level, tissue hypoxia. Check G6PD if possible but do NOT delay treatment in critically ill patients.

G6PD status unknown, high-risk ethnicity — Goldfrank's: judicious MB use recommended in most G6PD-deficient patients with symptomatic MetHb. Favor MB if life-threatening; favor alternatives if stable.

Ascorbic acid IV or exchange transfusion — Ascorbic acid 1.5-3g IV q6h — slow onset (hours-days), safe in G6PD deficiency. Exchange transfusion — definitive, works regardless of G6PD status.
