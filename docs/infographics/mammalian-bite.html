Mammalian Bite — ED Evaluation and Management

ED evaluation and management of dog, cat, human, and other mammalian bites — animal-specific risk assessment, wound assessment and care, antibiotic prophylaxis/treatment, rabies risk assessment and PEP, tetanus prophylaxis, and disposition.

⚠ ALL cat bites should receive prophylactic antibiotics — deep puncture wounds cannot be adequately irrigated and infection rates run 30-50%.
⚠ A human 'fight bite' (tooth striking a clenched fist over the MCP joint) is a surgical emergency — examine in extension AND flexion; high rate of septic arthritis and osteomyelitis.
⚠ Give rabies PEP for any bat bite/scratch, or when a bat is found with a sleeping, unattended, or impaired person and contact cannot be excluded — ~6% of tested bats are rabid and rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic.
⚠ Primate (monkey) bites carry Herpes B virus risk — 70-80% mortality if untreated. ID consult immediately; prophylaxis must start within hours.
⚠ Rabies Immune Globulin (RIG) is given with the FIRST vaccine dose ONLY — never after Day 7 of the vaccine series.
⚠ All bite wounds are considered tetanus-prone.

Initial assessment

Assess animal type, time since bite, wound location/depth, tetanus and rabies risk, and whether the patient is immunocompromised.

  • Cat bites (deep punctures, high infection rate)
  • Human bites (hand "fight bites")
  • Crush injuries
  • Delayed presentation (>8-12 hours)
  • Immunocompromise
  • Involvement of hand, face, joint, or tendon

Animal-specific risk

Dog bites are the most common mammalian bite; roughly 1 in 5 becomes infected. Typical pathogens: Pasteurella spp., Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, anaerobes, and Capnocytophaga canimorsus (a particular risk in asplenic patients). Domestic vaccinated dogs carry low rabies risk — a known dog can often be observed for 10 days rather than treated empirically.

All cat bites need antibiotics
Cat teeth cause deep puncture wounds that cannot be adequately irrigated, inoculating bacteria deep into tissue — infection rates run 30-50% (vs 5-20% for dogs). Pasteurella multocida (75%) causes rapid-onset cellulitis (<24h). All cat bites should receive prophylactic antibiotics.
Fight bite = surgical emergency
A fight bite (tooth striking a clenched fist over the MCP joint) can penetrate the joint capsule and carries a high rate of septic arthritis and osteomyelitis. Examine in BOTH extension and flexion — patients often delay presentation or minimize the mechanism. Unique pathogen: Eikenella corrodens. Consider bloodborne pathogen exposure risk (Hep B, Hep C, HIV).

Rodents carry very low infection and rabies risk. Bats, wild carnivores, and primates carry high risk and route to dedicated pathways.

Bat exposure — treat empirically
Give rabies PEP for any bat bite or scratch, or when a bat is found in a room with a sleeping, unattended, or impaired person and contact cannot be excluded. Bat bites can go unnoticed; about 6% of tested bats are rabid, and rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic. If the bat is available, submit it for testing — discontinue PEP if it tests negative.
Herpes B — start prophylaxis within hours
Macaque monkey bites/scratches carry Herpes B virus (Cercopithecine herpesvirus 1) risk — about 70% of adult macaques are seropositive, and untreated infection is 70-80% fatal. Irrigate copiously, scrub with povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine, obtain an immediate ID consult, and consider prophylactic valacyclovir or acyclovir. This is a medical emergency — prophylaxis must start within hours.

Wound assessment & care

Assess location (hand = highest infection risk; face; over a joint; near a tendon), wound type (laceration vs puncture vs crush vs avulsion), depth, and time since injury (>12-24 hours raises infection risk).

  1. Copious high-pressure irrigation (≥150-200 mL normal saline or tap water) — the single most important step for infection prevention
  2. Debride devitalized tissue and remove foreign material
  3. Close face wounds primarily; leave hand wounds, puncture wounds, wounds >12-24h old, and infected wounds open
  4. Splint and elevate hand wounds
  5. Give tetanus prophylaxis (see below)
Puncture wounds — do not force irrigation
Do not probe or forcefully irrigate a puncture wound. Express blood/exudate, soak in antiseptic, leave the wound open, and start prophylactic antibiotics. Recheck in 24-48 hours; have a low threshold for admission if the hand is involved.
Hand bites — high risk
Obtain an x-ray, examine in extension AND flexion for fight bites, test tendon function, and assess for joint involvement. Debride and leave open, splint in position of function, elevate, and start antibiotics in all cases. Consult hand surgery for possible joint penetration, tendon involvement, or significant tissue loss.
Already infected
Culture the wound (aerobic + anaerobic), obtain blood cultures if systemic signs are present, and x-ray for osteomyelitis/foreign body/gas. Drain any abscess, debride necrotic tissue, and leave the wound open. Admit for sepsis, rapidly progressive cellulitis, hand involvement, deep space infection, immunocompromise, or failed outpatient treatment.

Antibiotics — prophylaxis vs treatment

Prophylaxis is indicated for all cat and human bites, dog bites to the hand/face/genital area, puncture wounds, wounds over a joint/tendon/bone, crush injuries, presentation >8-12 hours, and immunocompromised or asplenic patients. It is not routinely needed for minor, easily-irrigated dog bite lacerations <8 hours old.

Rabies risk & PEP

High-risk animals (bats, raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes, stray/unknown dogs or cats) warrant PEP. Low-risk animals (vaccinated domestic pets, rabbits, rodents, livestock) usually do not. A healthy domestic dog, cat, or ferret that can be observed for 10 days is an alternative to immediate treatment.

  1. Immediate, thorough wound irrigation
  2. Rabies Immune Globulin (RIG) — infiltrate into the wound, remainder IM at a distant site; Day 0 ONLY, never after Day 7
  3. Rabies vaccine — Days 0, 3, 7, 14 (add Day 28 if immunocompromised); IM deltoid, never gluteal
  4. Previously vaccinated patients: vaccine on Days 0 and 3 only, no RIG needed
  5. Contact the local/state health department
