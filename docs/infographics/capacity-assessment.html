Capacity Assessment: Four Abilities, Decision-Specific

Adults in the ED being assessed for decision-making capacity — refusing recommended treatment, leaving against medical advice, consenting to a procedure, or an end-of-life decision. A bedside clinical framework, not a substitute for a formal psychiatric or legal competency evaluation. Excludes minors (parent/guardian consent framework differs) and court competency determinations.

⚠ Capacity is decision-specific — a patient may have capacity for one decision but not another; assess for THIS decision, not globally.
⚠ Capacity != competency — capacity is a clinical determination made by the physician; competency is a legal determination made by a court. A diagnosis alone (dementia, psychosis, intoxication) does not determine capacity — patients are presumed to have capacity until proven otherwise.
⚠ Capacity requires all four abilities: understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and communication of a stable choice — for THIS decision.
⚠ If the patient lacks capacity, they cannot refuse treatment, cannot leave AMA, and cannot give informed consent — proceed via a surrogate decision-maker, or treat under implied consent in a true emergency.
⚠ Document the specific decision, the process, and your reasoning — every capacity determination must be charted, not just the conclusion.

1 · Fundamentals

Capacity is decision-specific — a patient may have capacity for one decision but not another. A patient with mild dementia may have capacity to refuse a blood draw but not to refuse life-saving surgery.

  • Refusing recommended treatment
  • Leaving against medical advice (AMA)
  • Consenting to a high-risk procedure
  • Signing a DNR/POLST
  • Psychotic, intoxicated, or delirious presentation

Presume capacity
Patients are presumed to have capacity until proven otherwise. Diagnosis alone (dementia, psychosis, intoxication) does not determine capacity. A patient who makes a "bad" decision may still have capacity. Capacity can fluctuate — reassess if the patient's condition changes. When in doubt, treat the emergency first (implied consent).

2 · Four-abilities framework

Capacity uses four abilities: understanding, appreciation, reasoning, and communication of a stable choice.

AbilityWhat it testsSample question
UnderstandingDescribe condition, treatment, risks/benefits, alternatives in own words"Can you tell me what we are worried about and what treatment we recommend?"
AppreciationApply the information to their own situation as personally relevant"What do you think will happen to you if you leave or decline treatment?"

Lack of capacity if

3 · What to do now

Set why capacity is being questioned above, and once assessed, set the determination — the matching guidance below updates and the algorithm path highlights.

Refusing treatment
Higher stakes = higher bar for capacity. If has capacity: respect the decision, document thoroughly, ensure informed refusal, offer alternatives/follow-up. If lacks capacity: cannot refuse — proceed with a surrogate, or treat under implied consent if it is an emergency.
Leaving AMA
Capacity assessment is REQUIRED before AMA. Intoxication raises concern but does not automatically remove capacity — assess clinically and reassess when it can safely wait. Delirious patients generally cannot leave AMA until delirium is addressed. If has capacity: they can leave, even if unsafe — document capacity, risks explained, alternatives offered; AMA does NOT mean punishment. If lacks capacity: cannot leave AMA — consider 1:1 sitter, security, medical hold, or psych evaluation.
Consenting to a procedure
Valid consent requires Disclosure, capacity, and voluntariness. Minors need parent/guardian consent (except emancipated/emergencies). Non-English speakers need a professional interpreter. Delay elective procedures in intoxicated patients until the four abilities can be clinically demonstrated; emergencies get implied consent.
End-of-life decision
Higher bar for irreversible decisions. Ensure the patient fully understands the implications, check consistency with their values, and rule out depression affecting the decision. If lacks capacity: use the advance directive, healthcare proxy/POA, substituted judgment, or best-interest standard. Ethics consult for complex or conflicted cases.
Patient has capacity
Respect the autonomous choice. Ensure an informed decision (risks/benefits/alternatives discussed) and document the assessment thoroughly — even if you disagree with the decision.
Patient lacks capacity
Identify a surrogate decision-maker (Healthcare proxy / POA first, then spouse, adult children, parents, siblings, other relatives — hierarchy varies by state). Surrogate uses substituted judgment ("What would patient want?") or best interest if wishes are unknown. Emergency with no surrogate → treat under implied consent.
Unclear / borderline
Options: more time (reassess when sober/rested/less stressed), psychiatry consult (formal evaluation, may use the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool), ethics consult, or court involvement for urgent guardianship. Meanwhile: treat emergencies, presume capacity for low-stakes decisions.

Surrogate standards (hierarchy varies by state)
Surrogate uses substituted judgment ("What would patient want?") or best interest if wishes are unknown. If emergency and no surrogate available: treat under implied consent and document that delay would harm the patient. If surrogate unavailable or disagrees: ethics consult or court-appointed guardian (takes time).
  • More time: repeat assessment when sober, rested, less stressed — intoxication clears, delirium may improve with treatment
  • Psychiatry consult: formal evaluation, especially with underlying psychiatric illness; may use the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool (MacCAT)
  • Ethics consult: mediate complex situations, help when family disagrees, clarify ethical frameworks
  • Court involvement: emergency guardianship if urgent, competency hearing for long-term incapacity

6 · Documentation

Document the specific decision, the process, and your reasoning — every capacity determination must be charted, not just the conclusion.

  1. The specific decision being assessed
  2. Clinical context — why capacity was questioned
  3. Mental status exam findings
  4. Assessment of each ability: understanding, appreciation, reasoning, communication (with the patient's actual responses)
  5. Your conclusion and reasoning
  6. What information was provided to the patient
  7. Signature, date, time
Example phrasing
"In my clinical judgment, this patient [has/lacks] capacity to [specific decision] because [reasoning]."

Other pitfalls4 items
Do NOT assume a diagnosis alone (dementia, psychosis, intoxication) removes capacity — assess functionally, for this specific decision, every time.
Do NOT let a risky or "bad" decision override an intact capacity assessment — respect autonomy even when you disagree.
Do NOT delay emergency treatment to complete a full four-abilities assessment — treat under implied consent, then reassess when safe.
