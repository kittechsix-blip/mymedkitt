Sedation Options — ED Sedative Selection

Adults ≥18 in the ED and ICU. Comparative sedative/analgesic reference across procedural sedation, agitation control, post-intubation/ICU sedation, and withdrawal/seizure-risk pathways, plus special-population modifiers (older/frail, severe acidosis/shock, QT risk, OSA/COPD/hypoventilation/intoxication, pediatric/pregnancy). Excludes detailed pediatric weight-based dosing tables and does not replace institution-specific procedural-sedation policy or local anesthesia protocols.

⚠ Procedural sedation: have a dedicated monitor (not the proceduralist), suction, oxygen, BVM, airway adjuncts, and rescue medications ready BEFORE giving any sedative — deeper sedation needs continuous SpO2, cycling BP, ECG, and capnography when available.
⚠ Propofol causes apnea/airway obstruction and dose-dependent hypotension within seconds and provides NO analgesia — avoid or reduce dose heavily in shock, severe hypovolemia, tenuous preload, or severe RV failure.
⚠ Ketamine can cause hypersalivation, vomiting, and rare laryngospasm — reassess airway after every dose; avoid in severe uncontrolled hypertension, aortic dissection, active myocardial ischemia, severe pulmonary hypertension/RV strain, or psychosis where emergence is unacceptable.
⚠ Benzodiazepines and opioids together cause SYNERGISTIC respiratory depression — wait and reassess ventilation before redosing, especially in older/frail, OSA/COPD, intoxicated, or recent-opioid patients.
⚠ Agitation sedation must match physiology, not the psychiatric label: immediate threat needs the fastest reliable agent; stimulant/withdrawal/seizure/hyperthermia needs benzodiazepine-first therapy — do not let the label drive the drug.
⚠ Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal requires seizure-preventing GABAergic therapy (benzodiazepine or phenobarbital) — dexmedetomidine is an ADJUNCT ONLY for autonomic symptoms and does NOT prevent withdrawal seizures.
⚠ Phenobarbital has a very long duration (half-life about 3-4 days) with NO rapid reversal — do not casually stack with large benzodiazepine/opioid loads without an airway/ICU plan.
⚠ Older/frail patients need 25-50% lower starting doses and slower redosing by observed effect (not by clock) — avoid stacking opioids, benzodiazepines, phenobarbital, alcohol, and sleep medications.
⚠ Severe acidosis/shock: the danger is loss of compensatory ventilation, not just the sedative — preoxygenate/resuscitate first, reduce induction/sedative doses, and match minute ventilation immediately after airway control.
⚠ OSA/COPD/hypoventilation/intoxication: use the lowest effective sedation depth, avoid rapid drug stacking, use capnography when available, and have BVM/airway adjuncts at bedside before the first dose.

Choose by target, not habit

Choose the sedative by the clinical target, not by habit. Analgesia, anxiolysis, amnesia, dissociation, deep hypnosis, cooperative sedation, and withdrawal control are different goals — the fastest drug is not always the safest drug.

Pick the clinical target first: short painful procedure, dangerous agitation/aggression, post-intubation/ICU sedation, withdrawal/seizure-risk, or fragile physiology (older/frail, severe acidosis/shock, QT risk, OSA/COPD/hypoventilation, pediatric/pregnancy).

  • Dedicated monitor, not the procedure operator
  • Suction, oxygen, BVM, airway adjuncts, and rescue medications ready
  • Continuous SpO2, BP cycling, ECG for deeper sedation or risk, and capnography when available
  • Painful procedures need analgesia unless using dissociative ketamine

▶ What to do now

Short painful procedure — pick by profile
Brief deep sedation, fast off → Propofol. Dissociation + analgesia (peds, asthma, need preserved respiratory tone) → Ketamine. Hemodynamic-neutral brief hypnosis → Etomidate. Anxiolysis + analgesia only (lower depth) → Midazolam + Fentanyl. Brief cooperative procedure, fast recovery → nitrous oxide.
Agitation — match the drug to physiology, not the label
Immediate violent threat → Ketamine. Stimulant, withdrawal, seizure, hyperthermia → Midazolam (benzodiazepine-first). Undifferentiated severe agitation → Droperidol + midazolam. Primary psychosis, not crashing → Haloperidol or Olanzapine. QT/Parkinson/elderly/respiratory-risk → route to the special-population branch first.
ICU / post-intubation — analgesia-first, RASS-targeted
Deep sedation or seizure suppression → Propofol. Light cooperative sedation (extubation bridge, NIV tolerance) → Dexmedetomidine. Analgesia + bronchodilation / less hypotension → Ketamine. Withdrawal or refractory seizure physiology → Phenobarbital. Propofol/dexmedetomidine are generally preferred over benzodiazepine infusions when feasible.
Withdrawal / seizure-risk — GABAergic therapy is mandatory
Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal → Phenobarbital or a benzodiazepine strategy. Active seizure or status epilepticus → Midazolam / benzodiazepines first. Autonomic agitation despite GABA therapy → Dexmedetomidine as an ADJUNCT ONLY — it does not treat seizure risk.
Fragile physiology — let the constraint choose the drug
Pick the dominant constraint: older/frail (25-50% lower doses), severe acidosis/shock (ventilation planning first), QT risk (ECG first if practical, avoid repeated butyrophenones if QTc >500 ms), OSA/COPD/intoxication/hypoventilation (lowest effective depth + capnography), or pediatric/pregnancy (weight/airway discipline + aspiration planning).
Modifier: older / frail
Start 25-50% lower for most sedatives and redose slowly by observed effect, not by clock. Avoid benzodiazepines for routine delirium and avoid dopamine-blocking antipsychotics in Parkinson disease / Lewy body dementia.
Modifier: severe acidosis / shock
Preoxygenate and resuscitate before sedation when feasible. Reduce induction/sedative doses. Match minute ventilation immediately after airway control to avoid post-intubation hypoventilation in metabolic acidosis.
Modifier: QT risk
Do not delay lifesaving chemical control for an ECG when the patient is an immediate threat. Get an ECG first when practical for known long-QT, QTc >500 ms, torsades history, or multiple QT-prolonging medications — avoid or minimize repeated droperidol/haloperidol dosing in that setting.
Modifier: OSA / COPD / intoxication / hypoventilation risk
Use the lowest effective depth, avoid rapid drug stacking, and use ETCO2 when available. Prefer agents that preserve ventilation when they fit the goal: ketamine for dissociation, dexmedetomidine for cooperative light sedation, nitrous for brief cooperative procedures.
Modifier: pediatric / pregnancy
Maternal oxygenation and perfusion are fetal resuscitation — avoid unnecessary deep sedation, anticipate aspiration risk, and use left uterine displacement in later pregnancy. Dose pediatric sedatives by weight with the same airway-rescue discipline.

IV

Etomidate (RSI induction)
0.3 mg/kg IV push
IV
Onset 15-45 seconds, Duration 5-15 minutes. Hemodynamically neutral.

Best use: RSI induction when hemodynamic neutrality is useful, very brief procedures when propofol hypotension is a concern. Major risks: myoclonus common (can interfere with reductions/imaging), nausea/vomiting, adrenal suppression signal (clinically insignificant after a single dose), NO analgesia. Avoid/caution: septic shock or adrenal insufficiency when alternatives are reasonable; do not use repeated dosing or infusion.

Midazolam: IV onset 1-5 min; IM 5-15 min; IN 5-10 min; duration usually 30-60 min IV (longer in older/frail, hepatic disease, or repeated dosing); no analgesia. Lorazepam: slower onset, longer duration — best for withdrawal/seizures, not quick procedure turnover. Diazepam: faster IV onset than lorazepam, but long active metabolites make it a poor routine procedural sedative. Fentanyl: IV onset 1-2 min, duration 30-60 min; analgesia, no amnesia. Avoid/caution: older/frail, OSA/COPD, intoxication, hypotension, co-ingestions, recent opioids, pregnancy near delivery — wait and reassess ventilation before redosing.

Nitrous oxide (folded from flow — see brief.md fold #2)Onset 2-5 min, offset 3-5 min, brief cooperative procedures

Rapid inhaled anxiolysis with mild analgesia. Onset 2-5 min; offset usually 3-5 min after stopping and oxygen washout. Best use: short pediatric or adult procedure — laceration repair, IV start, abscess setup, minor orthopedic manipulation; patient needs to leave quickly and can cooperate with mask. Major risks: nausea/vomiting, dizziness, dysphoria, diffusion into closed gas spaces. Avoid/caution: pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, middle ear surgery/air, intracranial air, decompression sickness, first-trimester pregnancy, severe B12 deficiency, recent bleomycin, inability to cooperate with mask. Not enough for major painful procedures alone. No drug-store entry exists for nitrous oxide.

Agitation agents

Droperidol
2.5-5 mg IM/IV
IM/IV
Drug-store per-dose figure; max 10 mg in 24h; may repeat once at 15-20 min; often paired with midazolam 2-5 mg. Tree text states 5-10 mg — logged as a source conflict, drug-store per-dose figure used here.

Onset 3-10 min IV, 5-15 min IM; duration often 2-4 h, sometimes longer. Best use: undifferentiated severe agitation, combined with midazolam for faster control when QT/stimulant hyperthermia are not dominant. Major risks: QT prolongation in susceptible patients, EPS/akathisia/dystonia, hypotension, oversedation when combined. Avoid/caution: QTc >500 ms, congenital long QT, torsades history, severe hypoK/hypoMg, Parkinson disease/Lewy body dementia.

Haloperidol: IM onset 15-30 min (IV faster, needs QT/EPS monitoring); duration 4-8 h or longer. Best for psychosis/delirium agitation when slower onset is acceptable and benzodiazepine respiratory depression is undesirable. Risks: QT prolongation, EPS, dystonia, akathisia, NMS, Parkinson/Lewy worsening. Olanzapine: IM onset 15-30 min; PO/ODT 30-60 min; duration often 12-24 h. Best for primary psychosis/agitation when longer calming is desired. Risks: sedation, orthostasis, anticholinergic effects, metabolic history. Do NOT give IM olanzapine close to parenteral benzodiazepines (respiratory/cardiopulmonary risk). Use lower doses in older/frail patients; avoid dopamine-blocking antipsychotics in Parkinson disease and Lewy body dementia.

Special population modifiers

Older / frail
Start 25-50% lower for most sedatives; redose slowly after observed effect, not by clock. Avoid benzodiazepines for routine delirium when possible; avoid dopamine-blocking antipsychotics in Parkinson disease/Lewy body dementia; avoid stacking opioids, benzodiazepines, phenobarbital, alcohol, and sleep meds. Propofol: small aliquots, high apnea/hypotension risk. Midazolam: lower dose, longer effect than expected. Ketamine: lower dose may suffice. Droperidol/haloperidol: lower dose and QT/EPS vigilance. Dexmedetomidine: brady/hypotension and slow onset.
Severe acidosis / shock
The danger is not just the sedative — it is loss of compensatory ventilation and collapse after apnea, paralysis, or hypotension. Preoxygenate and resuscitate before sedation when feasible; avoid post-intubation hypoventilation in metabolic acidosis (match minute ventilation immediately); reduce induction/sedative doses in shock. Ketamine may preserve BP in many patients, but catecholamine-depleted shock can still collapse. For pH <7.1 or shock: pair the sedation choice with airway, ventilator, vasopressor, and bicarbonate/hyperkalemia plans when indicated.
QT / ECG risk
Do not delay lifesaving chemical control for ECG when the patient is an immediate threat. Get ECG first when practical for known long-QT, QTc >500 ms, torsades history, multiple QT-prolonging medications, severe hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia/hypocalcemia, or repeated antipsychotic doses. Avoid/minimize repeated droperidol/haloperidol dosing when QTc >500 ms or major torsades risk; benzodiazepines and ketamine are generally QT-neutral alternatives. Correct K/Mg/Ca and monitor after control.
OSA / COPD / hypoventilation / intoxication
High-risk pattern: OSA, obesity hypoventilation, COPD, neuromuscular weakness, intoxication, opioid exposure, alcohol, sedative co-ingestion, pregnancy, older/frail. Use the lowest effective depth, avoid rapid stacking, use ETCO2 when available. Prefer agents that preserve ventilation: ketamine for dissociation, dexmedetomidine for cooperative light sedation, nitrous for brief cooperative procedures. Propofol and benzo/opioid combinations can convert partial obstruction into apnea — have BVM and airway adjuncts at bedside before medication.

Pediatrics: Ketamine is a common ED dissociative agent with strong pediatric experience. Nitrous is useful for brief cooperative procedures. Intranasal midazolam can help anxiolysis but has no analgesia. Dose by weight and monitor with the same airway rescue discipline. Pregnancy: Maternal oxygenation and perfusion are fetal resuscitation. Avoid unnecessary deep sedation. Anticipate aspiration risk and use left uterine displacement in later pregnancy. Nitrous is generally avoided in first trimester. Use the drug required for maternal emergency care when benefits outweigh risks.

Major risks: myoclonus common (can interfere with reductions/imaging), nausea/vomiting, adrenal suppression signal (clinically insignificant after a single dose), NO analgesia. Avoid/caution: septic shock or adrenal insufficiency when alternatives are reasonable; do not use repeated dosing or infusion.

Midazolam: IV onset 1-5 min; IM 5-15 min; IN 5-10 min; duration usually 30-60 min IV (longer in older/frail, hepatic disease, or repeated dosing); no analgesia. Lorazepam: slower onset, longer duration — best for withdrawal/seizures, not quick procedure turnover. Diazepam: faster IV onset than lorazepam, but long active metabolites make it a poor routine procedural sedative. Fentanyl: IV onset 1-2 min, duration 30-60 min; analgesia, no amnesia. Avoid/caution: older/frail, OSA/COPD, intoxication, hypotension, co-ingestions, recent opioids, pregnancy near delivery — wait and reassess ventilation before redosing.

Nitrous oxide (folded from flow — see brief.md fold #2)

Rapid inhaled anxiolysis with mild analgesia. Onset 2-5 min; offset usually 3-5 min after stopping and oxygen washout. Best use: short pediatric or adult procedure — laceration repair, IV start, abscess setup, minor orthopedic manipulation; patient needs to leave quickly and can cooperate with mask. Major risks: nausea/vomiting, dizziness, dysphoria, diffusion into closed gas spaces. Avoid/caution: pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, middle ear surgery/air, intracranial air, decompression sickness, first-trimester pregnancy, severe B12 deficiency, recent bleomycin, inability to cooperate with mask. Not enough for major painful procedures alone. No drug-store entry exists for nitrous oxide.

Agitation agents

Droperidol: 2.5-5 mg IM/IV IM/IV — Drug-store per-dose figure; max 10 mg in 24h; may repeat once at 15-20 min; often paired with midazolam 2-5 mg. Tree text states 5-10 mg — logged as a source conflict, drug-store per-dose figure used here.

Onset 3-10 min IV, 5-15 min IM; duration often 2-4 h, sometimes longer. Best use: undifferentiated severe agitation, combined with midazolam for faster control when QT/stimulant hyperthermia are not dominant. Major risks: QT prolongation in susceptible patients, EPS/akathisia/dystonia, hypotension, oversedation when combined. Avoid/caution: QTc >500 ms, congenital long QT, torsades history, severe hypoK/hypoMg, Parkinson disease/Lewy body dementia.

Haloperidol: IM onset 15-30 min (IV faster, needs QT/EPS monitoring); duration 4-8 h or longer. Best for psychosis/delirium agitation when slower onset is acceptable and benzodiazepine respiratory depression is undesirable. Risks: QT prolongation, EPS, dystonia, akathisia, NMS, Parkinson/Lewy worsening. Olanzapine: IM onset 15-30 min; PO/ODT 30-60 min; duration often 12-24 h. Best for primary psychosis/agitation when longer calming is desired. Risks: sedation, orthostasis, anticholinergic effects, metabolic history. Do NOT give IM olanzapine close to parenteral benzodiazepines (respiratory/cardiopulmonary risk). Use lower doses in older/frail patients; avoid dopamine-blocking antipsychotics in Parkinson disease and Lewy body dementia.

ICU sedation and withdrawal agents

Dexmedetomidine (loading dose): 0.5-1 mcg/kg over 10 min IV — Rare selected use — loading can cause bradycardia/hypotension. Avoid in labile, hypotensive, bradycardic, or frail patients.

Dexmedetomidine (maintenance infusion): 0.2-0.7 mcg/kg/hr IV IV infusion — Titrate q15-30 min. Skip loading dose in most ED/ICU patients.

No-load onset 15-30 min; loading onset 5-10 min; context-sensitive offset often 30-120 min after stopping. Best use: light ICU sedation, extubation bridge, NIV tolerance, awake airway cooperation, delirium-prone ventilated patient. Major risks: bradycardia and hypotension, transient hypertension with loading dose, too slow for crashing agitation or immediate procedural needs. Avoid/caution: severe bradycardia, high-grade AV block, active shock/hypotension. NOT monotherapy for alcohol withdrawal or seizures.

Phenobarbital (loading dose): 10 mg/kg IV IV — Drug-store loading-dose figure, capped at 1000 mg, over 30 min. Tree text states 10-15 mg/kg per local protocol — logged as a source conflict, drug-store loading figure used here.

Phenobarbital (titration): 130-260 mg IV increments IV — Titrate to endpoint; avoid casual stacking with large benzodiazepine/opioid loads.

IV onset about 5-30 min depending on rate/endpoint; clinical duration many hours; half-life about 3-4 days. Best use: alcohol withdrawal, especially escalating benzodiazepine needs or protocolized phenobarbital pathway. Major risks: long duration and stacking, respiratory depression, hypotension, coma — especially with benzodiazepines/opioids/alcohol/hepatic dysfunction, no rapid reversal. Avoid/caution: severe respiratory insufficiency, unstable airway, severe hepatic impairment, porphyria, heavy co-sedative load.

Special population modifiers

Start 25-50% lower for most sedatives; redose slowly after observed effect, not by clock. Avoid benzodiazepines for routine delirium when possible; avoid dopamine-blocking antipsychotics in Parkinson disease/Lewy body dementia; avoid stacking opioids, benzodiazepines, phenobarbital, alcohol, and sleep meds. Propofol: small aliquots, high apnea/hypotension risk. Midazolam: lower dose, longer effect than expected. Ketamine: lower dose may suffice. Droperidol/haloperidol: lower dose and QT/EPS vigilance. Dexmedetomidine: brady/hypotension and slow onset.

The danger is not just the sedative — it is loss of compensatory ventilation and collapse after apnea, paralysis, or hypotension. Preoxygenate and resuscitate before sedation when feasible; avoid post-intubation hypoventilation in metabolic acidosis (match minute ventilation immediately); reduce induction/sedative doses in shock. Ketamine may preserve BP in many patients, but catecholamine-depleted shock can still collapse. For pH <7.1 or shock: pair the sedation choice with airway, ventilator, vasopressor, and bicarbonate/hyperkalemia plans when indicated.

Do not delay lifesaving chemical control for ECG when the patient is an immediate threat. Get ECG first when practical for known long-QT, QTc >500 ms, torsades history, multiple QT-prolonging medications, severe hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia/hypocalcemia, or repeated antipsychotic doses. Avoid/minimize repeated droperidol/haloperidol dosing when QTc >500 ms or major torsades risk; benzodiazepines and ketamine are generally QT-neutral alternatives. Correct K/Mg/Ca and monitor after control.

High-risk pattern: OSA, obesity hypoventilation, COPD, neuromuscular weakness, intoxication, opioid exposure, alcohol, sedative co-ingestion, pregnancy, older/frail. Use the lowest effective depth, avoid rapid stacking, use ETCO2 when available. Prefer agents that preserve ventilation: ketamine for dissociation, dexmedetomidine for cooperative light sedation, nitrous for brief cooperative procedures. Propofol and benzo/opioid combinations can convert partial obstruction into apnea — have BVM and airway adjuncts at bedside before medication.

Pediatrics: Ketamine is a common ED dissociative agent with strong pediatric experience. Nitrous is useful for brief cooperative procedures. Intranasal midazolam can help anxiolysis but has no analgesia. Dose by weight and monitor with the same airway rescue discipline. Pregnancy: Maternal oxygenation and perfusion are fetal resuscitation. Avoid unnecessary deep sedation. Anticipate aspiration risk and use left uterine displacement in later pregnancy. Nitrous is generally avoided in first trimester. Use the drug required for maternal emergency care when benefits outweigh risks.

Side-effect pattern recognition (folded from flow — see brief.md fold #3)

Pattern | Drugs

Apnea / obstruction | Propofol, benzo/opioid stacking, intoxication, OSA/COPD

Hypotension | Propofol, dexmedetomidine loading, high sedative burden, shock physiology

Hypertension / tachycardia | Ketamine, stimulant physiology, pain under-treatment

Bradycardia | Dexmedetomidine, high vagal tone, beta-blockers, conduction disease

QT / EPS | Droperidol, haloperidol, other dopamine blockers

Myoclonus | Etomidate

Vomiting / saliva / laryngospasm | Ketamine

Long tail / stacking | Lorazepam, diazepam, phenobarbital, olanzapine, repeated benzodiazepines

Recovery rule: do not discharge or leave unmonitored until airway, ventilation, perfusion, mentation, pain/nausea, and sedation trajectory are all safe.
