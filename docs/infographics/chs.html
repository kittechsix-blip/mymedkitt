Cannabinoid Hyperemesis Syndrome: Diagnosis of Exclusion, Butyrophenones, Capsaicin

Adults in the ED with suspected cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (chronic cannabis use + cyclic nausea/vomiting ± compulsive hot water bathing). Excludes pediatric CHS-specific management beyond the weight-based calculator, hyperemesis of pregnancy workup, and outpatient addiction-medicine program design beyond discharge counseling.

⚠ CHS is a diagnosis of exclusion. Red flags (hematemesis/melena, severe localized abdominal pain, peritoneal signs, bilious emesis, fever >38.5°C, jaundice, age of first episode >50, no cannabis use history) mandate a targeted organic workup — appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and pregnancy must be excluded before treating as CHS.
⚠ Obtain ECG and baseline QTc BEFORE administering butyrophenones. QTc >500ms contraindicates haloperidol and droperidol — use olanzapine instead.
⚠ AVOID opioids and benzodiazepines in CHS (GRACE-4 Good Practice Statement) — opioids worsen gastroparesis, benzodiazepines are ineffective for CHS nausea, and both carry dependence risk in this population.
⚠ Do NOT combine olanzapine with parenteral (IM/IV) benzodiazepines — multiple case reports of respiratory arrest and death. PO olanzapine + PO lorazepam is acceptable (different pharmacokinetics).
⚠ Do NOT rely on ondansetron as sole first-line therapy — it typically fails in CHS (HaVOC trial showed butyrophenones superior); it may be used only as an adjunct.
⚠ Do NOT give IV cannabinoids (dronabinol) — paradoxically worsens CHS despite being a cannabinoid.

1 · Clinical Recognition & Diagnosis

CHS is a clinical syndrome in chronic cannabis users: cyclic nausea/vomiting with compulsive hot water bathing (~90% of patients). Traditional antiemetics (ondansetron) usually FAIL. GRACE-4 guidelines recommend butyrophenones as first-line therapy. Definitive treatment is cannabis cessation.[1][2][9]

ProdromalMorning nausea, food aversion, vague abdominal discomfort
HyperemeticIntense nausea/vomiting, compulsive hot bathing, significant dehydration
Recovery / Follow-upResolving symptoms with cannabis abstinence

Profuse vomiting (often >7 episodes/day), diffuse abdominal pain, compulsive hot water bathing for relief, significant volume depletion. Weight loss common with recurrent episodes.[6][7][11]

3 · Acute Antiemetic Management

Order baseline labs: BMP (Cr, electrolytes), magnesium, lipase, urine drug screen, pregnancy test (all women of reproductive age), lactate if severe dehydration/hypoperfusion.

Obtain ECG BEFORE administering butyrophenones (haloperidol, droperidol) — check baseline QTc. Ondansetron is typically ineffective in CHS — do not rely on it as first-line therapy.[2][3][7]

Is the baseline QTc prolonged (>500ms)? Butyrophenones carry QTc prolongation risk. If QTc >500ms, avoid butyrophenones and use olanzapine instead.[2][4]

Immediate interventions: IV access — 2 large-bore IVs if severe dehydration; NPO (nothing by mouth until vomiting controlled); cardiac monitor.[2][3][8]

AVOID opioids and benzodiazepines — opioids worsen gastroparesis, benzodiazepines are ineffective for CHS nausea, and both create dependence risk in this population.[2][3][8]

GRACE-4 Recommendation 1: Suggest butyrophenones over 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron) for CHS (conditional, low certainty evidence). The HaVOC Trial (2021) demonstrated haloperidol 0.05 mg/kg IV achieved greater nausea VAS reduction at 2 hours compared to ondansetron 4mg IV.[2][4][10]

Haloperidol and droperidol are contraindicated. Use Olanzapine instead: 5-10 mg IM or PO — no significant QTc prolongation (major advantage). Do NOT combine with parenteral benzodiazepines. Also apply topical capsaicin as adjunct.[2][3]

4 · Capsaicin Adjunct

GRACE-4 Recommendation 2: Suggest capsaicin in addition to standard antiemetics (conditional, very low certainty evidence). Mechanism: TRPV1 (transient receptor potential vanilloid 1) receptor agonist — the same pathway activated by hot water bathing, which is why CHS patients instinctively seek hot showers. Capsaicin provides targeted activation without the dehydration risk of prolonged hot showers.[2][5][12][13]

Capsaicin 0.075% Cream (Zostrix)
0.075% cream — 1-inch ribbon to periumbilical area (~15 cm diameter), reapply every 6-8 hours as needed
Topical
Wear gloves during application.
  • Initial burning sensation is expected and temporary
  • Avoid contact with eyes, nose, mouth
  • Do NOT apply heating pad over capsaicin
  • Wash hands thoroughly if gloves not used

5 · Treatment Response & Escalation

Reassess at 30-60 minutes after butyrophenone + capsaicin. Assess: nausea/vomiting frequency and severity, ability to tolerate PO fluids, vital sign improvement, patient-reported symptom relief.[2][3]

Symptoms improving
Continue supportive care: complete IV fluid resuscitation, trial PO fluids (small sips of clear liquids), recheck electrolytes if initially abnormal, continue capsaicin application. If tolerating PO, proceed to disposition planning.[2]
Refractory — persistent vomiting despite treatment
Escalation options: (1) repeat butyrophenone dose if first dose was subtherapeutic; (2) Olanzapine 5-10 mg IM/PO as second-line agent; (3) reassess for alternative diagnoses if truly refractory; (4) consider admission for continued IV antiemetics. Do NOT escalate to opioids or benzodiazepines.[2][3]

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT treat as CHS without excluding organic causes — appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and pregnancy must be ruled out when red flags are present.

Do NOT give butyrophenones without a baseline ECG — QTc >500ms contraindicates haloperidol and droperidol; use olanzapine instead.

Do NOT give opioids or benzodiazepines for CHS nausea — opioids worsen gastroparesis, benzodiazepines are ineffective, and both create dependence risk.

Do NOT combine olanzapine with parenteral (IM/IV) benzodiazepines — risk of respiratory depression and death.

Do NOT rely on ondansetron as sole therapy — it typically fails in CHS; the HaVOC trial showed butyrophenones are superior.

Do NOT give IV cannabinoids (dronabinol) — it paradoxically worsens CHS.
