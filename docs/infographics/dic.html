DIC: Find the Trigger, Read the Phenotype

Adults with suspected or confirmed disseminated intravascular coagulation in the ED or ICU. DIC is always secondary — this guides recognition, scoring, and phenotype-driven hemostatic management alongside definitive treatment of the trigger. Excludes isolated liver failure, HIT, TTP/HUS, and primary hyperfibrinolysis, which mimic DIC but need different management. Weight-based calculators assume adult dosing; confirm pediatric product volumes and fibrinogen targets separately.

⚠ DIC is always secondary — find and treat the underlying trigger first; product transfusion and anticoagulation are adjuncts, not definitive therapy.
⚠ Differentiate bleeding-dominant vs thrombotic-dominant phenotype before choosing therapy — the single most important branch in DIC management.
⚠ ISTH score ≥5 = overt DIC; SIC score ≥4 in sepsis identifies progression risk before overt DIC.
⚠ APL: start ATRA 45 mg/m²/day immediately on suspicion — do not wait for genetic confirmation. Keep fibrinogen >150-200, AVOID heparin and TXA.
⚠ Obstetric DIC: cryo to keep fibrinogen ≥2 g/L (not <1), TXA 1g IV within 3 hours, MTP 1:1:1.
⚠ Do not transfuse platelets, FFP, or cryoprecipitate for abnormal labs alone in a non-bleeding, non-pre-procedure patient — prophylactic correction has not shown benefit and may worsen thrombosis.
⚠ Avoid heparin/LMWH in bleeding-dominant DIC, APL, obstetric hemorrhage, or platelets <50 × 10⁹/L — reserve for thrombotic-dominant phenotype without active bleeding.

1 · Recognize & suspect

What DIC is
Disseminated intravascular coagulation is an acquired syndrome of systemic coagulation activation that consumes platelets and clotting factors — causing microvascular thrombosis and bleeding at the same time. It is always secondary: the single most important determinant of survival is prompt control of the underlying trigger.[2]

Suspect DIC in any critically ill patient with new coagulopathy and a known trigger: bleeding from multiple uncontrolled sites, petechiae/purpura without trauma, microvascular thrombosis (purpura fulminans, digital ischemia, organ dysfunction). Bleeding and thrombosis together is pathognomonic.[1]

  • CBC with platelets
  • PT/INR and aPTT
  • Fibrinogen
  • D-dimer (or fibrin degradation products if unavailable)
  • Peripheral smear (schistocytes in 10–50%)
No clear trigger — DIC mimics to consider instead4 look-alikes
Severe liver failure — prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen, low platelets. Distinguisher: Factor VIII is normal/high in DIC (acute phase), low in liver failure.
Vitamin K deficiency / warfarin — prolonged PT > PTT, normal fibrinogen and platelets. Reverses with vitamin K / 4F-PCC.
HIT — thrombocytopenia + thrombosis, heparin exposure 5–14 days prior. 4Ts score, anti-PF4. Stop all heparin.
TTP — microangiopathic hemolysis with normal coagulation studies. Needs plasma exchange, not product replacement.

ISTH interpretation
≥5 = overt DIC (sensitivity 91%, specificity 97%); repeat daily for trend. <5 = non-overt DIC; treat the trigger and repeat in 1–2 days.[5]

In sepsis, the SIC score flags coagulopathy earlier than overt-DIC criteria: SIC ≥4 (platelet + INR subscore ≥3) identifies patients at risk for progression to overt DIC. JAAM ≥4 is more sensitive than ISTH for early sepsis-associated DIC.[8]

Non-overt DIC — the window for upstream intervention
Aggressive trigger control (antibiotics/source control, definitive obstetric/oncologic management), avoid unnecessary product transfusion, and repeat scoring every 12–24 hours. Most non-overt DIC resolves with source control alone — resist the urge to "treat the labs."[4]

3 · The pivotal branch — bleeding vs thrombotic

The single most important decision
Once DIC is confirmed, classify the phenotype. Bleeding-dominant → replace what is consumed, to specific thresholds. Thrombotic / non-bleeding → minimize prophylactic transfusion and treat the clot.[2]

Set the patient's state with the toggles above (bleeding phenotype + etiology). The exactly-what-to-do card below updates live — highest-acuity etiology wins.

APL / acute leukemia
Start ATRA 45 mg/m²/day immediately on suspicion — do not wait for genetics. Keep fibrinogen >150–200 mg/dL with cryoprecipitate. AVOID heparin and TXA.[11]
Obstetric DIC / PPH
Cryoprecipitate to keep fibrinogen ≥2 g/L (not <1 — pregnant baseline is 4–6 g/L). TXA 1 g IV within 3 hours (WOMAN). Activate MTP 1:1:1 for massive hemorrhage.[12]
Trauma-induced coagulopathy
TXA 1 g IV within 3 hours of injury (CRASH-2), MTP 1:1:1, keep fibrinogen ≥1.5–2 g/L, replace calcium, maintain normothermia.[13]
Sepsis-induced DIC
Source control + appropriate antibiotics within 1 hour. Goal-directed resuscitation (MAP ≥65, lactate clearance). Hemostatic support only if bleeding. Score with SIC; anticoagulation remains controversial.[9]
Bleeding-dominant DIC
Replace what is consumed to thresholds: platelets ≥50 × 10⁹/L, FFP 15 mL/kg if PT/aPTT >1.5× ULN, cryoprecipitate for low fibrinogen. See the weight-based doses below.[14]
Thrombotic / non-bleeding DIC
Minimize prophylactic transfusion. Treat the clot: LMWH is first-line for Trousseau syndrome. Transfuse only pre-procedure or if a critical threshold is breached.[2]
