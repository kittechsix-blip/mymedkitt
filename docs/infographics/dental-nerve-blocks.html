Dental / Intraoral Nerve Blocks — ED Procedure

ED intraoral regional anesthesia for odontogenic (toothache) pain, dental fracture/avulsion management, and repair of intraoral/dentoalveolar lacerations (alveolar abscess management as an adjunct). Excludes active infection/cellulitis at the planned injection site, known true allergy to the local anesthetic class, and patients unable to cooperate or protect the airway with an anesthetized tongue/floor of mouth (absolute contraindications).

⚠ No active infection/cellulitis at the injection site and no true LA allergy before any block — apply topical anesthetic first.
⚠ Calculate the maximum weight-based LA dose BEFORE drawing up — dental cartridges are 1.8 mL; track cartridge count and convert to mg.
⚠ ASPIRATE before every injection; double-aspirate (rotate 90°) for the inferior alveolar block — the inferior alveolar artery/vein run with the nerve.
⚠ For the IANB, advance until you GENTLY contact bone before injecting — no bone contact risks parotid injection and transient facial-nerve (CN VII) palsy.
⚠ Deposit anesthetic ADJACENT to the foramen, never INTO it — intraneural injection causes persistent paresthesia.
⚠ Warn every patient (especially children) not to bite or burn the numb lip, cheek, or tongue.
⚠ Wait for onset and TEST the territory (lower-lip numbness for IANB) before starting the procedure.
⚠ Suspected LAST (local anesthetic systemic toxicity) → stop injecting, O2, treat seizures, give 20% lipid emulsion 1.5 mL/kg.

1 · Principle

Intraoral regional anesthesia controls odontogenic (toothache) pain, anesthetizes for dental fracture/avulsion management, and enables repair of intraoral and dentoalveolar lacerations — often more effective than systemic analgesia alone.

Key principle: maxillary teeth (V2) are usually anesthetized by supraperiosteal infiltration or a superior-alveolar block because maxillary bone is porous. Mandibular teeth (V3) require an inferior alveolar nerve block because dense cortical bone blocks infiltration.

Porous maxilla vs. dense mandible
Maxillary teeth (V2) = supraperiosteal/superior-alveolar (porous bone). Mandibular teeth (V3) = inferior alveolar block (dense bone).

Select the tooth or intraoral region. Maxillary = upper arch; mandibular = lower arch. Confirm no overlying infection at the injection site and calculate the max LA dose first.

2 · Contraindications + pre-procedure checks

Absolute contraindications
Active infection/cellulitis at the planned injection site (do not inject through infected tissue — risks spreading infection and the block often fails in acidic infected tissue); known true allergy to the local anesthetic class; patient unable to cooperate / protect the airway with an anesthetized tongue and floor of mouth.

Relative — weigh risk/benefit: therapeutic anticoagulation (intraoral blocks are mostly superficial/low-risk except the deep IANB, where the inferior alveolar vessels run with the nerve — aspirate, apply pressure); bisphosphonate/antiresorptive use (caution with any dentoalveolar trauma; coordinate with dentistry); trismus limiting access for an IANB.

  1. Informed consent (transient numbness of lip/tongue, self-inflicted bite injury while numb, failed/partial block, hematoma, vascular injection, transient facial nerve palsy with a misdirected IANB)
  2. Allergies (LA, latex, antiseptic, topical benzocaine)
  3. Anticoagulation status
  4. Calculate the maximum safe LA dose BEFORE drawing up — dental cartridges are 1.8 mL; track cartridge count
  5. Apply topical anesthetic (benzocaine 20% or lidocaine gel) to the dried mucosa for 1–2 min to reduce needle-stick pain
  6. Aspirate before every injection

3 · Shared technique — all intraoral blocks

Equipment: 3 mL syringe (or dental aspirating syringe), 25–27 gauge needle (1 inch for infiltration; 1.5 inch/"long" for IANB); topical benzocaine 20% gel on a cotton applicator; 2×2 gauze to dry mucosa and retract; good light + suction.

Lidocaine 2% with epinephrine 1:100,000
Standard ED/dental agent — volume varies by technique (0.3–3 mL per block)
Local (intraoral)
Fast onset (~2–5 min), ~60 min pulpal / 3–4 h soft-tissue anesthesia; epinephrine prolongs duration and reduces bleeding (intraoral end-arteries are not a concern).
Bupivacaine 0.5% with epinephrine
Longer-acting alternative — volume varies by technique
Local (intraoral)
Slower onset, long duration — good for prolonged pain control / overnight relief. Articaine 4% (dental) is widely used by dentistry for superior diffusion; lidocaine remains the default ED agent. Buffer lidocaine 9:1 with sodium bicarbonate to reduce sting.
  1. Dry mucosa, apply topical for 1–2 min
  2. Retract the lip/cheek; keep the tissue taut
  3. Insert with bevel toward bone; advance to the target depth
  4. ASPIRATE (rotate 90° and re-aspirate for the IANB)
  5. Inject SLOWLY (≥1 min per cartridge — slow = less pain and safer)
  6. Wait for onset (3–5 min infiltration; 5–10 min IANB) and TEST before proceeding

5 · Maxillary (V2) blocks

Indication: anesthesia of a SINGLE maxillary tooth and its buccal soft tissue — the workhorse for one upper tooth. Works because maxillary alveolar bone is thin and porous.

Distribution: the targeted tooth (pulp) and overlying buccal gingiva/mucosa. Does NOT anesthetize the palatal soft tissue.

Landmark: the mucobuccal fold (gingivobuccal sulcus) at the apex of the target tooth.

  1. Retract the lip; dry and apply topical at the mucobuccal fold over the tooth
  2. Orient the bevel toward bone; insert at the height of the mucobuccal fold above the apex of the tooth
  3. Advance only a few millimeters until the needle tip is at/above the apex (do not contact bone forcefully); aspirate
  4. Deposit ~1–1.8 mL slowly — onset 3–5 min
Depositing below the apex (too shallow) gives soft-tissue but not pulpal anesthesia — get the tip to the apex level.

Indication: maxillary central/lateral incisors and canine on one side — useful for multiple anterior teeth or when infiltration over an infected anterior tooth is undesirable.

Distribution: via the infraorbital nerve and its ASA branch — the incisors and canine (pulp), plus the lower eyelid, lateral nose, and upper lip skin.

Landmark: the infraorbital foramen ~1 cm below the infraorbital rim at the mid-pupillary line; intraoral entry at the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary 1st premolar/canine.

  1. Palpate the infraorbital foramen externally; keep a fingertip over it to guide and to prevent superior spread toward the orbit
  2. Retract the upper lip; insert at the mucobuccal fold above the 1st premolar, parallel to the tooth's long axis, advancing toward the palpating finger (~1.5–2 cm)
  3. Aspirate; deposit 1.5–3 mL adjacent to (NOT into) the foramen
  4. Massage over the foramen to encourage anterior/middle superior alveolar diffusion (needed for pulpal anesthesia of the incisors)
Never force the needle into the foramen (paresthesia). The finger over the foramen protects the eye and directs flow toward the teeth.

Indication: maxillary premolars (and the mesiobuccal root of the 1st molar when present) — the MSA nerve is absent in ~30–50% of people, in whom the ASA/infraorbital block covers the premolars.

Distribution: the 1st and 2nd premolars (pulp) and overlying buccal gingiva; the mesiobuccal root of the 1st molar.

Landmark: the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary 2nd premolar.

  1. Retract the cheek; dry and apply topical at the mucobuccal fold above the 2nd premolar
  2. Bevel toward bone; insert until the tip is above the apex of the 2nd premolar; aspirate
  3. Deposit ~0.9–1.8 mL slowly — onset 3–5 min
If anesthesia of the premolars fails, the MSA may be absent — fall back to a supraperiosteal infiltration over each tooth or an ASA/infraorbital block.

Indication: maxillary molars (except the mesiobuccal root of the 1st molar, often MSA-supplied) and their buccal gingiva.

Distribution: the 2nd and 3rd molars and the distobuccal/palatal roots of the 1st molar (pulp), plus overlying buccal gingiva. Add a supraperiosteal infiltration over the 1st molar if its mesiobuccal root is not numb.

Landmark: the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary 2nd molar, aiming for the posterosuperior maxillary tuberosity where the PSA nerve enters foramina.

  1. Retract the cheek; dry and apply topical above the 2nd molar
  2. Insert at the mucobuccal fold above the 2nd molar; advance the needle UP, IN, and BACK at ~45° to ~16 mm depth following the curve of the tuberosity
  3. ASPIRATE carefully — the pterygoid venous plexus lies here; this is the highest-hematoma-risk intraoral block
  4. Deposit ~0.9–1.8 mL slowly
Over-insertion or positive aspiration → pterygoid plexus hematoma (rapid facial swelling). Aspirate, do not over-advance, and apply pressure.

Indication: palatal soft-tissue anesthesia of the POSTERIOR hard palate — palatal lacerations, palatal abscess drainage, or palatal manipulation of maxillary premolars/molars.

Distribution: palatal mucosa and bone from the distal canine back to the posterior hard palate, on the injected side. Does NOT anesthetize tooth pulp — pair with a buccal/PSA block for tooth work.

Landmark: the greater palatine foramen ~1 cm medial to the gingival margin of the maxillary 2nd/3rd molar.

  1. Apply firm pressure with a cotton applicator at the foramen site for ischemia/pre-anesthesia (palatal injections are painful) and topical
  2. Insert at/just anterior to the foramen with the bevel toward the tissue; advance gently to bone
  3. Aspirate; deposit ONLY 0.3–0.5 mL slowly — the palatal mucosa is tightly bound
Palatal injections hurt — use pressure anesthesia, small volume, and slow injection. Over-volume causes painful tissue blanching/sloughing.

Indication: palatal soft-tissue anesthesia of the ANTERIOR hard palate behind the upper incisors — anterior palatal lacerations or bilateral anterior palatal procedures.

Distribution: palatal mucosa and bone canine-to-canine (bilateral) — one injection covers both sides.

Landmark: the incisive foramen beneath the incisive papilla, the small midline mucosal bulge just behind the two central incisors.

  1. Apply pressure with a cotton applicator on the incisive papilla and topical (one of the most sensitive intraoral sites)
  2. Insert just LATERAL to the papilla (not directly into it), bevel toward tissue; advance gently toward the foramen until bone
  3. Aspirate; deposit ONLY 0.3–0.5 mL very slowly
The incisive papilla is exquisitely sensitive — pressure anesthesia, lateral approach, tiny volume, very slow injection.

6 · Mandibular (V3) blocks

Indication: the single most useful ED dental block — anesthetizes ALL mandibular teeth of one quadrant. Useful for any lower toothache, mandibular fracture/avulsion, or extensive lower-lip wounds. Mandibular teeth cannot be reliably infiltrated (dense cortical bone), so the IANB is required.

Distribution: all ipsilateral mandibular teeth (pulp), the body of the mandible, buccal mucosa anterior to the mental foramen, and — via the mental branch — the ipsilateral lower lip and chin. A lingual co-block (almost always achieved) adds the anterior 2/3 tongue, floor of mouth, and lingual gingiva. Does NOT cover the buccal gingiva of the molars (add a long buccal block).

Landmark: the mandibular foramen on the medial ramus. Entry at the coronoid notch / pterygomandibular raphe, ~1 cm above the mandibular occlusal plane.

  1. Palpate the coronoid notch (greatest concavity of the anterior ramus) with the thumb; rest fingers on the posterior border of the ramus to gauge depth
  2. Position the syringe barrel over the contralateral premolars. Insert lateral to the pterygomandibular raphe, ~1 cm above the occlusal plane, between the thumbnail (notch) and the raphe
  3. Advance ~20–25 mm until you GENTLY contact bone (medial ramus); withdraw ~1 mm
  4. ASPIRATE (the inferior alveolar artery/vein run with the nerve — rotate the syringe and re-aspirate)
  5. Deposit ~1.5 mL over the nerve; withdraw halfway and deposit ~0.5 mL to catch the lingual nerve
  6. Onset 5–10 min; the SIGN of success is numbness of the ipsilateral lower lip
Inserting too deep WITHOUT contacting bone risks injecting into the parotid → transient facial nerve (CN VII) palsy. Always contact bone before injecting. Positive aspiration → withdraw/redirect. Warn the patient not to bite the numb lip/tongue.

Indication: mandibular premolars to the midline (incisors, canine, premolars) and/or the lower lip and chin — when a full IANB is not needed. Adding intraforaminal pressure converts it to an incisive nerve block for pulpal anesthesia of the anterior teeth.

Distribution: mental block alone → lower lip, chin, labial/buccal mucosa anterior to the foramen. With incisive component (pressure to drive anesthetic into the foramen) → pulp of the ipsilateral premolars, canine, and incisors.

Landmark: the mental foramen below/between the apices of the mandibular 1st and 2nd premolars, in the mid-pupillary line. In edentulous patients it migrates toward the alveolar crest.

  1. Palpate the foramen externally; retract the lower lip and identify the mucobuccal fold at the premolars
  2. Insert at the mucobuccal fold just anterior to/at the foramen, directing toward it; advance ~5–6 mm; aspirate
  3. Deposit ~1–1.8 mL; for pulpal (incisive) anesthesia of the anterior teeth, apply finger pressure over the foramen for ~1–2 min to drive anesthetic in
Do not enter the foramen (paresthesia). For wounds crossing the midline, the lower lip needs a BILATERAL block.

Indication: anesthesia of the BUCCAL soft tissue (gingiva/mucosa) of the mandibular MOLARS — an adjunct to the IANB when buccal soft-tissue work is needed (e.g., buccal laceration, rubber-dam clamp, surgical access to molars). The IANB alone does NOT numb the buccal gingiva of the molars.

Distribution: the buccal soft tissue and gingiva lateral to the mandibular molars (does not anesthetize teeth).

Landmark: the buccal mucosa distal and buccal to the most posterior mandibular molar, at the occlusal plane.

  1. Retract the cheek laterally; dry and apply topical at the mucosa distal/buccal to the last molar
  2. Insert a few millimeters into the mucosa at the occlusal-plane level; aspirate
  3. Deposit ~0.3 mL as tissue blanches — onset is rapid (1–2 min)
This is a small soft-tissue infiltration — overshooting deep is unnecessary; keep it submucosal.

7 · Test the block before proceeding

Wait for onset (3–5 min for infiltration/maxillary blocks; 5–10 min for the IANB). Test soft tissue with a pinprick of the gingiva/lip vs. the contralateral side — for the IANB, lower-lip numbness is the classic sign. Test the tooth (pulp) with a cold test or gentle percussion, which is painless when anesthetized.

Fully numb — proceed with the repair / procedure / pain control.
Partial / patchy — supplement: re-block (verify landmark), add a buccal/palatal injection, or a supraperiosteal injection over the deficient tooth (stay under the max dose).
No effect after 10 min — recheck the landmark/depth; consider accessory innervation or an absent MSA; re-attempt once or escalate.

8 · Rescue a partial / failed block

Common causes of failure: IANB — too anterior/low, or not deep enough (the most commonly missed block); re-confirm the coronoid notch and that you contacted bone ~20–25 mm in. Accessory innervation (mylohyoid nerve) can supply lower molars — add a buccal-floor infiltration. Maxillary — tip below the apex (soft tissue only, no pulp), or absent MSA for premolars. Also: insufficient wait (bupivacaine 8–10 min), too little volume, or a block attempted in acidic infected tissue.

  1. Re-confirm the landmark and re-inject (aspirate first)
  2. Add a supraperiosteal infiltration over the specific deficient maxillary tooth, or a buccal/palatal injection for soft-tissue gaps
  3. For a failed IANB, consider repeating with attention to depth/bone contact, or an alternative mandibular technique
  4. ALWAYS re-check the running total against the max LA dose before adding more

Landmark: the infraorbital foramen ~1 cm below the infraorbital rim at the mid-pupillary line; intraoral entry at the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary 1st premolar/canine.

  • Palpate the infraorbital foramen externally; keep a fingertip over it to guide and to prevent superior spread toward the orbit
  • Retract the upper lip; insert at the mucobuccal fold above the 1st premolar, parallel to the tooth's long axis, advancing toward the palpating finger (~1.5–2 cm)
  • Aspirate; deposit 1.5–3 mL adjacent to (NOT into) the foramen
  • Massage over the foramen to encourage anterior/middle superior alveolar diffusion (needed for pulpal anesthesia of the incisors)

Never force the needle into the foramen (paresthesia). The finger over the foramen protects the eye and directs flow toward the teeth.

Indication: maxillary premolars (and the mesiobuccal root of the 1st molar when present) — the MSA nerve is absent in ~30–50% of people, in whom the ASA/infraorbital block covers the premolars.

Distribution: the 1st and 2nd premolars (pulp) and overlying buccal gingiva; the mesiobuccal root of the 1st molar.

Landmark: the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary 2nd premolar.

  • Retract the cheek; dry and apply topical at the mucobuccal fold above the 2nd premolar
  • Bevel toward bone; insert until the tip is above the apex of the 2nd premolar; aspirate
  • Deposit ~0.9–1.8 mL slowly — onset 3–5 min

If anesthesia of the premolars fails, the MSA may be absent — fall back to a supraperiosteal infiltration over each tooth or an ASA/infraorbital block.

Indication: maxillary molars (except the mesiobuccal root of the 1st molar, often MSA-supplied) and their buccal gingiva.

Distribution: the 2nd and 3rd molars and the distobuccal/palatal roots of the 1st molar (pulp), plus overlying buccal gingiva. Add a supraperiosteal infiltration over the 1st molar if its mesiobuccal root is not numb.

Landmark: the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary 2nd molar, aiming for the posterosuperior maxillary tuberosity where the PSA nerve enters foramina.

  • Retract the cheek; dry and apply topical above the 2nd molar
  • Insert at the mucobuccal fold above the 2nd molar; advance the needle UP, IN, and BACK at ~45° to ~16 mm depth following the curve of the tuberosity
  • ASPIRATE carefully — the pterygoid venous plexus lies here; this is the highest-hematoma-risk intraoral block
  • Deposit ~0.9–1.8 mL slowly

Over-insertion or positive aspiration → pterygoid plexus hematoma (rapid facial swelling). Aspirate, do not over-advance, and apply pressure.

Indication: palatal soft-tissue anesthesia of the POSTERIOR hard palate — palatal lacerations, palatal abscess drainage, or palatal manipulation of maxillary premolars/molars.

Distribution: palatal mucosa and bone from the distal canine back to the posterior hard palate, on the injected side. Does NOT anesthetize tooth pulp — pair with a buccal/PSA block for tooth work.

Landmark: the greater palatine foramen ~1 cm medial to the gingival margin of the maxillary 2nd/3rd molar.

  • Apply firm pressure with a cotton applicator at the foramen site for ischemia/pre-anesthesia (palatal injections are painful) and topical
  • Insert at/just anterior to the foramen with the bevel toward the tissue; advance gently to bone
  • Aspirate; deposit ONLY 0.3–0.5 mL slowly — the palatal mucosa is tightly bound

Palatal injections hurt — use pressure anesthesia, small volume, and slow injection. Over-volume causes painful tissue blanching/sloughing.

Indication: palatal soft-tissue anesthesia of the ANTERIOR hard palate behind the upper incisors — anterior palatal lacerations or bilateral anterior palatal procedures.

Distribution: palatal mucosa and bone canine-to-canine (bilateral) — one injection covers both sides.

Landmark: the incisive foramen beneath the incisive papilla, the small midline mucosal bulge just behind the two central incisors.

  • Apply pressure with a cotton applicator on the incisive papilla and topical (one of the most sensitive intraoral sites)
  • Insert just LATERAL to the papilla (not directly into it), bevel toward tissue; advance gently toward the foramen until bone
  • Aspirate; deposit ONLY 0.3–0.5 mL very slowly

The incisive papilla is exquisitely sensitive — pressure anesthesia, lateral approach, tiny volume, very slow injection.

6 · Mandibular (V3) blocks

Indication: the single most useful ED dental block — anesthetizes ALL mandibular teeth of one quadrant. Useful for any lower toothache, mandibular fracture/avulsion, or extensive lower-lip wounds. Mandibular teeth cannot be reliably infiltrated (dense cortical bone), so the IANB is required.

Distribution: all ipsilateral mandibular teeth (pulp), the body of the mandible, buccal mucosa anterior to the mental foramen, and — via the mental branch — the ipsilateral lower lip and chin. A lingual co-block (almost always achieved) adds the anterior 2/3 tongue, floor of mouth, and lingual gingiva. Does NOT cover the buccal gingiva of the molars (add a long buccal block).

Landmark: the mandibular foramen on the medial ramus. Entry at the coronoid notch / pterygomandibular raphe, ~1 cm above the mandibular occlusal plane.

  • Palpate the coronoid notch (greatest concavity of the anterior ramus) with the thumb; rest fingers on the posterior border of the ramus to gauge depth
  • Position the syringe barrel over the contralateral premolars. Insert lateral to the pterygomandibular raphe, ~1 cm above the occlusal plane, between the thumbnail (notch) and the raphe
  • Advance ~20–25 mm until you GENTLY contact bone (medial ramus); withdraw ~1 mm
  • ASPIRATE (the inferior alveolar artery/vein run with the nerve — rotate the syringe and re-aspirate)
  • Deposit ~1.5 mL over the nerve; withdraw halfway and deposit ~0.5 mL to catch the lingual nerve
  • Onset 5–10 min; the SIGN of success is numbness of the ipsilateral lower lip

Inserting too deep WITHOUT contacting bone risks injecting into the parotid → transient facial nerve (CN VII) palsy. Always contact bone before injecting. Positive aspiration → withdraw/redirect. Warn the patient not to bite the numb lip/tongue.

Indication: mandibular premolars to the midline (incisors, canine, premolars) and/or the lower lip and chin — when a full IANB is not needed. Adding intraforaminal pressure converts it to an incisive nerve block for pulpal anesthesia of the anterior teeth.

Distribution: mental block alone → lower lip, chin, labial/buccal mucosa anterior to the foramen. With incisive component (pressure to drive anesthetic into the foramen) → pulp of the ipsilateral premolars, canine, and incisors.

Landmark: the mental foramen below/between the apices of the mandibular 1st and 2nd premolars, in the mid-pupillary line. In edentulous patients it migrates toward the alveolar crest.

  • Palpate the foramen externally; retract the lower lip and identify the mucobuccal fold at the premolars
  • Insert at the mucobuccal fold just anterior to/at the foramen, directing toward it; advance ~5–6 mm; aspirate
  • Deposit ~1–1.8 mL; for pulpal (incisive) anesthesia of the anterior teeth, apply finger pressure over the foramen for ~1–2 min to drive anesthetic in

Do not enter the foramen (paresthesia). For wounds crossing the midline, the lower lip needs a BILATERAL block.

Indication: anesthesia of the BUCCAL soft tissue (gingiva/mucosa) of the mandibular MOLARS — an adjunct to the IANB when buccal soft-tissue work is needed (e.g., buccal laceration, rubber-dam clamp, surgical access to molars). The IANB alone does NOT numb the buccal gingiva of the molars.

Distribution: the buccal soft tissue and gingiva lateral to the mandibular molars (does not anesthetize teeth).

Landmark: the buccal mucosa distal and buccal to the most posterior mandibular molar, at the occlusal plane.

  • Retract the cheek laterally; dry and apply topical at the mucosa distal/buccal to the last molar
  • Insert a few millimeters into the mucosa at the occlusal-plane level; aspirate
  • Deposit ~0.3 mL as tissue blanches — onset is rapid (1–2 min)

This is a small soft-tissue infiltration — overshooting deep is unnecessary; keep it submucosal.

7 · Test the block before proceeding

Wait for onset (3–5 min for infiltration/maxillary blocks; 5–10 min for the IANB). Test soft tissue with a pinprick of the gingiva/lip vs. the contralateral side — for the IANB, lower-lip numbness is the classic sign. Test the tooth (pulp) with a cold test or gentle percussion, which is painless when anesthetized.

Fully numb — proceed with the repair / procedure / pain control.

Partial / patchy — supplement: re-block (verify landmark), add a buccal/palatal injection, or a supraperiosteal injection over the deficient tooth (stay under the max dose).

No effect after 10 min — recheck the landmark/depth; consider accessory innervation or an absent MSA; re-attempt once or escalate.

8 · Rescue a partial / failed block

Common causes of failure: IANB — too anterior/low, or not deep enough (the most commonly missed block); re-confirm the coronoid notch and that you contacted bone ~20–25 mm in. Accessory innervation (mylohyoid nerve) can supply lower molars — add a buccal-floor infiltration. Maxillary — tip below the apex (soft tissue only, no pulp), or absent MSA for premolars. Also: insufficient wait (bupivacaine 8–10 min), too little volume, or a block attempted in acidic infected tissue.

  • Re-confirm the landmark and re-inject (aspirate first)
  • Add a supraperiosteal infiltration over the specific deficient maxillary tooth, or a buccal/palatal injection for soft-tissue gaps
  • For a failed IANB, consider repeating with attention to depth/bone contact, or an alternative mandibular technique
  • ALWAYS re-check the running total against the max LA dose before adding more

9 · Complications + recognition

Complication | Recognition / management

Failed / partial block | Especially the IANB — see rescue steps above

Self-inflicted bite/burn injury | Numb lip, cheek, or tongue — WARN every patient, especially children

Transient pain on injection | Palatal blocks worst — use pressure anesthesia and slow injection

Trismus / soreness | At the IANB site

Hematoma | Highest risk with the PSA block (pterygoid venous plexus → rapid facial swelling) and the IANB (inferior alveolar vessels). Aspirate, apply pressure, ice + reassurance

Transient facial nerve (CN VII) palsy | From an IANB needle placed too deep/posterior into the parotid gland — ipsilateral facial droop, inability to close the eye. Self-limited; protect the eye, reassure. Prevent by contacting bone before injecting

Intravascular injection | Aspirate before EVERY injection; the IANB and PSA are highest-risk

Nerve injury / persistent paresthesia | Deposit beside, not into, the foramen; stop if the patient reports an electric shock

Needle breakage | Rare — never bend the needle or insert to the hub; use a long needle for the IANB

Perioral numbness, tinnitus, metallic taste → seizures, cardiac collapse. If suspected: stop injecting, O2, manage airway, treat seizures (benzodiazepine), and give 20% lipid emulsion 1.5 mL/kg bolus then infusion. Keep total dose under the calculated maximum.

20% Lipid Emulsion (Intralipid): 1.5 mL/kg IV bolus over ~1 min IV — Standard of care for LAST; administer immediately alongside supportive care.

20% Lipid Emulsion (Intralipid): 0.25 mL/kg/min continuous infusion × 30–60 min IV — Follows the bolus for LAST.

10 · Procedure note template

  • Procedure: block type, tooth #/region, right/left/bilateral
  • Indication: odontogenic pain / dental fracture / dentoalveolar or intraoral laceration repair / abscess management, at site
  • Consent: verbal informed consent obtained; risks of transient lip/tongue numbness, self-inflicted bite injury, failed/partial block, hematoma, intravascular injection, and (for IANB) transient facial palsy discussed
  • Allergies / anticoagulation: documented
  • Agent / dose: agent and concentration, cartridges → mL → mg total, confirmed under the maximum weight-based dose
  • Topical: benzocaine 20% applied to dried mucosa prior to injection
  • Landmarks: identified by palpation (mucobuccal fold at apex / infraorbital foramen / greater palatine foramen / incisive papilla / coronoid notch with bone contact / mental foramen)
  • Technique: negative aspiration before each injection (double-aspiration for IANB); slow injection adjacent to (not into) the foramen
  • Effect: adequate anesthesia confirmed at [X] min (lower-lip numbness for IANB); procedure performed
  • Tolerance / complications: tolerated well; no hematoma, intravascular injection, facial palsy, or LAST
  • Disposition: dental follow-up, bite-precaution and return instructions given
