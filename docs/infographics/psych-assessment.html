Psychiatric Assessment: The 10-Component MSE

Adults and adolescents (age 12+) undergoing a psychiatric evaluation or Mental Status Examination in the ED, inpatient, or outpatient behavioral health setting. Excludes formal cognitive screening instruments themselves (MMSE/MoCA scoring is referenced but not administered here) and does not replace a full psychiatric consultation.

⚠ Screen ALL patients age 12+ for suicidal and homicidal ideation using a validated tool (C-SSRS, PHQ-2, Patient Safety Screener, or ASQ) per Joint Commission NPSG.15.01.01 — ask directly, including about firearms access.
⚠ Active suicidal ideation with plan/intent, or homicidal ideation, is a psychiatric emergency — do NOT discharge: place 1:1 observation, remove means, and obtain psychiatric consultation before disposition.
⚠ Command hallucinations must be asked about specifically and their content documented — do not infer presence or absence from general hallucination screening alone.
⚠ Homicidal ideation can trigger Tarasoff duty-to-warn obligations that vary by state; even in a no-mandatory-duty jurisdiction (e.g., Texas — Thapar v. Zezulka, 1999), document the threat content, target, your decision to warn or not, and actions taken.

1 · Overview & History

A comprehensive psychiatric assessment combines historical information with a structured Mental Status Examination (MSE) — a snapshot of current cognitive, behavioral, and emotional functioning. The MSE is broader than the MMSE, which is a cognitive-screening tool only.

CPT 90792 documentation
Billing for a full psychiatric diagnostic evaluation (CPT 90792) requires complete history, mental status examination, initial diagnosis, evaluation of capacity to respond to treatment, and an initial treatment plan.

History taking covers the chief complaint in the patient's own words, psychiatric history (prior diagnoses, hospitalizations, treatments, self-harm), medication history, substance use (alcohol/illicit/tobacco, withdrawal history), medical history, family psychiatric history, and social history. Document collateral sources — family, EMS, pharmacy records, prior ED visits — and who provided the information.

Appearance — hygiene/grooming, dress, posture, facial expression, apparent age vs. stated age, body habitus, and distinguishing features (tattoos, scars, track marks) — offers insight into level of functioning and mood before the patient speaks.

3 · Behavior & Speech

Behavior covers eye contact, cooperativeness/attitude, psychomotor activity, movement disorders, body language, gait, level of arousal, and rapport. Watch specifically for tardive dyskinesia (lip smacking, tongue writhing, grimacing) and akathisia/dystonia from antipsychotics.

Acute agitation changes the path
If the patient is acutely agitated, combative, or poses immediate risk, move directly to the Safety (SI/HI) assessment rather than continuing sequentially through speech, mood, affect, thought, and cognition.

Speech is assessed for rate, rhythm, volume, content, tone, fluency, articulation, and latency. Normal speech rate is 100-150 words/minute; rapid/pressured speech suggests mania or anxiety, while slow, hesitant speech with increased latency suggests depression or sedation.

4 · Mood & Affect

Mood vs. Affect
Mood = what the patient TELLS you (subjective, sustained emotional state, document in their own words). Affect = what you OBSERVE (objective, moment-to-moment intensity, quality, range, and congruence with the stated mood).

Ask directly: "How is your mood today?" "Have you felt sad, discouraged, anxious, or worried?" Document the answer in quotes. Common descriptors: euthymic, depressed, irritable, anxious, euphoric, apathetic, labile.

DomainRange of findings
IntensityNormal, flat, blunted, exaggerated
FluctuationStable vs. labile (mania, TBI, pseudobulbar affect)
RangeBroad/full, restricted, flat, expansive
CongruenceMood-congruent vs. mood-incongruent (e.g., laughing while describing sadness → psychosis)

5 · Thought & Cognition

Thought process = HOW the patient thinks — the form and flow. Goal-directed/linear is normal; circumstantial, tangential, loose associations, flight of ideas (mania), thought blocking (schizophrenia), and clang associations are abnormal patterns.

Command hallucinations — ask specifically
Thought content = WHAT the patient thinks — delusions, obsessions, and perceptual disturbances (hallucinations across all sensory modalities). Command hallucinations must be asked about directly and their content documented; do not assume they are absent just because general hallucinations were screened.

6 · Safety — SI/HI Screening

Screen every patient age 12+
Joint Commission NPSG.15.01.01 (renumbered NPSG.08.01.01 in 2026) requires screening all patients age 12+ evaluated for a primary behavioral health condition using a validated tool (C-SSRS, PHQ-2, Patient Safety Screener, or ASQ). C-SSRS is the most widely validated tool and is explicitly endorsed by this standard. A positive screen requires a full risk assessment.
  • "Do you feel that life is not worth living?"
  • "Have you had thoughts of hurting yourself?"
  • "Have you thought about killing yourself? Do you have a plan? The means?"
  • "Have you ever attempted suicide before?" (previous attempt = strongest predictor)
  • "Have you had thoughts of hurting anyone else? Is there anyone you feel angry enough to harm?"
  • Ask specifically about firearms access — for both SI and HI
  1. Identify warning signs that a crisis is developing
  2. Internal coping strategies
  3. People and social settings that provide distraction
  4. People to contact for help
  5. Professionals and agencies to contact
  6. Make the environment safe (lethal means counseling)
Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention
The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) above is 6 steps, completed in about 20 minutes. SPI+ (adding structured follow-up phone calls) cut suicidal behavior by ~50% in a VA cohort (NNT 44, Stanley/Brown JAMA Psychiatry 2018). Provide the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and arrange outpatient psychiatric follow-up within 48-72 hours, plus a structured follow-up call at 24-72 hours.
Active SI — immediate actions
1:1 sitter/continuous observation, remove all potential means (sharps, cords, medications), place in a ligature-resistant room. Consult psychiatry for risk assessment, medication evaluation, and voluntary vs. involuntary commitment determination. Consider labs: urine drug screen, blood alcohol, TSH/CBC/BMP, acetaminophen/salicylate levels if ingestion suspected.
Homicidal ideation — Tarasoff & safety
Ensure staff safety (do not interview alone), search for weapons per protocol, place 1:1 observation. Duty-to-warn (Tarasoff) obligations are mandatory in ~23 states (e.g., CA, NJ), permissive in TX/NY/CT/FL/OR, and absent in ME/NC/ND/NV. Texas specifically: *Thapar v. Zezulka* (1999) — the Texas Supreme Court declined to adopt a Tarasoff duty; disclosure is permitted, not mandated. Discharge the duty (where it exists) by warning the victim, notifying law enforcement, and/or hospitalizing the patient — and document threat content, target, decision, and actions taken regardless of jurisdiction.

7 · Insight, Labs & Disposition

Insight ranges good/complete → fair/partial → poor/limited → absent. Judgment is assessed via hypothetical scenarios and recent decisions. Capacity assessment relies on 4 criteria: express a consistent choice, understand the relevant information, appreciate how it applies to their situation, and rationally manipulate the information. Synthesize all MSE findings into an overall risk formulation — danger to self, danger to others, or gravely disabled.

ACEP Clinical Policy and AAEM Position (consistent, 2024) agree: routine laboratory testing — including urine drug screens (Level C) — is NOT indicated for the alert, cooperative, asymptomatic adult psychiatric patient. Testing should be driven by history, vital signs, and physical exam findings. Abnormal findings should prompt medical workup before attributing symptoms to primary psychiatric illness.

TestIndication
CBC / BMPInfection, anemia, electrolytes, renal function
TSHThyroid symptoms, mood syndrome, unclear cause
Urine drug screen / EtOHSubstance-induced symptoms or intoxication concern
B12/folateCognitive/mood changes, malnutrition risk
Acetaminophen/salicylateSuspected overdose
CT headFocal neuro findings, trauma, delirium features, elderly late-onset new psychiatric symptoms

Documentation must include complete history, all 10 MSE components, initial diagnosis, capacity evaluation, and initial treatment plan. Use objective, descriptive language (not "seems sad") and include direct quotes for mood, thought content, or delusions.

Discharge if
No active SI/HI, not acutely intoxicated, adequate support system, patient agrees to a completed safety plan, and reliable outpatient follow-up is arranged. Provide means restriction counseling, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, and psychiatry follow-up within 48-72 hours. Return precautions: worsening SI/HI, inability to maintain safety, new/worsening psychosis, medication adverse effects.
Admit if
Active SI/HI with plan or intent, acute psychosis requiring stabilization, inability to care for self (gravely disabled), failed outpatient management, or need for monitored medication adjustment/withdrawal management. Voluntary requires documented capacity to consent; involuntary criteria vary by state (typically danger to self/others or gravely disabled) and require physician certification.
Other documentation pitfalls3 items
Do NOT confuse the MSE with the MMSE — the MSE is a comprehensive psychological-state exam; the MMSE is a cognitive screen only.
Do NOT document mood and affect interchangeably — mood is the patient's own words (subjective), affect is your observation (objective).
Do NOT skip collateral sources — family, EMS, pharmacy records, and prior ED visits often reveal risk factors the patient does not volunteer.
