Overview

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) affect 15-25% of diabetics and precede 85% of diabetes-related amputations. The 'diabetic foot triad' driving every ED evaluation: neuropathy (loss of protective sensation), peripheral arterial disease (ischemia), and infection (polymicrobial, often indolent).[1][2]

  1. Rule out limb-threatening emergencies (necrotizing fasciitis, wet gangrene, severe ischemia, compartment syndrome).
  2. Classify wound severity (Wagner, UT, or SINBAD).
  3. Assess vascular status — TBI, not ABI.
  4. Determine IDSA/IWGDF infection severity (Grade 1-4).
  5. Evaluate for osteomyelitis (probe-to-bone + imaging).
  6. Recognize acute Charcot neuroarthropathy — 25% is misdiagnosed.

2 · Wound examination & classification

Systematic exam: location (forefoot best prognosis; midfoot = Charcot zone; hindfoot/heel worst), size (>2 cm² is an independent osteomyelitis risk factor), depth (superficial / deep to tendon-joint-fascia / bone visible or palpable via blunt probe), tissue type (granulating/slough/eschar/exposed structures), surrounding skin (erythema extent, warmth, induration, callus), exudate, and neuropathy (10g monofilament, 128 Hz tuning fork, ankle reflexes).[1][7]

Wagner gradeDescription
0Pre-ulcerative (intact skin, deformity, cellulitis)
1Superficial ulcer (epidermis/dermis only)
2Deep ulcer (tendon, joint, fascia — no abscess/osteo)
3Deep ulcer WITH abscess, osteomyelitis, or tendonitis
4Partial gangrene (toes or forefoot)
5Extensive gangrene (entire foot)

University of Texas (UT) system: Grade 0-3 (depth) × Stage A-D (complications). Stage A = no infection/no ischemia; B = infection; C = ischemia; D = BOTH infection AND ischemia (worst prognosis).[7]

4 · Vascular assessment — TBI over ABI

ABI is unreliable in diabetics
Medial arterial calcification makes vessels non-compressible, falsely elevating ABI. ABI sensitivity for PAD in diabetics: ~35%. Digital arteries are typically spared from calcification, so Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) is more accurate in diabetics and ESRD patients.[5][6]

5 · Osteomyelitis workup

Osteomyelitis complicates 20-60% of diabetic foot infections. High-risk features: ulcer present >2 weeks, ulcer size >2 cm², positive probe-to-bone test, visible bone or 'sausage-shaped' toe, ESR >70 mm/hr, prior history of foot osteomyelitis, Wagner Grade 3+.[12][13]

Probe-to-bone (PTB) test
Insert a sterile blunt probe into the ulcer base — positive = hard, gritty sensation of bone. Sensitivity: 87% | Specificity: 83% (positive LR 4.3-9.4). Negative PTB does NOT exclude osteomyelitis — best used in combination with imaging.[12]

6 · Charcot foot — the commonly missed diagnosis

Charcot neuroarthropathy — up to 13% of diabetics with neuropathy
Classic triad: unilateral swollen foot/ankle, warmth (2-8°C warmer than the contralateral foot), and erythema — usually WITHOUT an open wound. 25% of acute Charcot is MISDIAGNOSED (as cellulitis, gout, DVT, sprain) — average delay to diagnosis: 7 months. Risk factors: diabetic peripheral neuropathy, long-standing diabetes (>10 years), tight glycemic control, prior foot surgery/trauma, obesity, renal transplant recipients. Autonomic neuropathy → increased blood flow → bone resorption; sensory neuropathy → continued weight-bearing despite injury. Most common location: midfoot (Lisfranc joints).[14][15]

Acute Charcot — strict non-weight-bearing NOW
CAM boot or wheelchair; strict non-weight-bearing. Continued walking causes permanent deformity — patient education is critical. Optimize glycemic control. Urgent podiatry/foot-ankle ortho referral within 24-48 hours — do NOT discharge without a clear follow-up plan. Total contact cast (TCC) is gold-standard offloading but is not typically applied in the ED. Consider admission if unable to comply with non-weight-bearing, inadequate social support, concurrent infection, or severe deformity with skin compromise.[14][15]
Charcot not currently suspected
Continue the standard wound/infection/vascular workup above. Reconsider Charcot if the foot is red, hot, and swollen WITHOUT an open wound, pain is minimal, and labs/vitals are normal.

When both Charcot and infection are possible: treat the infection AND offload for Charcot — this combination is more common than isolated Charcot and carries a worse prognosis. On MRI, Charcot shows diffuse marrow edema (often bilateral, no focal bone destruction) versus osteomyelitis' focal bone destruction adjacent to the ulcer. When uncertain, treat for BOTH until proven otherwise — better to offload unnecessarily than to miss Charcot.[14][15]

Core multidisciplinary team: emergency medicine, podiatry/foot surgery, vascular surgery, infectious disease, endocrinology, wound care. 8 · Disposition

Outpatient (ALL must be met): mild infection (Grade 2) or uninfected, no systemic toxicity, no deep abscess requiring I&D, adequate perfusion (or PAD being addressed), adequate home support, follow-up within 48-72 hours, can obtain antibiotics, no prior failed outpatient treatment. Admission (ANY warrants it): severe infection (Grade 4), moderate infection with complications, need for IV antibiotics or urgent surgery, critical limb ischemia, acute Charcot with inability to offload, metabolic instability (DKA/HHS), inadequate home support, or failed outpatient management.[1][9]

  • Wound cleaned and dressed appropriately.
  • Offloading device fitted (CAM boot, half-shoe, or referral for TCC).
  • Antibiotics prescribed (if infected) with clear duration.
  • Follow-up arranged within 48-72 hours (wound clinic, podiatry, or PCP; vascular surgery if PAD).
  • Written return precautions: spreading redness, increasing pain/swelling/drainage, fever >38°C, new necrosis, foul odor, feeling generally unwell.
  • Surgical/podiatry consult if abscess, necrosis, or osteo; vascular surgery if PAD; ID if osteomyelitis or complex infection.
  • IV antibiotics per IDSA grade; glucose monitoring with sliding scale or insulin drip.
  • Strict non-weight-bearing or offloading; daily wound checks and dressing changes.
  • Labs: CBC, BMP, ESR, CRP daily initially; X-ray if not done, MRI if osteomyelitis suspected; DVT prophylaxis.
  • Discharge when improving, tolerating oral antibiotics, reliable follow-up arranged, offloading plan in place, glucose controlled.
  • Sepsis bundle if applicable (3-hour and 6-hour); broad-spectrum antibiotics STAT; surgical consultation STAT.
  • Central line and arterial line PRN; aggressive fluid resuscitation; vasopressors if needed.
  • Glucose management (target 140-180 mg/dL).
  • Consultations: surgery (emergent), vascular surgery if ischemic, infectious disease, endocrinology if DKA/HHS.
  • Prepare for OR: NPO status, type and screen (consider crossmatch), consent for amputation if indicated.

10 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls7 items
Do NOT treat uninfected ulcers with antibiotics — even if osteomyelitis is seen on imaging. Wait for infection to declare itself clinically.
Do NOT delay emergent surgery for necrotizing fasciitis to obtain imaging or a LRINEC result — LRINEC only supports the diagnosis.
Do NOT trust ABI alone in a diabetic — if ABI >1.4 or PAD is still clinically suspected, obtain TBI or TcPO2.
Do NOT let a negative probe-to-bone test alone exclude osteomyelitis — combine with imaging.
Do NOT discharge suspected acute Charcot without strict non-weight-bearing and a confirmed 24-48 hour podiatry/orthopedic follow-up plan.
Do NOT rely on advanced dressings, NPWT, silver, honey, or hyperbaric O2 for infection control — no strong evidence supports any of them for that purpose.
Do NOT skip offloading — it is as important as antibiotics for neuropathic ulcer healing.
2 additional pearls2 items
Necrotizing fasciitis pain is out of proportion early, but can become absent late (nerve destruction) — do not be falsely reassured by reduced pain in a rapidly progressing wound.
Multidisciplinary care (ID, vascular, podiatry, wound care, endocrine) improves outcomes across every module of diabetic foot wound management.

2 | Deep ulcer (tendon, joint, fascia — no abscess/osteo)

3 | Deep ulcer WITH abscess, osteomyelitis, or tendonitis

4 | Partial gangrene (toes or forefoot)

5 | Extensive gangrene (entire foot)

University of Texas (UT) system: Grade 0-3 (depth) × Stage A-D (complications). Stage A = no infection/no ischemia; B = infection; C = ischemia; D = BOTH infection AND ischemia (worst prognosis).[7]

Score <3: 60% heal at 12 weeks, 0.7% major amputation. Score ≥3: 35% heal at 12 weeks, 2.7% major amputation.[7][8]

Even if osteomyelitis is seen on imaging — antibiotics are not indicated for Grade 1 (uninfected) wounds.[1][9]

Grade 4 (Severe) criteria: Temperature >38°C or <36°C; Heart rate >90 bpm; Respiratory rate >20/min; WBC >12,000 or <4,000 or >10% bands; severe hyperglycemia, acidosis, or azotemia.[1][9]

4 · Vascular assessment — TBI over ABI

Medial arterial calcification makes vessels non-compressible, falsely elevating ABI. ABI sensitivity for PAD in diabetics: ~35%. Digital arteries are typically spared from calcification, so Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) is more accurate in diabetics and ESRD patients.[5][6]

WIfI Classification (SVS): Wound (0-3) + Ischemia (0-3) + foot Infection (0-3). Stage 4 carries a 38% one-year amputation risk and guides timing/aggressiveness of revascularization.[5]

Mild-moderate PAD (TBI 0.30-0.59, TcPO2 25-40): optimize wound care and infection control; vascular surgery referral (semi-urgent, within 1-2 weeks); statin regardless of LDL, antiplatelet (aspirin/clopidogrel), smoking cessation, glucose and BP control. Consider vascular imaging if not improving at 4 weeks.

Severe PAD / CLTI (TBI <0.30, toe pressure <30, TcPO2 <25): STAT vascular surgery consultation, CTA or duplex imaging. Avoid compression bandages, heat/cold application, and elevation (worsens ischemia). If non-revascularizable, discuss realistic prognosis; primary amputation may be the best option.

Non-compressible vessels (ABI >1.4, common in diabetes/ESRD/advanced age): obtain TBI (preferred) or TcPO2 for accurate assessment. If unavailable, use clinical pulse exam, capillary refill, and Doppler waveform quality (monophasic suggests significant PAD even with a 'normal' ABI), and refer for formal vascular lab evaluation.

5 · Osteomyelitis workup

Osteomyelitis complicates 20-60% of diabetic foot infections. High-risk features: ulcer present >2 weeks, ulcer size >2 cm², positive probe-to-bone test, visible bone or 'sausage-shaped' toe, ESR >70 mm/hr, prior history of foot osteomyelitis, Wagner Grade 3+.[12][13]

Insert a sterile blunt probe into the ulcer base — positive = hard, gritty sensation of bone. Sensitivity: 87% | Specificity: 83% (positive LR 4.3-9.4). Negative PTB does NOT exclude osteomyelitis — best used in combination with imaging.[12]

6 · Charcot foot — the commonly missed diagnosis

Classic triad: unilateral swollen foot/ankle, warmth (2-8°C warmer than the contralateral foot), and erythema — usually WITHOUT an open wound. 25% of acute Charcot is MISDIAGNOSED (as cellulitis, gout, DVT, sprain) — average delay to diagnosis: 7 months. Risk factors: diabetic peripheral neuropathy, long-standing diabetes (>10 years), tight glycemic control, prior foot surgery/trauma, obesity, renal transplant recipients. Autonomic neuropathy → increased blood flow → bone resorption; sensory neuropathy → continued weight-bearing despite injury. Most common location: midfoot (Lisfranc joints).[14][15]

Feature | Acute Charcot | Osteomyelitis/Cellulitis

Open wound | Usually ABSENT | Usually PRESENT

Fever | Absent | May be present

WBC | Normal | Often elevated

ESR/CRP | Mildly elevated (<40) | Significantly elevated (>70)

Pain | Minimal (neuropathy) | Variable

Pulses | Often bounding | Variable

Stage | Name | Clinical | Radiographic

0 | Pre-clinical | Warm, swollen, painful | Normal X-ray, marrow edema on MRI

1 | Development/Fragmentation | Red, hot, swollen | Osteopenia, fragmentation, subluxation

2 | Coalescence | Decreased warmth/swelling | Absorption of debris, sclerosis

3 | Reconstruction | No inflammation | Remodeling, fusion, stable deformity

CAM boot or wheelchair; strict non-weight-bearing. Continued walking causes permanent deformity — patient education is critical. Optimize glycemic control. Urgent podiatry/foot-ankle ortho referral within 24-48 hours — do NOT discharge without a clear follow-up plan. Total contact cast (TCC) is gold-standard offloading but is not typically applied in the ED. Consider admission if unable to comply with non-weight-bearing, inadequate social support, concurrent infection, or severe deformity with skin compromise.[14][15]

Continue the standard wound/infection/vascular workup above. Reconsider Charcot if the foot is red, hot, and swollen WITHOUT an open wound, pain is minimal, and labs/vitals are normal.

When both Charcot and infection are possible: treat the infection AND offload for Charcot — this combination is more common than isolated Charcot and carries a worse prognosis. On MRI, Charcot shows diffuse marrow edema (often bilateral, no focal bone destruction) versus osteomyelitis' focal bone destruction adjacent to the ulcer. When uncertain, treat for BOTH until proven otherwise — better to offload unnecessarily than to miss Charcot.[14][15]

7 · Treatment pillars, surgery, and offloading

Core multidisciplinary team: emergency medicine, podiatry/foot surgery, vascular surgery, infectious disease, endocrinology, wound care. Treatment pillars: infection control, vascular optimization, offloading (the most underutilized intervention), wound care, glucose control, and risk-factor modification.[1][10]

8 · Disposition

Outpatient (ALL must be met): mild infection (Grade 2) or uninfected, no systemic toxicity, no deep abscess requiring I&D, adequate perfusion (or PAD being addressed), adequate home support, follow-up within 48-72 hours, can obtain antibiotics, no prior failed outpatient treatment. Admission (ANY warrants it): severe infection (Grade 4), moderate infection with complications, need for IV antibiotics or urgent surgery, critical limb ischemia, acute Charcot with inability to offload, metabolic instability (DKA/HHS), inadequate home support, or failed outpatient management.[1][9]

  • Wound cleaned and dressed appropriately.
  • Offloading device fitted (CAM boot, half-shoe, or referral for TCC).
  • Antibiotics prescribed (if infected) with clear duration.
  • Follow-up arranged within 48-72 hours (wound clinic, podiatry, or PCP; vascular surgery if PAD).
  • Written return precautions: spreading redness, increasing pain/swelling/drainage, fever >38°C, new necrosis, foul odor, feeling generally unwell.
  • Surgical/podiatry consult if abscess, necrosis, or osteo; vascular surgery if PAD; ID if osteomyelitis or complex infection.
  • IV antibiotics per IDSA grade; glucose monitoring with sliding scale or insulin drip.
  • Strict non-weight-bearing or offloading; daily wound checks and dressing changes.
  • Labs: CBC, BMP, ESR, CRP daily initially; X-ray if not done, MRI if osteomyelitis suspected; DVT prophylaxis.
  • Discharge when improving, tolerating oral antibiotics, reliable follow-up arranged, offloading plan in place, glucose controlled.
  • Sepsis bundle if applicable (3-hour and 6-hour); broad-spectrum antibiotics STAT; surgical consultation STAT.
  • Central line and arterial line PRN; aggressive fluid resuscitation; vasopressors if needed.
  • Glucose management (target 140-180 mg/dL).
  • Consultations: surgery (emergent), vascular surgery if ischemic, infectious disease, endocrinology if DKA/HHS.
  • Prepare for OR: NPO status, type and screen (consider crossmatch), consent for amputation if indicated.

9 · One-glance algorithm

Diabetic foot wound presents to ED — Assess the diabetic foot triad: neuropathy, PAD, infection.

Limb-threatening emergency present? — Necrotizing fasciitis, wet gangrene, or critical limb ischemia.

Necrotizing fasciitis / wet gangrene → emergent antibiotics + surgery — Nec fasc: vanc 25-30mg/kg load + pip-tazo + clindamycin. Wet gangrene: vanc 15-20mg/kg + pip-tazo. Both → ICU + emergent surgery.

Critical limb ischemia → STAT vascular consult — Ankle pressure <50, ABI <0.4, toe pressure <30, TcPO2 <25. Avoid compression/heat/cold.

Wound exam + Wagner/SINBAD classification — Location, size, depth, tissue type, exudate, neuropathy testing.

IDSA infection severity (Grade 1-4)? — Grade 1 uninfected → Grade 4 severe/systemic toxicity.

Grade-specific antibiotics (or none if uninfected) — Grade1: none. Grade2: oral 7-14d. Grade3: oral/IV 1-2wk(-4wk). Grade4: vanc+pip-tazo/meropenem 10d(-6wk), STAT surgical consult.

Vascular status (TBI preferred over ABI)? — Adequate / mild-moderate PAD / severe PAD-CLTI / non-compressible.

PAD management + revascularization urgency — Mild-mod: semi-urgent referral 1-2wk. Severe: STAT vascular consult. Non-compressible: obtain TBI/TcPO2.

Osteomyelitis workup: probe-to-bone + imaging — PTB sens 87%/spec 83%. X-ray → MRI if negative but concerning → bone biopsy if definitive dx/culture needed.

Screen for acute Charcot neuroarthropathy — Red, hot, swollen foot WITHOUT open wound. 25% misdiagnosed. If suspected: strict non-weight-bearing now.

Treatment pillars: surgery indications, wound care, offloading — Offloading (TCC 90% healing) is as important as antibiotics.

Disposition? — Discharge / admit floor / admit ICU.

Discharge with offloading + follow-up 48-72h

Admit floor — IV antibiotics, consults, offloading

Admit ICU — sepsis bundle, emergent surgery

10 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT treat uninfected ulcers with antibiotics — even if osteomyelitis is seen on imaging. Wait for infection to declare itself clinically.

Do NOT delay emergent surgery for necrotizing fasciitis to obtain imaging or a LRINEC result — LRINEC only supports the diagnosis.

Do NOT trust ABI alone in a diabetic — if ABI >1.4 or PAD is still clinically suspected, obtain TBI or TcPO2.

Do NOT let a negative probe-to-bone test alone exclude osteomyelitis — combine with imaging.

Do NOT discharge suspected acute Charcot without strict non-weight-bearing and a confirmed 24-48 hour podiatry/orthopedic follow-up plan.

Do NOT rely on advanced dressings, NPWT, silver, honey, or hyperbaric O2 for infection control — no strong evidence supports any of them for that purpose.

Do NOT skip offloading — it is as important as antibiotics for neuropathic ulcer healing.

2 additional pearls

Necrotizing fasciitis pain is out of proportion early, but can become absent late (nerve destruction) — do not be falsely reassured by reduced pain in a rapidly progressing wound.

Multidisciplinary care (ID, vascular, podiatry, wound care, endocrine) improves outcomes across every module of diabetic foot wound management.
