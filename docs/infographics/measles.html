Measles: Isolate, Diagnose, Treat, PEP

ED patients with suspected or confirmed measles (rubeola), all ages, plus exposed contacts requiring post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) triage. Excludes non-measles exanthems once ruled out and routine well-child MMR scheduling outside an exposure/outbreak context.

⚠ Suspected measles → immediate airborne isolation (AIIR, N95/PAPR, patient surgical mask) BEFORE further workup, and report to the health department IMMEDIATELY — do not wait for lab confirmation.

1 · Recognition & isolation

Suspect measles with fever (often >104°F/40°C), the classic triad cough, coryza, conjunctivitis (the 3 Cs), and a maculopapular rash spreading cephalocaudally, especially in an unvaccinated or under-vaccinated patient with recent travel or exposure. Prodrome runs 2-4 days before rash; Koplik spots may appear 1-2 days before rash. R0 = 12-18 (extremely contagious), 90% attack rate in susceptible household contacts, contagious 4 days before to 4 days after rash onset.

Immediate airborne isolation
If measles is suspected, initiate airborne isolation before further workup: negative-pressure AIIR (≥6 air changes/hr, 12 preferred), N95/PAPR + gown/gloves for all staff, patient wears a surgical mask, minimize transport. Maintain isolation 4 days after rash onset (immunocompromised: duration of illness). Report to the local health department IMMEDIATELY — do not wait for lab confirmation.

2 · Immunity assessment & diagnosis

Evidence of presumptive immunity (any ONE): documented 2 MMR doses ≥28 days apart (1st dose at age ≥12 months), positive IgG, laboratory-confirmed prior measles, or birth before 1957 in the US (presumed immune from natural exposure).

If immunity present: breakthrough & DDxvaccine effectiveness, 6 alternative diagnoses

Vaccine effectiveness is 93% (1 dose) to 97% (2 doses); breakthrough measles is rare but possible and typically milder.

Koplik spots — pathognomonic
1-3mm grayish-white papules on the buccal mucosa opposite the upper molars — "salt grains on a red background." Appear 1-2 days before the rash, disappear 1-2 days after. Present in 60-70% of cases. Rash starts on the face/hairline/behind the ears, spreads cephalocaudally over 24-48h to trunk then extremities (including palms/soles), fades in the same order over 5-7 days.
Laboratory confirmationIgM vs PCR sensitivity/specificity

IgM serology: sensitivity 83-89%, specificity 95-99%; may be falsely negative in the first 72 hours after rash. RT-PCR (preferred): sensitivity 94%, specificity 99%; NP/throat swab AND urine, collected within 7 days of rash onset — urine + NP swab together improves sensitivity and allows outbreak genotyping.

3 · What to do now — this patient

Neurologic complication → encephalitis workup
Onset 3-10 days after rash: fever recrudescence, seizures (50%), altered mental status, focal deficits. Workup: CT head, LP (lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein), MRI, EEG. Supportive care and seizure management only — no proven specific therapy. Prognosis: 25% mortality, 25% permanent neurologic sequelae, 50% full recovery.
Respiratory complication → pneumonia workup
Leading cause of measles death (1 in 20 children). CXR, CBC, procalcitonin, blood cultures, ± respiratory viral panel. O2 target SpO2 ≥92%, IV fluids, vitamin A if not given. Antibiotics only for documented bacterial superinfection (ampicillin-sulbactam or ceftriaxone + vancomycin if MRSA concern).
High-risk or severe — hospitalize
IV fluids, O2 PRN (target SpO2 ≥92%), antipyretics, continuous monitoring. Vitamin A is MANDATORY for hospitalized patients. No specific antiviral (ribavirin is in-vitro only — ID consult if used). Antibiotics only for documented bacterial infection.
Uncomplicated — outpatient supportive care
Oral hydration, antipyretics, rest, dim lighting for photophobia. Give vitamin A to all children with measles, even in the US, especially with malnutrition risk, recent immigration, or suspected vitamin A deficiency. NOT antibiotics (unless bacterial superinfection documented), NOT steroids (no benefit, may prolong viral shedding), NOT antivirals. Strict home isolation 4 days after rash onset.

6 · Complications

Most common: otitis media (7-9%), pneumonia (1 in 20 children — leading cause of measles death, primary viral giant-cell or secondary bacterial), diarrhea (8%), croup, keratitis (can cause blindness with vitamin A deficiency). Neurologic: acute encephalitis (1 in 1,000 cases, onset 3-10 days post-rash, 25% mortality, 25% permanent sequelae). Overall death 1-3 per 1,000 cases in developed countries.

SSPE — late fatal complication1/100,000 historical, 1/609 infants <15mo

Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE): persistent CNS measles infection, onset 7-10 years after infection. Historical incidence 1 in 100,000; more recent data as high as 1 in 609 for infants infected before 15 months of age. Four-stage course (behavioral change → myoclonus/seizures → rigidity/autonomic instability → coma) is nearly always fatal within 1-3 years; no effective treatment; prevented only by measles vaccination (caused by wild-type virus only, not vaccine strains).

7 · Post-exposure prophylaxis

Exposure = same airspace as the infectious patient from 4 days before to 4 days after rash onset; virus remains airborne up to 2 hours. MMR window 72 hours; immune globulin window 6 days.

PEP: MMR vaccine (≤72h)
Eligible: age ≥6 months, no live-vaccine contraindications, not pregnant, not severely immunocompromised. Standard SC MMR dose. If 6-11 months: dose does not count toward the routine series (revaccinate at 12-15 months). Quarantine 21 days from last exposure regardless of PEP.
PEP: Immune globulin (72h-6 days)
IGIM 0.5 mL/kg (max 15 mL) for infants <6 months, or 6-11 months if >72h post-exposure. IVIG 400 mg/kg for pregnant women (any trimester), severely immunocompromised patients, or when the IGIM volume would be excessive. Wait 6 months (IGIM) or 8 months (IVIG) before giving MMR. Quarantine 28 days with immune globulin (vs 21 without) — incubation may be prolonged. Healthcare workers: exclude from work day 5-21 after exposure (day 28 if IG given).

PEP: outside the window (>6 days)

Offer MMR vaccination to protect against future exposures if not contraindicated.

8 · Public health reporting & disposition

Mandatory immediate reporting
Measles is nationally notifiable — report to the local health department the same day, plus state epidemiologist and CDC. Include demographics, vaccination history, travel history (2-3 weeks prior), exposure history, contact list, and labs when available. Contact tracing covers 4 days before rash to present. Outbreak definition: >=3 cases linked in time and place. Do NOT wait for lab confirmation to report suspected cases.
Admit if
Respiratory compromise or hypoxia, severe dehydration (unable to maintain PO), altered mental status or neurologic concerns, high-risk patient (immunocompromised, pregnant, extremes of age), cannot maintain isolation at home, or a suspected/confirmed complication. AIIR, N95/PAPR staff, continuous pulse oximetry, neuro checks if encephalitis is a concern, vitamin A day 1+2, antibiotics only for documented bacterial infection, ID + public health ± pulmonology/neurology consults.
Discharge if
Maintaining adequate hydration, no respiratory distress, no high-risk features, able to isolate at home, reliable follow-up. Strict home isolation 4 days after rash onset (separate room; no school/work/public places/healthcare visits without calling ahead); household members assessed for immunity; offer the age-appropriate 2-dose vitamin A regimen at discharge; return precautions for respiratory distress, inability to keep fluids down, confusion/drowsiness, seizure, or worsening/new symptoms; PCP follow-up in 2-3 days.
Other pitfalls4 items
Do NOT delay isolation for lab confirmation — isolate on clinical suspicion alone.
Do NOT give antibiotics or steroids prophylactically — antibiotics only for documented bacterial superinfection; steroids may prolong viral shedding.
Do NOT substitute cod liver oil for vitamin A — variable content and toxicity risk.
Do NOT miss that healthcare workers need documented immunity (2 MMR doses or positive titers) — unprotected staff are a major exposure risk.
