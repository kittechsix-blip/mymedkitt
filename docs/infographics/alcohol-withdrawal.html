Alcohol Withdrawal — ED Management

Adults ≥18 with alcohol withdrawal in the ED/inpatient setting. Diagnosis of exclusion — excludes hepatic encephalopathy (near-opposite pathophysiology), infection, trauma, and other metabolic/drug causes of altered mental status.

⚠ Give thiamine 100-500 mg IV BEFORE glucose/dextrose — prevents precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy.
⚠ Hepatic encephalopathy is a STRONG CONTRAINDICATION to phenobarbital and caution with any GABAergic drug — HE (excess GABA → obtundation) is a near-opposite pathology to withdrawal (inadequate GABA → hyperexcitability); giving GABAergics to HE risks prolonged coma.
⚠ Phenobarbital contraindications: severe respiratory depression without ventilatory support, porphyria, or a patient who already received a large benzodiazepine dose (synergistic sedation risk).
⚠ Withdrawal seizure: phenobarbital 15-20 mg/kg IV treats both the seizure AND the underlying withdrawal. Phenytoin is NOT effective for alcohol withdrawal seizures — do not use.
⚠ NARD (non-alcohol-related delirium): if agitation persists despite phenobarbital >20 mg/kg without improvement, STOP escalating GABAergic medications — the diagnosis may not be alcohol withdrawal.
⚠ Refractory DT (phenobarbital added to benzodiazepines) is synergistic — respiratory depression is common and intubation is frequently required.

Initial assessment

Alcohol withdrawal occurs when chronic alcohol use is abruptly reduced or stopped. Chronic alcohol down-regulates GABA receptors and up-regulates glutamate receptors; cessation unmasks these adaptations, producing CNS hyperexcitability ranging from mild tremor to delirium tremens (DT).

Thiamine BEFORE glucose
Give thiamine 100-500 mg IV before glucose/dextrose in any alcohol patient — glucose metabolism consumes remaining thiamine stores and can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy. Also obtain fingerstick glucose, electrolytes (Ca/Mg/Phos), CBC, INR, LFTs, and CXR; consider CT head to exclude subdural hematoma.
Onset windowPresentation
6+ hoursMinor symptoms: tremor, anxiety, insomnia, diaphoresis
8-12 hoursAlcoholic hallucinosis (visual > auditory, clear sensorium)
12-48 hoursWithdrawal seizures (usually generalized tonic-clonic)
48-96 hoursDelirium tremens (hallucinations + disorientation + autonomic instability)

Admission blood alcohol level >200 mg/dL predicts high withdrawal risk — withdrawal can begin while BAL is still elevated.

  • How much does the patient drink? What pattern (daily vs binge)?
  • Does the patient withdraw when they stop drinking?
  • History of withdrawal seizures, DT, or intubation?
  • Verify EMR documentation — "alcoholism" may be years outdated
  • Obtain collateral from family/friends if needed

Alcohol withdrawal is a diagnosis of exclusion. Consider infection (meningitis), trauma (intracranial hemorrhage), metabolic derangements, drug overdose, or hepatic failure. Clues AGAINST withdrawal: patient becomes somnolent after a moderate-low dose of benzodiazepine (true withdrawal shows benzodiazepine tolerance); patient remains agitated despite >15 mg/kg phenobarbital; delirium without other withdrawal features (no hypertension, no tremor).

Diagnostic uncertainty — midazolam test dose

IV Midazolam 2-5 mg IV can help clarify the diagnosis. If the patient becomes sedated → withdrawal is LESS likely (true withdrawal patients exhibit benzodiazepine tolerance). If there is a favorable response (symptom improvement without oversedation) → withdrawal is likely; proceed to severity assessment.

Hepatic encephalopathy — contraindication screen

HE vs withdrawal are near-opposite pathologies
Alcohol withdrawal: inadequate GABA → hyperexcitability. Hepatic encephalopathy: excess GABA → obtundation. Giving GABAergic medications (benzodiazepines or barbiturates) to a patient with HE risks prolonged coma.
  • Advanced cirrhosis (ascites, jaundice, coagulopathy)
  • Asterixis
  • No tremor, no autonomic hyperactivity
  • Elevated ammonia
  • History of prior hepatic encephalopathy episodes
If HE is suspected
Treat the encephalopathy (lactulose, rifaximin). Do NOT give phenobarbital. If a benzodiazepine is necessary, use only small titrated doses of Lorazepam (short-acting, no active metabolites).

Severity assessment

PAWSS Calculator — score ≥4 predicts clinically significant withdrawal. CIWA-Ar Calculator for ongoing monitoring. Minor symptoms: tremor, anxiety, insomnia, GI upset, headache, diaphoresis (CIWA-Ar <15). Moderate-severe: marked autonomic instability, hallucinosis, or agitated delirium. DT: hallucinations + disorientation + tachycardia + hypertension + hyperthermia + diaphoresis.

Withdrawal seizure management

Withdrawal seizures tend to recur — up to one-third progress to DT if untreated. Usually generalized tonic-clonic, brief, self-limiting, peak risk 12-48h after last drink, tend to cluster (2-6 seizures). Recurrent or prolonged seizures should prompt investigation for structural/infectious causes.

Phenobarbital
15-20 mg/kg IV at 50-100 mg/min
IV
Treats both the seizure AND underlying withdrawal. Provides ongoing seizure prophylaxis via long half-life auto-taper.
Phenytoin is NOT effective
Phenytoin is NOT effective for alcohol withdrawal seizures — do not use. Pyridoxine 100 mg IV/PO may be considered (pyridoxine deficiency in alcoholics may contribute to seizures).

If phenobarbital is unavailable: Lorazepam 4 mg IV or Diazepam 10 mg IV — benzodiazepines have lower anti-seizure efficacy in alcohol withdrawal due to tolerance.

After the seizure is controlled and phenobarbital loaded (15-20 mg/kg), assess for ongoing withdrawal symptoms. Patients who have had a withdrawal seizure are at high risk for DT and generally should be admitted.

Post-seizure — stable

Seizure controlled with phenobarbital loading (15-20 mg/kg). The long half-life (~3-4 days) provides ongoing seizure prophylaxis via auto-taper. Admit for observation (minimum 24 hours). Do NOT add benzodiazepines on top of a large phenobarbital load (synergistic sedation). Communicate the total phenobarbital dose to the receiving team.

Treatment strategy

Phenobarbital monotherapy: dual mechanism (↑GABA + ↓glutamate via AMPA/kainate), no paradoxical agitation, predictable pharmacokinetics with measurable levels, auto-taper. Benzodiazepine-first: traditional, most widely studied, preferred for diagnostic uncertainty or advanced cirrhosis; ~5-10% of patients are benzodiazepine-refractory and require phenobarbital rescue.

Phenobarbital — contraindication screen

  • Hepatic encephalopathy (strong contraindication)
  • Severe respiratory depression without ventilatory support
  • Porphyria
  • Already received a large dose of benzodiazepines (synergistic sedation risk)

Use caution in: older adults (>65), cardiovascular disease/COPD, volume depletion, advanced cirrhosis without encephalopathy.

ICU admission

Continuous monitoring: vitals, SpO2, neurologic status. Continue treatment pathway (phenobarbital or benzodiazepine). Frequent reassessment using RASS (preferred over CIWA-Ar for intubated patients). Aggressive electrolyte repletion. Thiamine 500 mg IV q8h until alert.

  • Aspiration pneumonia
  • Rhabdomyolysis (from prolonged agitation/restraints)
  • Cardiac dysrhythmias (correct Mg/K first)
  • Hyperthermia >40°C associated with increased mortality

Transfer to floor when RASS 0 to -1 on a stable medication regimen. Continue oral phenobarbital or benzodiazepine taper. Initiate AUD medication before discharge if appropriate.

Floor admission

Transition to oral medications: Phenobarbital 130 mg PO/IM PRN for breakthrough symptoms, OR Chlordiazepoxide 25-50 mg PO q6h with taper (if patient did NOT receive phenobarbital loading). CIWA-Ar monitoring q4-6h. Continue thiamine and electrolyte repletion.

Communicate to the floor team
Total cumulative phenobarbital dose given. Avoid benzodiazepines if the patient received a large phenobarbital load — phenobarbital auto-tapers and additional doses may not be needed.

Discharge planning and AUD medication

  • Withdrawal symptoms resolved and tolerating oral medications
  • Electrolytes corrected
  • Thiamine 100 mg PO daily × 5 days, folate 1 mg PO daily, multivitamin daily
  • If on phenobarbital: auto-taper provides ongoing coverage for days
  • Follow-up within 1 week: primary care, addiction medicine, AA/peer support, social work
Kindling
Risk of future withdrawal episodes increases with each successive episode ("kindling"). Counsel on medication adherence for AUD. Return precautions: seizure, confusion, hallucinations.

Medication for Alcohol Use Disorder (MAUD) should be offered to patients with moderate-severe AUD or those who request medical therapy. In-hospital initiation: patients are sober and receptive, side effects can be managed, it is associated with reduced ED visits and readmissions, and combination with psychosocial interventions is ideal.

Contraindications to in-hospital MAUD initiation
Patient actively refusing treatment. Unstable medical condition requiring full focus. Unable to arrange follow-up.
Naltrexone (first-line)
25 mg PO daily × 3-7 days, then 50 mg PO daily (maintenance, max 100 mg daily)
PO
NNT 12 to prevent return to heavy drinking. Take with food. Contraindicated: current opioid use, LFTs >5× normal, Child-Pugh C cirrhosis, GFR <45 mL/min. LFTs at baseline, then q6 months. Minimum 6 months duration.
Acamprosate (cirrhosis-safe)
333 mg PO TID (start), then 666 mg PO TID (maintenance)
PO
NNT 9-12 to prevent return to any drinking. Not hepatically metabolized — safe in advanced cirrhosis. GFR 30-50: 333 mg TID. Contraindicated if GFR <30. No routine labs required. Minimum 6 months duration.
Other AUD medications (third-line)

Gabapentin: least evidence among AUD medications; may help residual withdrawal symptoms and neuropathy; misuse risk (~1%); renally cleared, avoid with opioid use. Topiramate: increased odds of maintaining abstinence (OR 1.88); requires slow titration; hepatically cleared, avoid in moderate-advanced cirrhosis. Disulfiram: aversion therapy, most toxic AUD agent (hepatotoxicity, disulfiram reactions), requires close LFT monitoring (baseline + 2 weeks + monthly) and supervised administration; multiple contraindications (cirrhosis, renal dysfunction, seizure disorder, coronary disease, psychosis). For most patients, naltrexone or acamprosate should be tried first.

If still refractory: propofol infusion — opens chloride channels independently of GABA and also antagonizes excitatory amino acids. Requires intubation and ICU.

ICU admission required, intubation likely. Once controlled, do NOT simultaneously titrate both phenobarbital and benzodiazepines — continue phenobarbital titration, avoid further benzodiazepines if possible.

Phenobarbital (ICU maintenance): 130-260 mg IV total per day, divided into 2-3 doses, × 3-5 days IV — This is the ongoing ICU maintenance regimen — distinct from the acute titration dose above. Consult medical toxicology or the poison center (1-800-222-1222) for refractory cases. Prolonged phenobarbital use induces hepatic enzymes — anticipate altered drug metabolism (e.g., monitor methadone levels if applicable).

Management algorithm

Alcohol Withdrawal: Initial Assessment — Thiamine BEFORE glucose; check glucose/electrolytes/LFTs; GABA down-regulation + glutamate up-regulation drives hyperexcitability.

Diagnostic confidence? — Diagnosis of exclusion — exclude infection, trauma, metabolic causes. Somnolence after low-dose benzodiazepine argues against withdrawal.

Hepatic encephalopathy — do NOT give phenobarbital — HE and withdrawal are near-opposite GABA states. Treat HE; if benzodiazepine needed, small titrated lorazepam only.

Severity assessment — PAWSS ≥4 predicts significant withdrawal. CIWA-Ar <15 = minor; ≥15 = moderate-severe/DT.

Outpatient: minor withdrawal / at-risk prophylaxis — Minor: chlordiazepoxide or phenobarbital taper. At-risk asymptomatic: phenobarbital 10-15 mg/kg divided or chlordiazepoxide prophylaxis — different regimens, not interchangeable.

Withdrawal seizure → phenobarbital 15-20 mg/kg IV; reassess — Phenobarbital treats seizure AND withdrawal. Phenytoin NOT effective. After load, assess: resolved & stable vs ongoing symptoms.

Seizure resolved — admit ≥24h — Auto-taper over 3-4 days. Do NOT add benzodiazepines on top of phenobarbital load.

Phenobarbital vs benzodiazepine-first? — Phenobarbital: dual GABA+glutamate mechanism, predictable levels, auto-taper. Benzodiazepine: traditional, ~5-10% refractory.

Phenobarbital contraindications? — Screen for hepatic encephalopathy, severe respiratory depression, porphyria, prior large benzodiazepine dose.

Phenobarbital: load 10 mg/kg, then 130 mg IV q30min to RASS 0/-1 — controlled? — 20 mg/kg = soft stop (re-evaluate diagnosis). 30 mg/kg = hard stop.

Phenobarbital — controlled, auto-tapers — Do NOT add benzodiazepines. Breakthrough: PB 130 mg IV/PO/IM PRN.

NARD: STOP escalating GABAergics, reassess diagnosis — Symptoms persisting beyond 20 mg/kg phenobarbital without improvement — likely NOT alcohol withdrawal. Haloperidol 2-5 mg IV PRN for agitation.

Benzodiazepine dosing (diazepam preferred / lorazepam if cirrhosis) — Symptom-triggered via CIWA-Ar ≥8, or front-loading in high-risk patients. Avoid IM diazepam.

Response to benzodiazepines? — Refractory: >50mg diazepam/1h or >200mg/3-4h (or lorazepam equivalents). ~5-10% refractory.

Controlled — supportive care — Thiamine (500mg q8h if AMS, else 100mg daily), aggressive Mg/K/phosphate repletion, fluids, nutrition.

Refractory DT: add phenobarbital to benzodiazepines → ICU — 130-260mg IV q15-20min, max 15mg/kg/24h with benzos. Synergistic respiratory depression — intubation often needed. Ketamine bridges while PB takes effect. ICU admission required; do not simultaneously titrate both agents once controlled.

Disposition — ICU vs floor vs discharge based on severity, refractory course, and comorbidities.

ICU admission — Continuous monitoring, RASS-guided, aggressive supportive care, watch for aspiration/rhabdo/dysrhythmia/hyperthermia.

Floor admission — Oral transition, CIWA-Ar q4-6h, communicate cumulative phenobarbital dose to receiving team.

Discharge + AUD medication — Thiamine/folate/MVI. Screen for AUD: naltrexone first-line, acamprosate if cirrhosis. Follow-up within 1 week.

Disposition

Disposition | Criteria

ICU | Delirium tremens; refractory withdrawal requiring phenobarbital + benzodiazepine; intubation/mechanical ventilation; significant comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, sepsis); recurrent seizures; cumulative phenobarbital >15 mg/kg with benzodiazepines

Floor | Controlled withdrawal requiring ongoing monitoring; transitioning to oral phenobarbital or benzodiazepine taper

Discharge | Mild withdrawal fully resolved; tolerating oral medications; safe social situation and follow-up arranged; consider initiating medication for AUD before discharge

ICU admission

Continuous monitoring: vitals, SpO2, neurologic status. Continue treatment pathway (phenobarbital or benzodiazepine). Frequent reassessment using RASS (preferred over CIWA-Ar for intubated patients). Aggressive electrolyte repletion. Thiamine 500 mg IV q8h until alert.

  • Aspiration pneumonia
  • Rhabdomyolysis (from prolonged agitation/restraints)
  • Cardiac dysrhythmias (correct Mg/K first)
  • Hyperthermia >40°C associated with increased mortality

Transfer to floor when RASS 0 to -1 on a stable medication regimen. Continue oral phenobarbital or benzodiazepine taper. Initiate AUD medication before discharge if appropriate.

Floor admission

Transition to oral medications: Phenobarbital 130 mg PO/IM PRN for breakthrough symptoms, OR Chlordiazepoxide 25-50 mg PO q6h with taper (if patient did NOT receive phenobarbital loading). CIWA-Ar monitoring q4-6h. Continue thiamine and electrolyte repletion.

Total cumulative phenobarbital dose given. Avoid benzodiazepines if the patient received a large phenobarbital load — phenobarbital auto-tapers and additional doses may not be needed.

Discharge planning and AUD medication

  • Withdrawal symptoms resolved and tolerating oral medications
  • Electrolytes corrected
  • Thiamine 100 mg PO daily × 5 days, folate 1 mg PO daily, multivitamin daily
  • If on phenobarbital: auto-taper provides ongoing coverage for days
  • Follow-up within 1 week: primary care, addiction medicine, AA/peer support, social work

Risk of future withdrawal episodes increases with each successive episode ("kindling"). Counsel on medication adherence for AUD. Return precautions: seizure, confusion, hallucinations.

Medication for Alcohol Use Disorder (MAUD) should be offered to patients with moderate-severe AUD or those who request medical therapy. In-hospital initiation: patients are sober and receptive, side effects can be managed, it is associated with reduced ED visits and readmissions, and combination with psychosocial interventions is ideal.

Patient actively refusing treatment. Unstable medical condition requiring full focus. Unable to arrange follow-up.

Naltrexone (first-line): 25 mg PO daily × 3-7 days, then 50 mg PO daily (maintenance, max 100 mg daily) PO — NNT 12 to prevent return to heavy drinking. Take with food. Contraindicated: current opioid use, LFTs >5× normal, Child-Pugh C cirrhosis, GFR <45 mL/min. LFTs at baseline, then q6 months. Minimum 6 months duration.

Acamprosate (cirrhosis-safe): 333 mg PO TID (start), then 666 mg PO TID (maintenance) PO — NNT 9-12 to prevent return to any drinking. Not hepatically metabolized — safe in advanced cirrhosis. GFR 30-50: 333 mg TID. Contraindicated if GFR <30. No routine labs required. Minimum 6 months duration.

Other AUD medications (third-line)

Gabapentin: least evidence among AUD medications; may help residual withdrawal symptoms and neuropathy; misuse risk (~1%); renally cleared, avoid with opioid use. Topiramate: increased odds of maintaining abstinence (OR 1.88); requires slow titration; hepatically cleared, avoid in moderate-advanced cirrhosis. Disulfiram: aversion therapy, most toxic AUD agent (hepatotoxicity, disulfiram reactions), requires close LFT monitoring (baseline + 2 weeks + monthly) and supervised administration; multiple contraindications (cirrhosis, renal dysfunction, seizure disorder, coronary disease, psychosis). For most patients, naltrexone or acamprosate should be tried first.
