Status Epilepticus: Look at the Eyes, Then Start the Clock

Adults and weight-based pediatrics in the ED with convulsive status epilepticus (seizure >5 min or recurrent without recovery). Excludes primary neonatal seizures and post-anoxic myoclonus, which follow separate pathways.

⚠ Look at the eyes before starting the BZD algorithm. Dysconjugate gaze, skew deviation, vertical gaze palsy, ocular bobbing, or fixed/asymmetric pupils mean basilar artery occlusion (BAO) until proven otherwise — thrombectomy window extends to 24h from last known well.
⚠ Do NOT underdose the benzodiazepine. Timid weight-based dosing is the most common cause of 'refractory' SE.
⚠ Do NOT give fosphenytoin/phenytoin for drug- or alcohol-induced seizures. It is ineffective — use pyridoxine (INH) or phenobarbital (alcohol withdrawal) instead.
⚠ Do NOT give valproate to a pregnant patient. Category X, teratogenic — use levetiracetam instead.
⚠ Do NOT assume the seizure is over because visible movement stopped. Up to 48% of patients with altered consciousness after SE have ongoing nonconvulsive SE (NCSE) on EEG.
⚠ Continuous EEG monitoring is mandatory once a patient is intubated/paralyzed for refractory SE — seizure activity cannot be assessed clinically in a paralyzed patient.

1 · Before the algorithm — look at the eyes

Definition & the clock
Status epilepticus = a seizure lasting >5 min, or recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between them. Treatment time is outcome — every minute of ongoing SE internalizes GABA-A receptors and worsens benzodiazepine resistance.[11][1]
The BAO trap — the eyes are the brainstem window
Basilar artery occlusion (BAO) can mimic refractory SE — convulsive movements, posturing, hyperekplexia, coma with no cortical EEG correlate. Median ED-to-diagnosis when it presents this way is 8h 24min, and only 19% are caught inside the tPA window. Mortality without recanalization is ~75%.[12] A 'seizure' that will not respond to Ativan until you chemically coma the patient is basilar until proven otherwise.
  • Dysconjugate gaze — one eye drifts off the other. SE never breaks conjugacy (intact MLF); BAO does.
  • Skew deviation — vertical misalignment; part of a 3-step exam that is 100% sensitive / 96% specific for stroke.[15]
  • Vertical gaze palsy — present in >75% of top-of-basilar cases.[16]
  • Ocular bobbing, fixed pupils, or absent doll's eyes — structural brainstem, not a cortical seizure.
Why the eyes settle it in 60 secondsbrainstem vs cortex

In cortical SE the brainstem is intact, so eyes still move conjugately, doll's eyes work, and pupils react. In BAO the brainstem is structurally wrecked and the eyes give it away immediately. NCCT alone misses 30–40% of BAOs — the hyperdense basilar sign is specific but only ~60% sensitive.

2 · If the eyes are wrong — pivot to stroke

Stop chasing SE. Get the CTA. Call neuro-IR.
Any BAO red flag on the eye exam → stop the SE workup. This is a stroke, not a seizure.
  1. CTA head and neck STAT — 90 seconds; NCCT alone misses 30–40% of BAOs.
  2. Activate neuro-IR / stroke team — thrombectomy benefits out to 24h from last-known-well.
  3. Permissive hypertension — do not lower BP; the penumbra depends on it.
  4. Thrombectomy 0–12h (ATTENTION): mRS 0–3 at 90d 46% vs 23% medical; 6–24h (BAOCHE): 46% vs 24% — both stopped early for efficacy.[17][18]

3 · Stabilize & find the reversible cause

  • Airway: left lateral decubitus, suction ready, nasopharyngeal airway if needed — no bite block, no oral airway.
  • Breathing: high-flow O2 via non-rebreather, continuous SpO2.
  • Circulation: cardiac monitor, two IVs, full vitals including temperature.
  • Protect from injury, do NOT restrain. Start the clock.
Fingerstick glucose STAT
Hypoglycemia is a common, instantly reversible trigger. Glucose <60 mg/dL → D50W 25 g IV (adults) or D10W 2–5 mL/kg (peds). Send BMP, Ca/Mg/Phos, lactate, VBG, tox, ASM levels, troponin, CK, pregnancy test. CT head after stabilization; EEG early — 48% of altered post-SE patients have ongoing NCSE.[1]

5 · What to do right now

Pregnant / suspected eclampsia
Eclampsia (HTN, proteinuria, >20 wk, or new seizure in pregnancy) → Magnesium Sulfate 4–6 g IV over 15–20 min, then 1–2 g/hr. OB STAT, fetal monitoring, consider emergent delivery. If breakthrough epilepsy, use standard BZD then prefer levetiracetam (89% of neurologists' preferred 2nd-line agent in pregnancy) and AVOID valproate (teratogenic).[9][10]
Suspected toxic ingestion
INH toxicity → Pyridoxine (B6) 5 g IV empiric (gram-for-gram if amount known) — standard ASMs fail without it. Alcohol withdrawal → benzodiazepines + phenobarbital. Fosphenytoin/phenytoin are ineffective for drug- and alcohol-induced seizures.
No IV access — give it IM now
Do not wait for a line. Midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM into deltoid or vastus lateralis (band dosing: ≥40 kg → 10 mg; 13–40 kg → 5 mg), then keep trying for IV access while the drug works.
Standard adult / peds pathway
IV Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg) now, repeat once at 5–10 min if seizing. If still seizing after an adequate benzodiazepine, move to a second-line ASM within 5–10 min. Toggle a state above if the patient is pregnant, poisoned, or without IV access.

6 · Phase 2 — second-line ASM (ESETT)

BZD-refractory SE → give a second-line ASM within 5–10 min of benzodiazepine failure. ESETT (2019): levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, and valproate were equally effective (~47% cessation at 60 min) with no adverse-event difference — choose by comorbidity.[4]

Levetiracetam (Keppra) — no cardiac/hepatic effects, safe in pregnancy: 4200 mg IV over 10–15 min — 60 mg/kg (max 4500 mg). No telemetry needed, no drug interactions — the default when comorbidities are unknown.

Valproate (Depacon) — avoid in pregnancy / liver disease: 2800 mg IV over 10 min — 40 mg/kg (max 3000 mg). Contraindicated in pregnancy, hepatic disease, mitochondrial/urea-cycle disorders, pancreatitis. Check platelets and ammonia.

Fosphenytoin (Cerebyx) — needs cardiac monitoring: 1400 mg PE IV, max 150 mg PE/min — 20 mg PE/kg (max 1500 mg PE). Monitor for hypotension, bradycardia, AV block. INEFFECTIVE for drug/alcohol-induced seizures.

Phenobarbital — when ESETT agents unavailable: 1400 mg IV, max 50–100 mg/min — 20 mg/kg (max 2000 mg). High respiratory-depression risk — have intubation ready. Also first-line for alcohol-withdrawal and neonatal seizures.

Failure of a benzodiazepine and an adequate second-line ASM. RSI with a short-acting paralytic (succinylcholine over rocuronium) so you can still read seizures, arterial line, and continuous EEG is mandatory — seizure activity cannot be assessed clinically in a paralyzed patient. Titrate a midazolam, propofol, pentobarbital, or ketamine infusion to burst suppression for 24–48 h before weaning 25% q4–6h. Super-refractory SE (persists despite 24h of anesthetic therapy) needs a different agent, lacosamide adjunct, or immunotherapy if autoimmune etiology is suspected.[8]
