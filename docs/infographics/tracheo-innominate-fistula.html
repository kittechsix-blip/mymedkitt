Tracheo-Innominate Fistula (TIF)

Patients with an existing tracheostomy presenting with peristomal or intraluminal bleeding, evaluated in the ED/ICU setting for possible tracheo-innominate fistula (TIF). Covers risk stratification by time since tracheostomy placement, sentinel-bleed recognition, cuff hyperinflation, finger tamponade (Utley maneuver), surgical activation, and stabilization through disposition to the OR.

⚠ Massive hemorrhage occurring 3 days to 6 weeks after tracheostomy is TIF until proven otherwise
⚠ A sentinel bleed is NOT reassuring — it precedes massive hemorrhage by 24-48 hours and warrants immediate surgical activation
⚠ Do NOT remove the tracheostomy tube unless proceeding to finger tamponade — the tube provides tamponade and airway
⚠ Do NOT delay treatment for imaging — CTA, bronchoscopy, and angiography combined have only 20-30% sensitivity; diagnosis is clinical
⚠ Once finger tamponade (Utley maneuver) is started, do NOT release pressure until the surgeon takes over in the OR
⚠ Surgical ligation must include division of the innominate artery — ligation alone risks re-fistulization
⚠ There is no observation period for suspected TIF — all patients go directly to the OR

Recognition & timing

Tracheo-innominate fistula (TIF) is a catastrophic complication of tracheostomy with near-100% mortality without intervention and 50-70% mortality even with surgery. Key principle: massive hemorrhage occurring 3 days to 6 weeks after tracheostomy is TIF until proven otherwise. Incidence is 0.1-1% of tracheostomies, accounting for ~10% of significant tracheostomy bleeds. Time is critical — a sentinel bleed precedes massive hemorrhage by only 24-48 hours.

This consult covers: recognizing the sentinel bleed, cuff hyperinflation technique, finger tamponade (Utley maneuver), surgical activation, and stabilization/airway management. See the TIF Steps Summary for a rehearsal-ready overview.

< 72 hours agoSuctioning trauma, manipulation, surgical site oozing, coagulopathy — usually benignGentle suction, check coags, optimize cuff pressure; escalate to TIF workup if arterial, pulsatile, or >50 mL
3 days to 6 weeks agoPeak incidence 7-14 days; 72% of cases occur in the first 3 weeksHIGH RISK for TIF — proceed with full TIF protocol
> 6 weeks agoGranulation tissue most common; late TIF still reported (case reports >1 year)Lower risk but still possible — evaluate bleeding characteristics for arterial/pulsatile features

Early bleeding (<72h) is usually benign: suctioning trauma, manipulation, surgical site oozing, coagulopathy, or forming granulation tissue. Red flags requiring escalation: arterial-appearing blood, pulsatile bleeding, large volume (>50 mL), or bleeding not responding to conservative measures. If concerning features are present, proceed with the TIF workup despite the early timing.

Late bleeding (>6 weeks) is less commonly TIF but still possible — case reports of TIF beyond 1 year exist, with risk factors of high cuff pressure and chronic inflammation. Low-concern features: small volume, venous-appearing, associated with tube changes. High-concern features: arterial-appearing, pulsatile, large volume, or tube pulsating with heartbeat. If ANY high-concern feature is present, proceed with the full TIF protocol.

TIF management algorithm

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for complete node-by-node detail, or the TIF Steps Summary for a rehearsal-ready checklist.

Sentinel bleed recognition

  1. Sentinel bleeding (small bleed that spontaneously stops)
  2. Pulsations of the tracheostomy tube coinciding with the heartbeat
  3. Tracheostomy site bleeding

50% of TIF patients have a sentinel bleed before massive hemorrhage; only 35% exhibit the full pathognomonic triad. CTA, bronchoscopy, and angiography combined have only 20-30% sensitivity — diagnosis of TIF is clinical, not imaging-based.

  1. Call surgery STAT (CT surgery / vascular / trauma)
  2. Activate MTP — have blood at bedside
  3. Type and crossmatch — anticipate massive transfusion
  4. Keep patient NPO — prepare for emergent OR
  5. Do NOT send for imaging if it delays surgical consultation
Sentinel bleed changes the odds
2024 data: sentinel bleed presentation = 50% mortality vs 75% without warning (p=0.005). The sentinel bleed gives a narrow window to prepare for massive hemorrhage.

Hyperinflate cuff

  1. Slowly add air to the tracheostomy cuff balloon
  2. Target ~50 mL total volume (much higher than normal)
  3. Goal: tamponade the bleeding vessel by compressing it against the tracheal wall
85%Cuff hyperinflation success rate
Temporizing only
This is a temporizing measure only — even successful tamponade can fail with tube movement. Continue to prepare for OR; surgery is still required for definitive management.

If bleeding continues, the fistula may be proximal to the cuff, or the cuff may not be achieving adequate tamponade — proceed to finger tamponade. If bleeding stops, do NOT relax vigilance and do NOT move the tube.

Option 1 — add oral ETT: continue adding air to the trach cuff while performing deep oral intubation (ETT cuff 2 cm past the stoma) as a backup airway and additional tamponade. Option 2 — replace the trach with a cuffed ETT through the stoma, advanced distal to the bleeding site. If bleeding continues despite these measures, proceed immediately to finger tamponade.

Finger tamponade — Utley maneuver

The Utley maneuver is THE lifesaving bedside intervention when cuff hyperinflation fails. Success rate: >80% for controlling hemorrhage via direct tamponade. The provider who performs this maneuver MUST NOT MOVE THEIR FINGER until the patient is in the OR under surgical care — you will be physically attached to this patient during transport.

  • Ensure airway backup plan (oral ETT ready)
  • Have surgical team en route
  • Blood products running or ready
  • OR notified and preparing
  • Transport team assembled
  1. Secure airway: deflate trach cuff, insert deep cuffed oral ETT (2 cm past stoma), confirm ventilation
  2. Remove tracheostomy tube; extend stoma incision vertically if access is inadequate
  3. Insert index finger through stoma into the pretracheal space (blunt dissection if needed)
  4. Compress the innominate artery anteriorly against the posterior sternum/manubrium
  5. Place thumb in the sternal notch — pinch the artery between finger and thumb
  6. Maintain pressure — do NOT release until the patient is in the OR under surgical care

The innominate artery crosses anterior to the trachea, usually at rings 6-9 (can be higher — high-riding variant). If the stoma is too small, extend the incision inferiorly with a scalpel and use blunt dissection. If the pretracheal space cannot be accessed, apply external pressure at the sternal notch (less effective but may temporize). During transport, maintain continuous pressure, keep the patient supine, and ensure the OR team knows the provider is physically attached to the patient.

Surgical activation & options

TIF is a SURGICAL EMERGENCY. Mortality without surgery: ~100%. Mortality with surgery: 50-70% (but survival is possible). Call CT surgery, vascular surgery, trauma surgery, anesthesia, and the OR charge nurse immediately. Prepare a sternotomy tray, vascular instruments, cell saver, blood products in the room, and perfusion on standby.

ApproachTechniqueKey point
Median sternotomy (standard)Ligate AND divide the innominate artery proximal and distal to the fistula; cover the tracheal defect with a muscle flap (usually pectoralis major)Ligation WITHOUT division is contraindicated — the artery can re-fistulize
Endovascular stent-graft (emerging)Stent-graft placed across the fistula; occlusion balloon can buy time for resuscitationEquivalent mortality to open surgery (48.6% both); lower complication rate (30% vs 50%, p=0.045)
Neurological risk of innominate ligation
Up to 10% incidence of right-sided neurological deficit, dependent on Circle of Willis retrograde flow — vigilant postoperative neuro monitoring is required.

Stabilization

  • Activate MTP immediately; blood products at bedside; anticipate massive blood loss (liters); 1:1:1 ratio PRBCs:FFP:platelets
  • Large-bore IV x2 minimum; consider central line if time permits; rapid infuser ready
  • Airway: if trach in place with controlled bleeding, leave it; if finger tamponade, oral ETT already placed; always have a backup airway plan
  • TXA if institutional protocol; vasopressors if needed for blood pressure
  • Team: airway control AND hemorrhage control simultaneously, respiratory therapy, blood bank liaison

Critical DO NOTs

Actions that can be FATAL in TIF
These actions can be fatal in TIF and must be avoided even under pressure to move quickly.
  • Do NOT remove the tracheostomy tube unless proceeding to finger tamponade — the tube provides tamponade and airway
  • Do NOT delay for imaging if clinical suspicion is high — CTA/bronchoscopy only 20-30% sensitive; clinical diagnosis is sufficient
  • Do NOT attempt ligation without division — the artery can re-fistulize
  • Do NOT suction aggressively through the trach during active bleeding — may dislodge clot and worsen bleeding
  • Do NOT move the patient unnecessarily, especially with cuff tamponade — direct transport to OR only
  • Do NOT release finger tamponade before surgical takeover — you are committed once you start

Disposition

All TIF patients go directly to OR. There is no observation period for suspected TIF. Pathway: ED/ICU recognition → immediate hemorrhage control (cuff/finger tamponade) → surgical activation → direct transport to OR → postoperative ICU. Postoperative: ICU admission mandatory, vigilant neuro monitoring (right-sided deficits possible), airway management, continued blood product resuscitation, watch for re-bleeding.
