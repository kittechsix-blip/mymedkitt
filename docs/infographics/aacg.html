Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma: Cascade to IOP <40, Then Pilocarpine

Adults presenting to the ED with suspected acute angle-closure glaucoma. Excludes pediatric angle closure, chronic/plateau-iris glaucoma without an acute crisis, and post-surgical secondary glaucoma — each needs a distinct pathway.

⚠ Rock-hard globe + classic presentation = treat empirically — do not delay acetazolamide, topical agents, and ophthalmology consultation waiting for tonometry or a formal consult.
⚠ Do NOT give pilocarpine until IOP <40 mmHg — the ischemic iris sphincter will not respond at higher pressure; giving it early is ineffective and wastes time.
⚠ Bilateral angle closure is drug-induced (usually topiramate) until proven otherwise — the mechanism is ciliary body effusion, not pupillary block. STOP the offending drug, use cycloplegics (atropine) instead of pilocarpine, and know that laser iridotomy is NOT effective for this mechanism.
⚠ Refractory AACG (IOP remains elevated despite maximal medical therapy) must NOT be discharged — it requires ophthalmology-performed anterior chamber paracentesis, emergent laser iridotomy, or surgical iridectomy.
⚠ Avoid anticholinergics, antihistamines, and sympathomimetics — these classes precipitate or worsen AACG by causing mydriasis and pupillary block.
⚠ Keep lights bright and position the patient supine — dim lighting dilates the pupil and worsens pupillary block; supine positioning moves the lens posteriorly and relieves the block.

1 · Recognition & mimics

Ophthalmologic emergency — rapid pupillary-block IOP elevation causes optic nerve damage within hours. IOP typically 50-80 mmHg — mechanism: pupillary block → aqueous outflow obstruction. 1/3 of AACG patients initially see a non-ophthalmologist — don't miss it.[1][2]

Classic triad: severe unilateral eye pain, fixed mid-dilated pupil, rock-hard globe. Halos around lights are pathognomonic. Nausea/vomiting is prominent and may mimic GI illness. Risk factors: age >50 (peak 55-65), female (2-4x higher), Asian > Caucasian, hyperopia, shallow anterior chamber, precipitating medications.[1][2][3]

Cluster headacheLacrimation, rhinorrhea, Horner syndrome
Intracranial pathologyOther neuro findings, papilledema

Pearl: if uncertain, palpate both globes — the affected side feels significantly harder. AACG distinguishers: unilateral, fixed MID-dilated pupil (not constricted like uveitis), rock-hard globe, elevated IOP.[1][3]

StatusIOP (mmHg)

Tonometry (gold standard): Goldmann applanation, Tono-Pen, iCare rebound tonometer. Transpalpebral palpation (no equipment): patient looks down, palpate through the upper lid, compare both eyes — AACG feels rock hard vs a normal soft globe. Can't measure IOP? Rock-hard globe + classic presentation = treat empirically — don't delay treatment for equipment.[1][2]

The 22-39 mmHg tier is managed as subacute angle closure; the actual gate used by every downstream treatment decision to enable pilocarpine is IOP <40 mmHg, not the <35 mmHg figure in the summary table above.

Do NOT discharge refractory AACG — it needs ophthalmology-performed anterior chamber paracentesis or surgery.

Do NOT dim the lights — bright light constricts the pupil and may help open the angle; dim lighting dilates the pupil and worsens pupillary block.

Do NOT overlook precipitating medications (anticholinergics, antihistamines, sympathomimetics, TCAs, SSRIs) — ask about any new drug or OTC medication in the past 2 weeks.

Do NOT confuse AACG with uveitis — uveitis has a CONSTRICTED pupil, not the mid-dilated fixed pupil of AACG.

Do NOT delay antiemetic control — nausea is prominent in AACG and impairs use of PO medications.
