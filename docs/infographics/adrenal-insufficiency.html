Adrenal Crisis: Steroids Now, Diagnose Later

Adults and children with suspected adrenal insufficiency or adrenal crisis in the ED. Toggle the patient's state to drive the recommendation. Excludes definitive endocrine management — arrange endocrine follow-up for every new diagnosis.

⚠ Failure to stress-dose is the #1 cause of adrenal crisis. Double oral hydrocortisone for fever >38°C, triple for >39°C; give 100 mg IM and go to the ED if vomiting prevents oral intake.
⚠ Give fluids and draw cortisol/ACTH before steroids — but never delay treatment for labs. A random cortisol <18 μg/dL during physiologic stress supports AI; >18 μg/dL makes it very unlikely.
⚠ Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV/IM immediately, then 200 mg/24h. At ≥50 mg/day of hydrocortisone the mineralocorticoid effect is adequate, so no fludrocortisone is needed in the acute phase. Use dexamethasone if the diagnosis is uncertain (does not interfere with the cortisol assay).
⚠ Adrenal crisis can be the first presentation of undiagnosed AI in up to 50% of cases. Maintain high suspicion in unexplained shock refractory to fluids and vasopressors.
⚠ Bilateral adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome — meningococcemia, DIC, HIT, anticoagulation) causes permanent primary AI. Presents as unexplained hemodynamic instability with flank/abdominal/back pain; CT abdomen is diagnostic. Give hydrocortisone 100 mg IV immediately and hold/reverse anticoagulation if possible.

1 · Recognize the crisis

Adrenal crisis is a medical emergency
Acute hemodynamic deterioration — SBP ≤100 mmHg or ≥20 mmHg below baseline — that resolves within 1–2 hours of parenteral hydrocortisone. Mortality is 0.5 per 100 patient-years in known-AI patients, and crisis is the *first* presentation of undiagnosed AI in up to 50% of cases.[2][4]
FeatureDetail
Hemodynamic collapseSBP ≤100 mmHg or ≥20 mmHg below baseline; shock refractory to fluids
HyponatremiaPresent in 84% of cases
HypoglycemiaCommon — check glucose early, treat with dextrose
HyperkalemiaPrimary AI only (mineralocorticoid deficiency)
OtherFever, abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, altered mental status

Infection is the #1 precipitant — viral in children, bacterial in adults. Other triggers: trauma or surgery without stress dosing, abrupt steroid withdrawal, CYP3A4 inducers (rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine), and GI illness with vomiting. ~10% of crises have no identifiable precipitant.[3][7]

2 · What to do now

Set the toggles above to match your patient. Exactly one instruction below applies — crisis overrides everything else.

Adrenal crisis — treat immediately
1. IV access · 1 L NS bolus, repeat PRN; add dextrose if hypoglycemic.
2. Draw cortisol + ACTH before steroids if possible — do NOT delay.
3. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV/IM now, then 200 mg/24h.
4. Find and treat the precipitant (infection is #1). Reassess hemodynamics at 1–2 h.[2][7]
—Pediatric D10W bolus for hypoglycemia (5 mL/kg)
Stable Primary AI — replace both hormones
Glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid: Hydrocortisone 15–25 mg PO daily (2/3 AM, 1/3 early afternoon) plus Fludrocortisone 50–200 mcg PO daily. Hyperpigmentation and hyperkalemia point to PAI. Check 21-OH antibodies, CT adrenals, and TSH — treat cortisol before thyroxine.[1][8]
Stable Secondary/Tertiary AI — glucocorticoid only
No hyperpigmentation, no hyperkalemia (RAAS axis intact) → no fludrocortisone needed. Hydrocortisone 15–25 mg PO daily. Tertiary (steroid-induced) AI is the most common form overall; HPA-axis recovery takes 6–12 months after stopping steroids. Screen for other pituitary deficiencies in SAI.[5][9][11][13]

5 · Diagnostic workup

Morning cortisol + ACTH (draw 8–9 AM)
AM cortisol <3 μg/dL → AI virtually confirmed. >15 μg/dL → AI unlikely. 3–15 μg/dL → indeterminate, needs cosyntropin stimulation. Then ACTH sorts the type: >2× ULN = Primary; low/normal = Secondary or Tertiary.[1][5]
Cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test
250 mcg IV/IM
IV/IM
Draw baseline cortisol, give 250 mcg, then cortisol at 30 and 60 min. Peak ≥18 μg/dL = normal (excludes PAI). Does NOT reliably detect SAI or recent-onset PAI — refer for insulin tolerance or metyrapone testing if suspicion remains.
Type-specific workup3 types
Primary (Addison)
21-hydroxylase antibodies (positive in ~85% of autoimmune PAI), CT adrenals (TB = calcified/enlarged; hemorrhage = enlarged, high-density), renin + aldosterone (high renin + low aldosterone = PAI).[8]
Secondary
MRI pituitary with contrast, full anterior pituitary panel (TSH, LH/FSH, GH, prolactin). Consider Sheehan syndrome after severe postpartum hemorrhage.[9]
Tertiary
History of exogenous steroid exposure; HPA recovery takes 6–12 months. CYP3A4 inducers may double the maintenance requirement.[11]

Even treated, crisis mortality is real
In-hospital adrenal crisis mortality is approximately 2-6% even with prompt treatment (2.4% in a cohort of 799 hospitalized crisis patients, higher in elderly and pediatric series), and rises significantly with delayed recognition.[2][4]

6 · One-glance pathway

Suspected adrenal insufficiency

Adrenal crisis? (SBP ≤100 or ≥20 below baseline) — Shock refractory to fluids/pressors, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia — treat empirically if in doubt.

1 L NS bolus · draw cortisol + ACTH — Do NOT delay steroids for labs. Add dextrose if hypoglycemic. Peds: 20 mL/kg NS bolus.

Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV/IM → 200 mg/24h — Dexamethasone 4 mg IV if diagnosis uncertain. Peds bolus 2 mg/kg (max 100 mg).

Hemodynamics improve in 1–2 h? — Adrenal crisis should respond to parenteral hydrocortisone within 1–2 hours.

Broaden differential · norepinephrine — Reassess for septic, cardiogenic, hypovolemic shock. Random cortisol >18 μg/dL makes AI unlikely.

ICU admission — Continuous hydrocortisone, q1–2h glucose, q4–6h electrolytes, treat precipitant, endocrine consult.

Tolerating PO and hemodynamically stable? — Ability to take oral steroids and resolved precipitant drive disposition. If type was never confirmed on exam, AM cortisol + ACTH (± cosyntropin if 3–15 μg/dL) is drawn here before committing to a maintenance regimen.

7 · Disposition & prevention

In-hospital adrenal crisis mortality is approximately 2-6% even with prompt treatment (2.4% in a cohort of 799 hospitalized crisis patients, higher in elderly and pediatric series), and rises significantly with delayed recognition.[2][4]
