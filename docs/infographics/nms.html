NMS: Recognize, Treat, Differentiate, Dispose

Adults on dopamine antagonists (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, promethazine, droperidol, or lithium/dopamine-agonist withdrawal) presenting with hyperthermia, rigidity, and autonomic instability in the ED. Excludes pediatric NMS dosing (not modeled in myMedKitt) and malignant hyperthermia (anesthesia/succinylcholine trigger, different mechanism) — see the differentiation table to rule those out first.

⚠ Stop ALL dopamine antagonists (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, promethazine, droperidol) immediately when NMS is suspected — hyperthermia + lead-pipe rigidity + antipsychotic exposure = NMS until proven otherwise.
⚠ Moderate NMS (temp 39-40°C, autonomic instability) requires stopping all antagonists, aggressive IV fluids for rhabdomyolysis prevention, external cooling, lorazepam 2-4 mg IV q5-10min PRN, and bromocriptine 2.5 mg PO/NG q8h (max 45 mg/day) — admit ICU/step-down.
⚠ Severe/life-threatening NMS (temp >40°C, severe rigidity) is an ICU-mandatory emergency: intubate if severe rigidity/hyperthermia, stop all dopamine antagonists, dantrolene 1-2.5 mg/kg IV q6h (max 10 mg/kg/day — NOT first-line for mild-moderate disease), bromocriptine 2.5-5 mg PO/NG q8h, aggressive cooling, and target UOP 200-300 mL/hr.
⚠ Distinguish NMS from serotonin syndrome before treating: NMS = slow onset (days), lead-pipe rigidity, normal/decreased reflexes, NO clonus; serotonin syndrome = fast onset (hours), hyperreflexia, clonus. Treatments differ (dantrolene/bromocriptine vs cyproheptadine).
⚠ Bromocriptine is prothrombotic — increases MI/stroke/VTE risk; consider VTE prophylaxis/anticoagulation in immobilized NMS patients receiving it.

1 · Recognition

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a life-threatening reaction to dopamine antagonists. Classic tetrad: hyperthermia (often >40°C), lead-pipe rigidity, autonomic instability, and mental status changes. Onset is usually within 2 weeks of drug initiation or dose change (4-14 days).

ClassExamples
Typical antipsychoticsHaloperidol, droperidol, chlorpromazine
Atypical antipsychoticsOlanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, clozapine
AntiemeticsMetoclopramide, prochlorperazine, promethazine
OtherLithium withdrawal, dopamine agonist withdrawal

Risk factors: high-potency agents (haloperidol highest risk), rapid dose escalation, IM administration, dehydration, organic brain disease, and a prior NMS episode (17-30% recurrence).

  • Fever (>38°C)
  • Rigidity
  • Elevated CK (>1000 U/L typical)

Levenson criteria: 3 major above (if no other cause) plus 2 of these minor: tachycardia, abnormal BP (hyper or hypotension), tachypnea, altered consciousness, diaphoresis, leukocytosis.

CK is markedly elevated — often >1000 U/L, can be >100,000 — with leukocytosis (10,000-40,000), metabolic acidosis, elevated LFTs, and myoglobinuria.

On exam: lead-pipe rigidity (constant resistance through full ROM), normal or decreased reflexes (unlike serotonin syndrome), bradykinesia, tremor, hyperthermia (often >40°C, can reach 42°C), tachycardia, labile BP, diaphoresis, and confusion progressing to coma.

Step-down / telemetry
Mild-moderate symptoms, stable vitals, CK <10,000, no respiratory compromise. Continue bromocriptine, benzodiazepines PRN, aggressive hydration, and serial CK/renal function. Average hospitalization 7-14 days.

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT rely on hyperreflexia or clonus to diagnose NMS — those point to serotonin syndrome instead; NMS shows normal or decreased reflexes and no clonus.

Do NOT delay ICU-level care for temp >40°C with severe rigidity awaiting confirmatory labs.

Do NOT use dantrolene as first-line therapy for mild-moderate NMS — it is indicated for severe hyperthermia/rigidity; use bromocriptine and benzodiazepines first.

Do NOT restart a dopamine antagonist within 2 weeks of NMS resolution — use a different class and start at a low dose when rechallenge is necessary.

Do NOT overlook bromocriptine's prothrombotic risk in immobilized NMS patients — consider VTE prophylaxis.
