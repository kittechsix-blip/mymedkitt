Low Back Pain — ED Assessment & Management

Adults presenting to the ED with acute low back pain, traumatic or atraumatic. Excludes pediatric low back pain (different differential — discitis, spondylolisthesis) and pregnancy-specific back pain evaluation (separate red-flag thresholds not addressed by this consult).

⚠ Cauda equina syndrome: urinary retention, saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness — emergent MRI + STAT neurosurgery consult; decompression within 48 hours improves outcomes. Do NOT delay for other workup.
⚠ Spinal epidural abscess / vertebral osteomyelitis: fever + IVDU/immunosuppression + back pain — obtain blood cultures BEFORE antibiotics (unless septic), MRI with contrast, empiric vancomycin + ceftriaxone.
⚠ Screen ALL low back pain patients for red flags before treating as routine mechanical pain: cauda equina (urinary retention, saddle anesthesia, bilateral deficit), infection (fever + IVDU/immunosuppression), malignancy (cancer history, unexplained weight loss, night pain, age >50).
⚠ DO NOT image uncomplicated low back pain <6 weeks duration with no red flags and no neurologic deficit — no outcome benefit, and imaging adds radiation, incidental findings, and overtreatment.
⚠ NSAIDs are first-line analgesia; avoid opioids — no more effective than NSAIDs for low back pain and carry addiction/hyperalgesia risk. Avoid benzodiazepines — no evidence of benefit.
⚠ Activity is better than bed rest — bed rest delays recovery; advise patients to stay active.
⚠ Return precautions: urinary retention/incontinence, fecal incontinence, saddle-area numbness, worsening leg weakness, fever with back pain, or uncontrolled severe pain.

Traumatic LBP — fracture risk

  • High-energy mechanism: fall from height (>3 feet or >5 stairs)
  • Motor vehicle collision (ejection, rollover, >40 mph)
  • Direct blow to spine or axial loading (diving, compression)
  • Age >65 (osteoporosis risk)
  • Known osteoporosis or chronic steroid use (>3 months)
  • History of spinal cancer or metastases
  • Midline tenderness to palpation, step-off deformity, or neurologic deficit on exam

Cauda equina syndrome

Classic triad — emergent
Urinary retention (most common — post-void residual >200 mL), saddle anesthesia (perineal numbness), bilateral leg weakness or numbness. Also: fecal incontinence, decreased anal tone, sexual dysfunction, progressive bilateral radiculopathy.
  • Rectal tone — decreased or absent
  • Perianal sensation — test light touch S2-S5
  • Bladder scan — post-void residual >200 mL raises suspicion
  • Bilateral lower extremity strength — hip flexion, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion
  1. Emergent MRI (gold standard)
  2. POCUS bladder scan while awaiting MRI
  3. Neurosurgery STAT consult — decompression within 48 hours improves outcomes
  4. Do NOT delay for other workup

Spinal infection

Epidural abscess / vertebral osteomyelitis — high mortality if missed. Risk factors: IVDU (most common), recent spinal injection or surgery, immunosuppression (diabetes, HIV, steroids, chemo), bacteremia/endocarditis, indwelling catheter or recent hospitalization. Clinical features: fever + localized back pain (may be subtle if immunocompromised), point tenderness over spine, neurologic deficit (late finding — suggests cord compression).

  • CBC, CRP, ESR — ESR >20 highly sensitive
  • Blood cultures ×2 — positive in 60%
  • Procalcitonin
  • MRI with contrast — gold standard (plain films often normal early; CT if MRI unavailable, less sensitive for soft tissue)
Management
Blood cultures BEFORE antibiotics (unless septic). Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics: vancomycin + ceftriaxone (or pip-tazo). Neurosurgery consult if neurologic deficit or large abscess.

Malignancy / metastatic disease

High-risk features: history of cancer (especially breast, lung, prostate, kidney, thyroid, myeloma), unexplained weight loss >10 lbs / 4.5 kg, age >50 with new severe back pain, pain worse at night or with rest, failure to improve with conservative treatment. Exam: point tenderness over vertebral body, neurologic deficit (suggests cord compression), lymphadenopathy or masses.

  • CBC (anemia, leukocytosis), CMP (hypercalcemia in myeloma/renal mets)
  • PSA if prostate cancer suspected; SPEP/UPEP if myeloma suspected
  • MRI with contrast — best for soft tissue and cord compression (CT with contrast if MRI unavailable)

Straight leg raise (SLR) — positive if radicular pain at <60° (high sensitivity for L5/S1 disc herniation). Crossed SLR — pain in the affected leg when raising the contralateral leg (high specificity). Gait: heel walk (L4-L5), toe walk (S1).

Lumbar radiculopathy

Most common levels: L5 (45%) — weakness of great toe/ankle dorsiflexion, numbness dorsum of foot; S1 (45%) — weakness of plantarflexion/toe walking, numbness lateral foot, decreased Achilles reflex; L4 (5%) — weakness of knee extension, numbness medial leg, decreased patellar reflex.

Natural history
90% improve within 6-12 weeks with conservative management. Disc herniations often resorb spontaneously. Imaging in the first 6 weeks rarely changes management.
  • Progressive neurologic deficit
  • Severe, disabling pain despite 4-6 weeks conservative treatment
  • Red flags present
  • Considering surgical intervention

Mechanical / musculoskeletal LBP

Benign, self-limited
Worse with activity, better with rest; paraspinal muscle tenderness; no neurologic deficit; no red flags. Differential: muscular strain (most common), facet joint arthropathy, degenerative disc disease, lumbar spondylosis. DO NOT IMAGE — ACR/ACEP guidelines recommend against routine imaging. Most cases resolve in 2-4 weeks; staying active is better than bed rest.
Why we don't image uncomplicated back painRadiation dose, incidental findings, and cost data behind the no-imaging recommendation

ACEP Choosing Wisely / ACR guidelines: do not image uncomplicated LBP with no red flags, no neurologic deficit, duration <6 weeks, non-traumatic. Harms: lumbar CT = 6 mSv (equivalent to 3 years of background radiation); incidental findings lead to more tests, anxiety, and unnecessary surgery; "degenerative changes" on MRI are NORMAL in asymptomatic adults (67% at age 40, 88% at age 60); RCTs show no difference in pain or function at 1 year. Costs: lumbar MRI $1,000-3,000; unnecessary workup from incidentals adds thousands more.

What to tell patients
"Your exam shows this is a muscle strain. An MRI would show normal age-related changes that everyone has — it wouldn't help and might lead to unnecessary treatments. The best evidence says you'll recover with time and staying active."

Emergent imaging

MRI is the gold standard for emergent spinal imaging. Emergent MRI indications: cauda equina syndrome, spinal cord compression, epidural abscess (fever + IVDU + back pain), severe/progressive neurologic deficit. MRI with contrast preferred for suspected infection or malignancy. MRI without contrast acceptable for disc herniation or cauda equina (no infection concern). If MRI unavailable or contraindicated: CT myelogram or CT without contrast.

  • POCUS bladder scan for post-void residual
  • Pain control
  • Dexamethasone 10 mg IV if cord compression suspected
  • Early neurosurgery consult

Fracture imaging

Start with plain films (AP + lateral) — adequate for most compression fractures, sensitivity ~85% for acute fractures. CT indicated for: high-energy mechanism, neurologic deficit, plain films suspicious but inconclusive, known malignancy, polytrauma. MRI indicated for: neurologic deficit, ligamentous injury concern, osteoporotic fracture of unclear acuity, malignancy with possible cord compression.

Disposition

  • NSAIDs ± acetaminophen
  • Muscle relaxant if significant spasm (short course)
  • Activity modification (avoid heavy lifting, not bed rest)
  • Follow-up: PCP in 2-4 weeks if not improving; PT referral if symptoms >2-4 weeks

Return precautions
Urinary retention or incontinence, fecal incontinence, numbness in groin/buttocks (saddle area), worsening leg weakness, fever with back pain, or severe pain not controlled by medications.
  • ICU/close monitoring: hemodynamically unstable with spinal pathology, sepsis from spinal infection
  • Floor admission: cauda equina syndrome (awaiting surgery), epidural abscess (IV antibiotics, surgical planning), malignant cord compression (steroids, XRT planning), unstable spine fracture
  • Consults: neurosurgery (CES, abscess, unstable fracture), orthopedic spine, oncology/radiation oncology, infectious disease
  • Pre-op orders if surgical candidate: NPO, type and screen, baseline labs (CBC, BMP, coags), Foley catheter for urinary retention
  • Stable compression fracture (most common): discharge with pain control, spine/ortho follow-up in 1-2 weeks, TLSO brace if prescribed, osteoporosis workup (DXA, vitamin D, calcium), return if neurologic symptoms develop
  • Unstable fracture or neurologic deficit: admit to spine service, strict spinal precautions, neurosurgery/orthopedic spine consult, consider TLSO/CTLSO bracing
  • Pathologic fracture (malignancy): admit for workup if new cancer diagnosis, oncology consult, consider vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty for pain
  • Follow-up imaging: repeat X-ray in 2-4 weeks to assess healing; MRI if concern for ligamentous injury or malignancy
