High-Dose Insulin Euglycemia Therapy (HDI/HIET) — BB/CCB Poisoning

Adults with poison-induced cardiogenic shock from calcium channel blocker (CCB) or beta-blocker (BB) overdose in the ED/ICU. A brief pediatric dextrose-bolus note is included but this protocol is written for adult dosing. Excludes routine asymptomatic ingestion without hemodynamic instability — see the not-yet-indicated branch.

⚠ HDI is for hemodynamically significant BB/CCB toxicity (shock, bradycardia with poor perfusion, myocardial dysfunction) — NOT for stable/asymptomatic ingestion.
⚠ Use HDI EARLY — do not wait for prolonged failure of low-yield therapies, and do not treat glucagon as definitive in BB toxicity (bridge only: vomiting, tachyphylaxis, shortage, transient response).
⚠ Do NOT delay the insulin bolus for central access or a perfect lab setup in peri-arrest shock.
⚠ If glucose <200 mg/dL, give dextrose (adult 25 g D50W) before or with the insulin bolus.
⚠ Do NOT delay HDI for a pending potassium in crashing shock. Replace K if <3.0 mEq/L, but avoid overcorrection — HDI-associated hypokalemia is usually redistribution, not depletion.
⚠ HDI dosing is intentionally FAR ABOVE routine (DKA) insulin rates — verify the rate is NOT the DKA insulin rate before hanging the bag.
⚠ Maintain continuous dextrose to sustain euglycemia; check glucose q15-20 min during initiation/titration; delayed hypoglycemia can occur up to 24 hours after insulin stops.
⚠ Monitor potassium at least hourly during titration — hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia/hypomagnesemia, fluid overload, and rebound instability from early weaning are expected complications.
⚠ If no improvement, escalate insulin toward the common upper target and add parallel therapies (vasopressors, IV calcium for CCB, glucagon bridge for BB, lipid emulsion) — contact ECMO/cardiac surgery EARLY for refractory reversible shock.
⚠ HDI requires ICU-level care with poison center/toxicology involvement and a detailed handoff.
⚠ Before and during HDI, verify: dextrose plan + glucose monitoring in place, potassium monitoring/replacement plan in place, infusion pump concentration clearly labeled, and poison center/toxicology/pharmacy involved for severe toxicity.

Indication

High-dose insulin euglycemia therapy (HDI/HIET) treats poison-induced cardiogenic shock — especially calcium channel blocker (CCB) and beta-blocker (BB) toxicity. Use early when myocardial dysfunction is present; this is not DKA dosing.

Why HDI works
In shock, the poisoned myocardium shifts to carbohydrate metabolism and becomes insulin-resistant. Supraphysiologic insulin restores myocardial glucose uptake and provides a positive inotropic effect independent of calcium-channel or beta-receptor blockade.
HDI not yet indicated
Do not start HDI for a stable, asymptomatic patient solely because a BB/CCB was ingested. Call poison center for agent-specific observation time; obtain ECG, glucose, electrolytes, lactate; decontaminate if safe; observe extended-release products longer; prepare an HDI kit early if deterioration is plausible. Start HDI immediately if shock, bradycardia with hypoperfusion, or myocardial dysfunction develops.

CCB toxicity — HDI is first-line alongside IV calcium and vasopressors. Use early for hypotension/shock, bradycardia or high-grade AV block with poor perfusion, cool extremities/low cardiac output/rising lactate, severe hyperglycemia with shock, or failure to stabilize rapidly with fluids/calcium/vasopressors.

Beta-blocker toxicity — use HDI for persistent hypotension, bradycardia, or cardiogenic shock. Glucagon may bridge but is not definitive (vomiting, tachyphylaxis, shortage, transient response). Use early for persistent hypotension despite fluids/atropine/glucagon, bradycardia with poor perfusion, myocardial dysfunction on POCUS, escalating vasopressors, or arrest/peri-arrest.

Mixed / unknown cardiotoxic shock — start HDI while diagnostic clarification continues. Parallel therapies: IV calcium if CCB possible, glucagon if BB possible, norepinephrine/epinephrine for perfusion, lipid emulsion for refractory lipophilic toxicity, sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening, and early VA-ECMO discussion. Call poison center/toxicology early: 1-800-222-1222.

Before starting HDI

  • Call poison center/toxicology and pharmacy
  • Assign glucose, potassium, insulin pump, and hemodynamic monitoring roles
  • ≥2 reliable IVs; central access preferred for concentrated dextrose — do not delay insulin
  • Arterial line and bedside echo/POCUS where available
  • Baseline labs: glucose, BMP (K/Mg/phos), VBG/ABG + lactate, ECG/telemetry, ± APAP/ASA/EtOH

Potassium replacement
HDI shifts potassium intracellularly — hypokalemia usually reflects redistribution, not total-body depletion. Continue HDI in life-threatening shock with frequent K/ECG monitoring. Avoid overcorrection — rebound hyperkalemia may occur when insulin is weaned. Approach: replace if K <3.0 mEq/L; target ~3.0-3.5 mEq/L during HDI.

Higher doses are reported but should be specialist-guided. Targets: SBP >90 mmHg or MAP >65 mmHg, HR >50/min when clinically relevant, improving perfusion/lactate/mentation/urine output/cardiac function.

Dextrose infusion — maintain euglycemia

Adult starting option: D10W 1/2NS at 100 mL/hr, then titrate. Use D20W/D25W/D50W through central access when fluid load becomes excessive. Glucose target: 100-200 mg/dL (Ontario Poison Centre); some US protocols use 150-250 mg/dL — follow local poison center guidance.

Dextrose needs can rise
Glucose q15-20 minutes for the first hour and during insulin titration, then q1h once stable. Dextrose needs may increase as toxicity improves and insulin resistance resolves. Continue supplemental dextrose if needed — delayed hypoglycemia can occur up to 24 hours after insulin is stopped.

Monitoring during HDI

  • Continuous telemetry and frequent BP; arterial line if available
  • Glucose q15-20 min first hour and during titration, then q1h once stable
  • Potassium hourly during titration, then q2h if stable
  • Magnesium/phosphate q4-6h or per ICU protocol
  • Lactate, VBG/ABG, urine output, mental status, perfusion exam
  • Serial ECG for conduction/QRS/QT changes; bedside echo when available
Common complications
Hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia/hypomagnesemia, fluid overload from dextrose infusion, and rebound instability if insulin is weaned too early.

Continue, escalate, or refractory shock

  • Track vasopressor requirement, MAP/SBP, HR
  • Track lactate clearance, urine output
  • POCUS/cardiac output if available
  • Continue glucose and potassium replacement needs
  • Confirm insulin is at an adequate dose and titrating q20-30 min
  • Increase insulin by 1 unit/kg/hr toward 10 units/kg/hr with toxicology guidance
  • Ensure glucose and potassium support are keeping pace
  • Continue vasopressors — norepinephrine and/or epinephrine first-line
  • Give/repeat IV calcium for CCB toxicity
  • Glucagon bridge for BB toxicity
  • Lipid emulsion for refractory lipophilic agent toxicity
  • Contact ECMO/cardiac surgery early for refractory reversible shock
  • Continue HDI if already started; escalate to toxicology-guided target
  • Norepinephrine/epinephrine for perfusion
  • IV calcium for CCB toxicity
  • Lipid emulsion for lipophilic agents
  • Sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening (sodium-channel blockade)
  • Activate VA-ECMO/ECPR pathway early when available and patient is a candidate
  • Continue prolonged toxicologic resuscitation when ingestion is potentially reversible

Weaning HDI

  1. Wean vasopressors first when possible
  2. Decrease insulin by 1 unit/kg/hr at a time, or by local ICU/toxicology protocol
  3. Continue glucose and potassium checks during every dose change
  4. Watch for recurrent hypotension, bradycardia, or lactate rise
  5. CCB patients may require increasing dextrose as toxicity improves
  6. Continue dextrose after insulin stops if needed — delayed hypoglycemia can persist up to 24 hours
Avoid abrupt discontinuation
Avoid abrupt discontinuation in large or sustained-release ingestions unless toxicology directs it.

Disposition

  • Agent, formulation, dose/time, and co-ingestions
  • Current insulin rate and highest rate used
  • Dextrose concentration/rate and recent glucose trend
  • Potassium/magnesium/phosphate replacement given
  • Vasopressor doses and response
  • Calcium/glucagon/lipid/bicarbonate therapies given
  • ECG intervals/conduction issues
  • Poison center recommendations and case number

Patients requiring HDI need ICU-level care. Observation duration depends on agent, formulation, clinical course, and poison center guidance.

STOP: HDI safety checks

  • Insulin running without a dextrose plan and glucose monitoring
  • No potassium monitoring/replacement plan
  • Infusion pump concentration unclear or unlabeled
  • Dose entered as DKA insulin rate instead of HDI rate
  • No poison center/toxicology/pharmacy involvement for severe toxicity
  • Persistent shock without escalation toward vasopressors, calcium, lipid, or VA-ECMO as appropriate
Why this matters
HDI dosing errors are high-risk because infusion rates are intentionally far above routine insulin protocols. Verify insulin concentration, dose units, dextrose, potassium, monitoring, and escalation plan before and during HDI.
