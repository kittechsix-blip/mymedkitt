Rhabdomyolysis: Fluids First, CK Guides Intensity

Adults ≥18 in the ED with suspected or confirmed rhabdomyolysis (CK >1,000 U/L). Excludes pediatrics, pregnancy, and dialysis-dependent ESRD — each needs a modified fluid strategy.

⚠ Compartment syndrome: pain with passive stretch, pressure >30mmHg or delta <30mmHg — fasciotomy within 6h, do NOT elevate limb
⚠ Check for hyperK (>6 or ECG changes), symptomatic hypocalcemia, and severe acidosis — treat immediately before fluid resuscitation
⚠ Aggressive IV fluids targeting UOP 1-3 mL/kg/hr - cornerstone of treatment
⚠ CK >15,000 significantly increases AKI risk (5-14% dialysis rate)
⚠ Lactated Ringers preferred over NS - better pH maintenance, avoids hyperchloremic acidosis
⚠ Bicarbonate (150 mEq NaHCO3 in 1L D5W) only if severe acidosis pH <7.1 - NOT routine, fluids are priority

1 · Recognize & diagnose

What it is
Skeletal muscle breakdown releasing intracellular contents — myoglobin, CK, potassium, phosphate — into the circulation. The classic triad of myalgia + weakness + dark/tea-colored urine is present in <10%, so diagnosis rests on CK, not symptoms.[1]

Diagnostic criteria: CK >5× the upper limit of normal; most clinicians use CK >1,000 U/L (~1,000 U/L) as the working threshold. AKI complicates 15-55% of cases and rises with CK level, dehydration, sepsis, and fluid resuscitation delayed >6 hours.[1]

The urine clue
Urine dipstick positive for blood but no RBCs on microscopy = myoglobinuria. Urine myoglobin is cleared faster than CK and may be negative even with a high CK — do not use it to rule out.

4 · Fluid resuscitation — the cornerstone

Aggressive IV crystalloid is the mainstay. Maintain urine output 1-3 mL/kg/hr, replace the deficit AND ongoing third-space losses, and start early (<6h) to blunt AKI. Always check magnesium — up to 50% have concurrent hypomagnesemia.[1][7]

LR vs NS — LR preferred
Lactated Ringers maintains alkaline urine pH better and avoids the hyperchloremic (NAGMA) acidosis that large-volume NS produces. Its K⁺ content (4 mEq/L) does not worsen hyperkalemia — a myth; LR may actually lower K⁺ by correcting acidosis. NS is an acceptable alternative but may need bicarbonate for alkalinization.[7]
  1. Initial bolus: 1-2 L IV crystalloid.
  2. Maintenance 200-500 mL/hr, titrated to UOP.
  3. Foley + hourly UOP; BMP and CK q6-12h.
  4. Continue until CK is trending down AND <5,000 U/L, or myoglobinuria clears.
  5. Watch for volume overload (JVD, pulmonary edema) — cut the rate if it appears.
Bicarbonate is NOT routine
Urinary alkalinization has not beaten fluids alone in RCTs. Reserve 150 mEq NaHCO3 in 1L D5W for severe acidosis (pH <7.1) — fluids remain the priority.[9][10]

6 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT elevate a crush limb** — it drops perfusion pressure and worsens ischemia. Keep it at heart level while ruling out compartment syndrome.

**Do NOT sequence hyperkalemia treatment.** Calcium, insulin/glucose, albuterol, and kaliuresis go together — not one, then wait.

**Do NOT reflexively give bicarbonate.** Urinary alkalinization has not beaten fluids alone in RCTs; reserve it for pH <7.1.

**Do NOT replace asymptomatic hypocalcemia.** Early Ca deposits in damaged muscle and rebounds later — replacing it worsens heterotopic calcification.
