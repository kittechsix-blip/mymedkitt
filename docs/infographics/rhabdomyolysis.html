Rhabdomyolysis: Fluids First, Gate Hyperkalemia, Watch AKI

Adults ≥18 in the ED with suspected or confirmed rhabdomyolysis (CK elevation from skeletal muscle breakdown). Excludes pediatrics and pregnancy — each needs a modified protocol. CHF and ESRD patients need a modified fluid strategy (see Special Populations).

⚠ Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency. Pain with passive stretch, compartment pressure >30 mmHg, or delta pressure <30 mmHg → urgent fasciotomy within 6 hours. Keep the limb at heart level — do NOT elevate.
⚠ Only treat SYMPTOMATIC hypocalcemia. Early hypocalcemia is usually asymptomatic and self-limited (calcium deposits in damaged muscle). Aggressive calcium replacement in asymptomatic patients may worsen heterotopic muscle calcification.
⚠ Life-threatening hyperkalemia is treated immediately, all at once — calcium for membrane stabilization, insulin/dextrose + albuterol to shift potassium, and kaliuresis/dialysis for elimination. Do not wait on confirmatory testing.
⚠ Bicarbonate for urine alkalinization is NOT proven effective. A 2025 meta-analysis found no significant benefit for preventing AKI, acute renal failure, or dialysis need. Aggressive fluids remain the priority — bicarbonate is not routine.
⚠ Mannitol is generally NOT recommended in established AKI and may worsen outcomes.

1 · Recognize & stratify

Rhabdomyolysis
Skeletal muscle breakdown releases myoglobin, CK, potassium, and phosphate into circulation. Classic triad (myalgia, weakness, tea-colored urine) is present in <10% of cases — do not wait for it. Diagnostic threshold: CK >1,000 U/L. AKI risk rises sharply above CK 15,000–16,000 U/L.[1][2]

2 · Etiology

Identify the underlying cause — it guides management and prognosis. Traumatic/crush carries compartment syndrome risk. Exertional rhabdo has the best prognosis. Drug/toxin causes include statins, cocaine, alcohol, NMS/serotonin syndrome. Medical/metabolic includes infection, electrolyte derangement, hypothyroidism.[4]

Compartment syndrome evaluation — crush/trauma
Pain with passive stretch, tense compartment, pressure >30 mmHg, or delta pressure (DBP − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg → fasciotomy indicated. Time-critical: delay >6 hours causes irreversible muscle necrosis. Keep limb at heart level — do NOT elevate. Do not wait for pressure measurement if clinical suspicion is high.[5]

Statin-induced: stop immediately; may rechallenge with a different statin after recovery. Cocaine/amphetamines: treat hyperthermia, avoid beta-blockers (unopposed alpha). NMS/serotonin syndrome: see the Serotonin Syndrome consult — cooling, benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine or dantrolene.[4]

3 · Workup & electrolytes

  • CK (most sensitive, peaks 24–72h), myoglobin, LDH/AST
  • BUN/Cr/GFR; UA — blood on dipstick but no RBCs = myoglobinuria
  • Potassium, calcium (often low early), phosphorus (often high), bicarbonate/ABG
  • CBC, coagulation studies (DIC risk), ECG for hyperkalemia changes

Check for life-threatening abnormalities that require immediate intervention before proceeding to routine fluid resuscitation.[1][6]

8 · Disposition

McMahon Score ≥6 predicts need for RRT — consider ICU. Components: age>70 (2), female (1), CK>40,000 (2), phosphate>4.0 (2), calcium<7.5 (2), elevated creatinine (2), bicarbonate<19 (2), non-benign cause (3).[3][11]

ICU admission
Recommended for CK>50,000, AKI with oliguria, life-threatening hyperkalemia, severe acidosis (pH<7.1), compartment syndrome, likely dialysis need, or McMahon Score ≥6.[3][8][11]
Floor admission
Moderate CK (5,000–15,000) or mild CK with risk factors, stable electrolytes (K<5.5, normal creatinine), adequate UOP, McMahon Score <6.[11]
Observation / discharge
All must be met: CK <5,000 U/L and trending down, normal creatinine, normal potassium, tolerating PO, cause addressed, reliable follow-up. Discharge instructions: aggressive oral hydration 2–3 L/day, avoid strenuous exercise, recheck CK/BMP in 48–72 hours.[11]
