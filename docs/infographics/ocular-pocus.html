Ocular POCUS — Point-of-Care Ocular Ultrasound

Point-of-care ocular ultrasound for adults and children in the ED — acute vision loss/floaters (RD vs PVD vs vitreous hemorrhage), suspected elevated ICP (ONSD measurement), ocular trauma (once globe rupture is excluded), and suspected CRAO. Absolute contraindication: suspected globe rupture — do not scan or apply any pressure if rupture is suspected.

⚠ ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION: suspected globe rupture — ANY pressure risks extrusion of intraocular contents; place a rigid eye shield, apply NO pressure, emergent ophthalmology
⚠ Retinal detachment is tethered at the optic disc and does NOT float freely (V-shaped/tent-shaped, restricted "leashed" movement); PVD floats freely and undulates ("seaweed in water"), continuing to move after the eye stops
⚠ When uncertain between RD and PVD, treat as RD — emergent ophthalmology consult; the consequences of missing RD far outweigh unnecessary consultation
⚠ ONSD >5.0 mm (adult), >4.5 mm (child 1-15y), >4.0 mm (infant <1y) suggests elevated ICP — measure 3 mm behind the globe, outer edge to outer edge
⚠ If globe rupture is suspected: do NOT check IOP, instill eye drops, apply an eye patch, apply pressure, or attempt foreign body removal — rigid shield only
⚠ Dense vitreous hemorrhage obscures the retina — cannot rule out concurrent retinal detachment; needs ophthalmology

When and why

Ocular ultrasound is a rapid bedside tool for vision-threatening emergencies when direct visualization is limited: acute vision loss of unknown etiology, eye trauma (once globe rupture is excluded), suspected elevated ICP, cannot visualize the fundus (hyphema, cataract, vitreous hemorrhage), or suspected retinal detachment.

Absolute contraindication: suspected globe rupture
ANY pressure risks extrusion of intraocular contents. Signs: peaked/teardrop pupil, shallow anterior chamber, visible extrusion of uveal tissue, full-thickness scleral laceration, severe subconjunctival hemorrhage + hypotony. If suspected: rigid eye shield, avoid any pressure, emergent ophthalmology.

Equipment: high-frequency linear probe (7.5-15 MHz) with copious gel or Tegaderm standoff over the closed lid. Position: supine, eyes closed, looking straight ahead. Pressure: minimal — rest the probe on the gel, never on the lid; excessive pressure compresses the globe and alters measurements. Depth: start at 4-5 cm.

Dynamic assessment
Have the patient move eyes side-to-side and observe vitreous/retinal movement — this differentiates PVD (moves freely) from RD (tethered).

Normal vitreous is completely anechoic (dark/black). Any echogenicity in the vitreous is abnormal. Lens is biconvex, hypoechoic with a hyperechoic capsule. Retina is normally adherent to the posterior globe wall — not visible separately. Globe diameter ~24 mm (adult).

▶ What to do now

Vision loss / floaters → differentiate RD, PVD, VH
Key question: is there something in the vitreous that shouldn't be there? RD (V-shaped, tethered to disc) is a surgical emergency. PVD (undulating, floats freely) is usually benign. VH (diffuse echoes, swirls) obscures the retina. When uncertain, treat as RD.
Elevated ICP → measure ONSD
Optic nerve sheath is continuous with dura mater — elevated ICP distends it within minutes. Non-invasive, bedside, repeatable. Measure 3 mm posterior to the globe, outer edge to outer edge, bilateral.
Trauma → rule out globe rupture FIRST
Do NOT scan if globe rupture is suspected — any pressure risks extrusion. If excluded, safe to scan for foreign body, lens dislocation, retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, and ONSD.
CRAO → diagnosis is clinical
Painless, sudden monocular vision loss + cherry-red spot + APD. POCUS is usually normal-appearing and is used primarily to rule out other causes (RD, VH, lens dislocation), not to confirm CRAO.
ONSD <5.0 mm — elevated ICP unlikely
High negative predictive value (95%+), but screening only. If clinical suspicion remains high, continue workup with CT/LP.
ONSD 5.0-5.8 mm — grey zone
Repeat the measurement, assess bilaterally, and correlate clinically. Trend is more informative than a single value — CT head if not done, consider LP/neurosurgery per clinical picture.
ONSD >5.8 mm — strongly suggests elevated ICP
PPV 88-93% for ICP >20 mmHg. HOB 30°, avoid hypoxia/hypercapnia, CT head, neurosurgery consult. If herniation suspected: mannitol or hypertonic saline (see doses below).
Pediatric ONSD thresholds
Child (1-15y) ≥4.5 mm; infant (<1y) ≥4.0 mm suggests elevated ICP. Open fontanelle may buffer ICP in infants — fewer validation studies; use as an adjunct, not sole decision-maker.

Clinical pearl
"If it floats freely like a jellyfish, it's PVD. If it's leashed to the disc like a dog on a chain, it's RD." When in doubt, call ophthalmology.

Vitreous hemorrhage & other pathology

Diffuse echogenic particles — "snowstorm" or "starry sky" pattern with swirling motion on eye movement ("washing machine sign"). Causes: diabetic retinopathy (most common), trauma, retinal tear with vessel disruption, PVD, AMD, retinal vein occlusion.

CRAO diagnosis is clinical: painless sudden monocular vision loss, cherry-red spot on fundoscopy, afferent pupillary defect. POCUS is usually normal-appearing; rarely a hyperechoic embolus is seen (highly specific, insensitive). Color Doppler (if available) may show absent/reduced flow. Ocular POCUS in CRAO primarily rules out other causes of vision loss.

Disposition

  1. Emergent ophthalmology consultation
  2. Position patient with detached area down (if known)
  3. Bilateral eye patching (reduces eye movement), keep calm, minimal head movement
  4. Macula-on (central vision intact): emergent surgery within 24 hours
  5. Macula-off (central vision already lost): urgent surgery within 72 hours
  6. Do NOT discharge without ophthalmology evaluation

PVD is usually benign and does NOT require emergent treatment, but 10-15% have concurrent retinal tear/detachment. Discharge with ophthalmology follow-up within 24-48 hours and return precautions: sudden increase in floaters, new flashes of light, curtain/shadow in vision, decrease in visual acuity. Higher risk for concurrent RD: high myopia, prior cataract surgery, family history of RD, prior RD in other eye, trauma mechanism.

Ocular POCUS algorithm

Ocular POCUS: indications & CI — Absolute CI: suspected globe rupture — any pressure risks extrusion of intraocular contents.

Probe selection & technique — High-frequency linear probe 7.5-15 MHz, copious gel standoff, minimal pressure, transverse/longitudinal planes, depth 4-5cm.

Systematic 6-structure scan — Anterior chamber, iris/pupil, lens, vitreous, retina, optic nerve. Dynamic assessment differentiates PVD from RD.

Normal anatomy reviewed — Normal vitreous is completely anechoic — any echogenicity is abnormal.

Primary indication? — Vision loss/floaters, elevated ICP/headache, eye trauma, or suspected CRAO.

Approach to acute vision loss — Is there something in the vitreous that shouldn't be there? RD vs PVD vs VH.

Retinal detachment: findings — V-shaped/tent-shaped, tethered at optic disc, thick hyperechoic, restricted "leashed" movement. Surgical emergency.

PVD: findings — Thin undulating membrane, NOT tethered to disc, "seaweed in water" motion, continues after eye stops. Usually benign.

RD vs PVD side-by-side — When in doubt, treat as RD — the consequences of missing RD far outweigh unnecessary consultation.

Vitreous hemorrhage — Diffuse echogenic particles, "snowstorm" pattern, swirls with eye movement. Cannot see retina = cannot rule out RD.

Other ocular pathology — Lens dislocation, intraocular foreign body, choroidal detachment, globe rupture signs.

ONSD for elevated ICP — Optic nerve is a direct CNS extension — elevated ICP distends the sheath within minutes.

ONSD measurement technique — 3mm posterior to globe, outer edge to outer edge, bilateral average. Adult >5.0mm, child >4.5mm, infant >4.0mm.

ONSD result? — Adult <5.0mm normal, 5.0-5.8mm borderline, >5.8mm strongly suggests elevated ICP; use age-specific thresholds in children.
