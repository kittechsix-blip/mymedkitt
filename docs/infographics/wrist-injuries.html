Wrist Injuries: Mechanism-Based Fracture, Dislocation & Ligament Differential

Adults presenting to the ED with acute wrist trauma (fracture, dislocation, or ligamentous injury). Excludes pediatric physeal/growth-plate injuries, isolated hand or finger fractures distal to the carpus, and simple soft-tissue sprains where no bony or ligamentous injury is suspected.

⚠ Perilunate/lunate dislocation is a true orthopedic emergency: acute carpal tunnel syndrome occurs in 15-50% of cases from median nerve compression by the displaced lunate or hematoma. Reduce emergently (Tavernier method) — do not delay for imaging optimization or specialist arrival. Median nerve symptoms resolve in 92% after reduction; decompress within 40 hours if symptoms persist.
⚠ Barton fracture (intra-articular rim fracture with carpal subluxation) is inherently unstable and cannot be adequately treated with closed reduction — do NOT attempt closed reduction in the ED. Splint for comfort only and obtain urgent orthopedic consultation.
⚠ Always assess the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) on the lateral radiograph with ANY distal radius or ulnar fracture. A distal radius fracture with DRUJ dislocation (Galeazzi fracture-dislocation) is the most commonly missed pattern in this differential and leads to chronic pain, instability, and restricted forearm rotation if overlooked.
⚠ Do not dismiss scapholunate dissociation as a simple wrist sprain. Untreated, it progresses to SLAC wrist (scapholunate advanced collapse) — severe degenerative arthritis. The surgical window for primary ligament repair is <6 weeks from injury; urgent hand specialist referral (24-48h) is required for all suspected cases.
⚠ A negative scaphoid series does not exclude a scaphoid fracture — plain X-rays are only 70% sensitive. Splint ALL clinically suspected scaphoid fractures (snuffbox tenderness, tubercle tenderness, pain with axial thumb load) in a thumb spica even when radiographs are negative, and arrange MRI/CT at 3-5 days.
⚠ Compartment syndrome is rare but devastating: assess for the 5 Ps (pain out of proportion, pressure, paresthesias, pallor, pulselessness — the last a late finding). A compartment pressure >30 mmHg absolute, or within 30 mmHg of diastolic BP, requires emergent fasciotomy.
⚠ Emergent orthopedic consultation is required for: open fractures, neurovascular compromise (absent pulses, motor deficit, acute carpal tunnel syndrome), compartment syndrome, tense swelling with skin tenting, and all perilunate/lunate dislocations (after the ED reduction attempt) or irreducible dislocations.

1 · Recognition — mechanism guides injury pattern

Wrist injuries account for ~15% of ED orthopedic visits. Mechanism guides the injury pattern:

FOOSH (fall on outstretched hand) → distal radius, scaphoid, perilunate dislocation
Fall on dorsum of hand → Smith fracture, triquetral fracture
Direct blow to hypothenar → hamate hook fracture
High-energy hyperextension → perilunate/lunate dislocation
Forced rotation → DRUJ dislocation, TFCC tear

Red flags requiring emergent management
Neurovascular compromise (absent pulses, motor deficit, median nerve symptoms), open fracture, compartment syndrome, obvious dislocation, or tense swelling with skin tenting.

2 · Distal radius fractures

Three main patterns — Colles (dorsal angulation, "dinner fork" deformity, most common), Smith (volar displacement, "garden spade" deformity, more unstable), and Barton (intra-articular rim fracture where the carpal bones dislocate with the rim fragment, inherently unstable).

Always assess median nerve status, ulnar styloid fracture, and DRUJ stability — acute carpal tunnel syndrome occurs at up to 20% incidence in distal radius fractures.

Colles — Charnley reduction technique
Procedural sedation ± hematoma block. Continuous traction → disimpact by exaggerating dorsal angulation → Robert-Jones maneuver (push distal fragment volarly) → return wrist to neutral/slight extension → sugar tong splint.

Acceptable reduction: Radial shortening <3mm, Dorsal tilt <10° (or <5° if intra-articular), Intra-articular step-off <2mm. Urgent ortho if unable to achieve this, intra-articular involvement, significant comminution, or loss of reduction after splinting.
Smith — expect surgical referral
More difficult to maintain closed reduction than Colles; high rate of operative intervention. If attempting ED reduction: traction, force distal fragment dorsally, return to neutral, volar splint. Low threshold for urgent orthopedic consultation (24-48h) — do not pursue prolonged conservative management in the ED.
Barton — do NOT attempt closed reduction
Dorsal or volar articular rim fracture with carpal subluxation — inherently unstable, high likelihood of operative fixation. Neurovascular assessment, sugar tong splint for comfort only, and urgent orthopedic consultation now. Missing this pattern (mistaking it for a simple distal radius fracture) risks post-traumatic arthritis from inadequate reduction.

4 · Perilunate/lunate dislocation — reduce emergently

Two stages of the same high-energy hyperextension injury spectrum. Perilunate dislocation: capitate + distal row dislocate dorsally, lunate stays aligned with the radius. Lunate dislocation (more severe): lunate rotates volarly into the carpal tunnel — the "spilled teacup" sign.

Acute carpal tunnel syndrome occurs in 15-50% of cases from median nerve compression by the displaced lunate or hematoma.

  1. Procedural sedation; document neurovascular status (especially median nerve)
  2. Finger trap traction × 10 minutes, elbow flexed 90°, then release traction
  3. Extend wrist while stabilizing the lunate volarly with the thumb
  4. Gradually flex the wrist so the capitate reduces into the lunate concavity; push the lunate dorsally while flexing
  5. Return to neutral; apply dorsal short arm thumb spica; post-reduction films; reassess median nerve
Emergent ortho after reduction — most need surgical repair
Tavernier method achieves >80% ED closed reduction success. Median nerve symptoms resolve in 92% after reduction; if acute carpal tunnel syndrome persists, decompress within 40 hours. Call orthopedics immediately after reduction — most require surgical repair (ligament reconstruction, K-wire fixation); if irreducible in the ED, urgent OR.

5 · DRUJ dislocation & ligament injuries

DRUJ dislocation — easily missed
Dorsal (more common, forced pronation + hyperextension) vs volar (forced supination). Reduce with longitudinal traction plus full supination (dorsal) or full pronation (volar). Above-elbow cast × 4-6 weeks in the position of stability. Urgent ortho referral, especially if unstable, irreducible, or associated with a distal radius fracture (Galeazzi fracture-dislocation).
Scapholunate dissociation — time-sensitive
Radial-sided pain, positive Watson (scaphoid shift) test. PA radiograph: scapholunate gap ≥5mm ("Terry Thomas sign"; normal gap: ≤2mm). Lateral: scapholunate angle >70° with DISI pattern (normal: 30-60°). Thumb spica splint, URGENT hand specialist referral (24-48h) — the surgical window for primary repair is <6 weeks; chronic injuries need more complex reconstruction.
TFCC tear — conservative trial first
Ulnar-sided pain, clicking with forearm rotation, often associated with DRUJ instability. Ulnar gutter or sugar tong splint, NSAIDs, activity modification — conservative treatment trial × 3-6 weeks; many improve without surgery. URGENT referral (24-48h) if DRUJ is unstable; routine (1-2 weeks) if conservative treatment fails or mechanical symptoms (clicking, locking) persist.

6 · Imaging & splinting

Standard series: PA, lateral, oblique. Special views: scaphoid view (PA with ulnar deviation) for suspected scaphoid fracture; carpal tunnel view for hook of hamate; clenched fist PA accentuates scapholunate dissociation.

Systematic review: three smooth carpal arcs on PA (disruption suggests dislocation); lunate should articulate with the radius on lateral ("cup of radius" — volar tilt = lunate dislocation); always check the DRUJ on the lateral view — the most commonly missed finding alongside a distal radius or ulnar fracture.

Hamate — standard views miss it — Carpal tunnel view or CT needed. Short arm cast 6 weeks if non-displaced; surgical excision if displaced/non-union. Routine referral.

Scapholunate dissociation — Gap ≥5mm, angle >70° (DISI). Thumb spica, urgent referral 24-48h. Surgical window <6 weeks — do not dismiss as a sprain.

TFCC tear — Ulnar gutter splint, NSAIDs, conservative trial 3-6 weeks. Urgent referral if DRUJ unstable; routine if trial fails.

Splint selection & NV check — Volar / sugar tong / thumb spica / ulnar gutter — choice depends on the specific injury above, not a single shared recommendation. Confirm cap refill, sensation, motion.

Complications screen — Acute CTS, AVN, missed Galeazzi/Monteggia, compartment syndrome, CRPS.

Referral tier & disposition — Emergent / urgent (24-48h) / routine (3-7d) per injury. Discharge instructions and return precautions for all.

9 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT attempt closed reduction of a Barton fracture in the ED — it is inherently unstable and requires surgical fixation.

Do NOT pursue prolonged conservative management of a Smith fracture in the ED — refer early; most require surgical fixation.

Do NOT skip DRUJ assessment on any distal radius or ulnar fracture — missing a Galeazzi fracture-dislocation causes chronic pain, malunion, and restricted rotation.

Do NOT dismiss scapholunate dissociation as a simple wrist sprain — untreated it progresses to SLAC wrist; the surgical repair window is <6 weeks.

Do NOT rely on a negative wrist X-ray to exclude a scaphoid fracture — plain films are only 70% sensitive; splint clinically suspected fractures regardless.

Do NOT delay reduction of a perilunate/lunate dislocation — delay increases the risk of permanent median nerve injury and avascular necrosis.

Do NOT assume standard wrist X-rays exclude a hook of hamate fracture — they frequently miss it; get a carpal tunnel view or CT in athletes with hypothenar pain and grip weakness.
