Cauda Equina Syndrome — ED Recognition & Management

Adults with acute back pain in the ED being screened for cauda equina syndrome (CES). Excludes known chronic stable radiculopathy with no new red flags. Not a substitute for a documented full neuro/rectal exam.

⚠ Ask EVERY back pain patient about bladder, bowel, and saddle sensation — CES is easily missed
⚠ Post-void residual >500 mL + bilateral sciatica = 48x odds of CES — get MRI STAT
⚠ Bulbocavernosus reflex INTACT = CES excluded (100% sensitivity) — if absent, workup required
⚠ MRI within 4 hours of suspected CES — call neurosurgery PARALLEL with imaging, not sequential
⚠ CES-Incomplete (still voiding) = true emergency — surgery <24h prevents progression to CES-Retention
⚠ Normal post-void residual does NOT exclude CES — 50% of CES-Incomplete have PVR <200 mL

Classic triad (when present = high suspicion): bilateral leg pain/weakness or sciatica; saddle anesthesia (S3-S5); bladder/bowel dysfunction. Imaging strategy

MRI is the gold standard. Suspected CES → MRI within 4 hours; do not defer to "next day". Call neurosurgery WHILE imaging is being obtained — do not wait for the MRI result before consultation.

CT when MRI unavailable 24/7
CT with optimized lumbar protocol (thin-cut reconstructions) achieves 97% sensitivity/specificity for disc pathology. Thecal sac effacement <50% reliably rules out CES (NPV 99%). CT can miss non-discal causes (abscess, tumor); if CT negative but high clinical suspicion, transfer for MRI.
Negative MRI with high clinical suspicion
Consider repeat imaging (dynamic compression), spinal infarction, conus medullaris syndrome, aortic dissection, or Guillain-Barré. Admit for serial neuro exams q4-6h, neurology consult, consider repeat MRI in 24-48h. Do NOT discharge patients with bilateral neuro deficits and bladder symptoms just because MRI is negative.

Management algorithm

Suspected CES — screen every back pain patient — Classic triad: bilateral leg pain/weakness, saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction. No single symptom is pathognomonic.

Mandatory red-flag screening (bladder/bowel/saddle) — Ask about bladder, bowel, sensation, motor, sexual function. PVR >500 + bilateral sciatica = 48x odds of CES.

Physical exam: saddle, rectal tone, BCR, PVR — BCR intact = CES excluded (100% sensitivity). Absent = full workup. PVR <200mL still misses 50% of CES-I.

High, intermediate, or low suspicion? — Synthesize BCR, rectal tone, PVR, and bilateral findings into one of three suspicion tiers.

MRI within 4 hours; neurosurgery in parallel — MRI is gold standard; CT (97% sens/spec) acceptable if MRI unavailable 24/7. Call neurosurgery WHILE imaging is obtained.

Imaging result? — Compression confirmed vs negative imaging with high or low ongoing suspicion.

Neurosurgical decompression — Surgery <24h for CES-I, as soon as feasible for CES-R. >48h delay: 9.6x mortality, 2.4x non-routine discharge. Foley + steroids if inflammatory.

Negative MRI, high suspicion → admit, repeat MRI — Consider dynamic compression, spinal infarction, conus medullaris, aortic dissection, GBS. Do NOT discharge bilateral deficits + bladder symptoms on a negative MRI alone.

Disposition planning — Confirmed = surgery; concerning but negative = admit; low suspicion = written precautions; no neurosurgery on-site = transfer.

Admit for observation — Serial neuro exams q4-6h, strict I/O with bladder scans, low threshold for repeat imaging, minimum 12-24h.

Transfer to neurosurgical center — Foley placed, steroids if indicated, imaging sent, direct MD-to-MD handoff. Do NOT delay transfer for additional workup.

Intermediate suspicion → urgent MRI 4-6h — Serial exams q2-4h; low threshold for consultation; upgrade to emergent workup if any worsening.

Low suspicion — outpatient + written precautions — Unilateral pain only, intact BCR, normal PVR, no red flags — NSAIDs, PT, spine follow-up, mandatory written return precautions.
