TCA Overdose: Widen the QRS, Alkalinize Fast

Adults in the ED with known or suspected tricyclic antidepressant (sodium-channel-blocker) overdose. Excludes pediatric weight nuances requiring specialist input, pregnancy, and isolated non-TCA ingestions with a different antidote pathway. Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222.

⚠ Na⁺ channel blockade → QRS widening → VT/VF. Lethal dose is only 3–5× therapeutic; a patient can progress from alert to cardiac arrest within 1 hour.
⚠ 12-lead ECG STAT — the single most important test. QRS >100 ms = seizure risk, QRS >160 ms = VT/VF risk; R wave >3 mm in aVR is a sensitive marker of Na⁺ channel blockade (may be positive even with a normal QRS). Repeat q15–30 min.
⚠ TCA serum levels are NOT useful for acute management — they do not correlate with toxicity. Do not wait on them.
⚠ Never hypoventilate after intubation. Respiratory acidosis frees more unbound drug and worsens toxicity — hyperventilate to ETCO₂ ~25–30 mmHg while bicarb takes effect.
⚠ Do NOT give phenytoin/fosphenytoin, procainamide, flecainide, or amiodarone — Class IA/IC and phenytoin block sodium channels and can precipitate arrest.
⚠ Seizures are a lethal feedback loop: they cause acidosis → more free TCA → worse cardiac toxicity. Terminate fast with benzodiazepines and alkalinize simultaneously.

1 · Recognize & stabilize

Five mechanisms, one killer
Sodium-channel blockade → QRS widening → ventricular arrhythmia is the lethal one.[16] The rest ride along: anticholinergic effects (tachycardia, mydriasis, AMS), α₁ blockade → hypotension, GABA-A antagonism → seizures, K⁺ channel blockade → QT prolongation.[3]
Anticholinergic toxidrome
"Blind as a bat, dry as a bone, hot as a hare, red as a beet, mad as a hatter."[3] Sinus tachycardia is the most common ECG finding and may be the only abnormality early.[1] Do NOT be reassured — rapid deterioration is possible; go straight to the 12-lead ECG.
  1. Continuous cardiac monitoring + two large-bore IVs.
  2. 12-lead ECG STAT — the single most important test.[1][3]
  3. BMP, blood gas (pH), lactate, glucose; acetaminophen + salicylate levels for co-ingestion.
  4. Intubate for GCS ≤12, seizures, or hypoventilation — then hyperventilate, never hypoventilate.
Activated charcoal (airway protected only)
1 g/kg PO
PO/NG, max 50 g
Give if the airway is protected. Anticholinergic effects delay gastric emptying, so charcoal may help beyond the usual 1–2 h window.[3][16] Do NOT induce emesis.[4]

2 · ECG risk stratification

The 12-lead ECG is the single most important test in TCA toxicity.[1][3] QRS >100 ms → seizure risk; QRS >160 ms → ventricular-arrhythmia risk;[1] an R wave >3 mm in aVR is a sensitive marker of Na⁺ channel blockade and may predict seizures/arrhythmia even when the QRS looks normal.[2] Enter the measured QRS above — the risk band and the algorithm below update live.

Check aVR even if QRS is narrow
R wave >3 mm in aVR, R/S ratio >0.7 in aVR, or a rightward terminal-40 ms axis all point to Na⁺ channel blockade. Liebelt found aVR may be more sensitive than QRS for predicting seizures and arrhythmias.[2] If QRS <100 ms: serial ECGs q15–30 min for 2 h, then q2h to a minimum of 6 h of observation.[3]

3 · What to do now (live)

Set the toggles and QRS above. Exactly one instruction below shows — the highest-acuity active state wins (seizure → hypotension → QRS band).

Active seizure — break it now
Give Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (see live dose below) and push sodium bicarbonate simultaneously to reverse the seizure-driven acidosis. Seizures worsen cardiac toxicity in a lethal feedback loop.[16][18] NO phenytoin.
Hypotensive — bicarb + norepinephrine
Balanced crystalloid or isotonic bicarb bolus, then Norepinephrine first-line (α-agonism counters α-blockade). Patients are often refractory to fluids alone — do not delay the pressor.[16] Give a sodium bicarbonate bolus if not already done.
QRS >160 ms — aggressive alkalinization
Sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mEq/kg IV push, repeat q3–5 min until QRS narrows; if intubated, hyperventilate simultaneously (fastest way to alkalinize). Add early Lidocaine if QRS is not responding, and prep vasopressors.[1][16]
QRS 100–160 ms — start sodium bicarbonate
Sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mEq/kg IV push, repeat q3–5 min until the QRS narrows.[4][16] Recheck the ECG q15–30 min. See the weight-based bolus in section 4.
QRS <100 ms — observe, do not relax
Low risk, but toxicity can develop fast. Serial ECGs q15–30 min ×2 h, then q2h for a minimum of 6 h; continuous telemetry. Check the aVR R wave >3 mm. If the QRS widens at any point, jump to the bicarbonate protocol.[3]

4 · Sodium bicarbonate — the cornerstone

Indications & dual mechanism
Give for QRS >100 ms, ventricular arrhythmia, fluid-refractory hypotension, or seizures (adjunct).[4][16] Two actions: (1) sodium loading competes for cardiac Na⁺ channels; (2) alkalosis raises protein binding so less free/toxic drug circulates. There is no fixed ceiling — one survivor received 2650 mEq (53 amps).[8]
Sodium bicarbonate — IV push bolus

IV push, repeat q3–5 min until QRS narrows

Lidocaine — for QRS >160 ms not responding to bicarb
1.5 mg/kg
IV push (high end), then 1–4 mg/min
Class IB agent that competes for the Na⁺ channel and paradoxically improves channel function in TCA blockade.[6][16] Shows when QRS >160 ms.
Targets & monitoring
Aim QRS <100 ms, serum pH 7.50–7.55, Na⁺ ≤155 mEq/L (amps are hypertonic, 1000 mOsm/L).[16][12] Alkalosis drives acute hypokalemia — replete K⁺ simultaneously.[19] Excessive alkalinization (pH >7.55) may raise arrhythmia/seizure risk.[13]

Maintenance after boluses narrow the QRS: 150 mEq (3 amps) in 1 L D5W at 150–250 mL/hr.[4][16] Isotonic bicarb is inferior to hypertonic boluses for acute widening — use it for maintenance only. Continue until QRS stays <100 ms for ≥6 h; TCA half-life is 24–36 h, so toxicity is prolonged.[3]

6 · Hemodynamics & rescue

Refractory hypotension may be the most common cause of death.[16] Three mechanisms: α₁ blockade → vasodilation, Na⁺ channel blockade → poor contractility, and arrhythmias → low output. Most patients are not hypovolemic, so fluids alone often fail.

Vasopressor selection
Norepinephrine 0.1–0.5 mcg/kg/min first-line — α-agonism directly counters TCA α-blockade.[16] Epinephrine 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min if bradycardic or NE-refractory (combined α+β); phenylephrine if markedly tachycardic (pure α, no TCA-specific dose defined — use standard push-dose protocol); Vasopressin 0.03–0.04 units/min (fixed, non-titratable) for refractory cases — bypasses the α receptor entirely, add to norepinephrine rather than using alone. Avoid pure β-agonists — unopposed β can drop the pressure further.
Wide-complex tachycardia
Wide QRS in TCA is usually sinus tachycardia with delayed conduction, not true VT.[16] Treat with sodium bicarbonate first, then Lidocaine for the Na⁺ channel; magnesium 2 g IV is an adjunct. Avoid amiodarone and Class IA/IC agents.[3]
Lipid emulsion 20% — rescue only
1.5 mL/kg
IV bolus over 1 min, then 0.25 mL/kg/min ×30–60 min
Reserve for cardiac arrest or refractory toxicity. May repeat bolus ×2 at 5-min intervals if no ROSC. Human evidence is limited; more rational for lipophilic agents like TCAs.[14][16]

VA-ECMO is the last resort for refractory arrest or collapse. TCA toxicity is potentially reversible — aggressive bridge therapy while the drug is metabolized (half-life 24–36 h) can yield full neurologic recovery. Contact the ECMO team early.[9][10]
