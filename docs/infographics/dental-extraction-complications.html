Dental Extraction Complications — ED Management

Adults presenting to the ED after a tooth extraction with post-extraction bleeding, dry socket (alveolar osteitis), infection, suspected oroantral communication, or an expected/uncomplicated recovery course. Excludes undifferentiated dental pain (routed to Dental Pain Hub) and dental trauma/avulsion (routed to Dental Trauma / Dental Avulsion).

⚠ Floor-of-mouth/tongue elevation, drooling, muffled voice, dysphagia, stridor, severe trismus, neck swelling, or toxic appearance after extraction is deep odontogenic infection until proven otherwise — airway-capable team first, CT only after airway safety addressed.
⚠ Post-extraction bleeding is managed with direct socket pressure first, then local hemostatic packing/suture, before systemic anticoagulant reversal — unless bleeding is life-threatening.
⚠ Dry socket is severe delayed socket pain with exposed bone and minimal swelling; antibiotics are not routine unless infection signs (cellulitis, fever, purulence with spread, immunocompromise) are present.
⚠ Suspected oroantral communication needs sinus precautions and urgent dental/OMFS follow-up — do not aggressively probe or forcefully irrigate through the socket.
⚠ For anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients, avoid reflex anticoagulant interruption or reversal for controlled oral bleeding; use local control first and involve the prescribing service only for major or persistent bleeding.

Sick check — sort the lane

First job: decide whether this is bleeding, dry socket, infection, sinus communication, or expected recovery. This consult covers complications after a tooth extraction — for undifferentiated dental pain use Dental Pain Hub; for trauma/avulsion use Dental Trauma or Dental Avulsion.

  1. Airway-risk odontogenic infection
  2. Persistent socket hemorrhage in an anticoagulated or coagulopathic patient
  3. Secondary hemorrhage from infection 5–10 days after extraction
  4. Dry socket masquerading as infection
  5. Oroantral communication after maxillary posterior extraction
Critical actions
Post-extraction bleeding is managed with direct socket pressure first, then local hemostatic packing/suture, before systemic reversal, unless bleeding is life-threatening. Dry socket is severe delayed socket pain with exposed bone/minimal swelling — antibiotics are not routine unless infection signs are present. Floor-of-mouth elevation, dysphagia, drooling, voice change, trismus, neck swelling, or toxicity after extraction is deep odontogenic infection until proven otherwise. For anticoagulated patients, avoid reflex anticoagulant interruption/reversal for controlled oral bleeding. Suspected oroantral communication needs sinus precautions and urgent follow-up — do not aggressively probe or irrigate through the socket.

▶ What to do now

Deep odontogenic infection / airway risk
Sit upright, continuous reassessment, airway-capable team early. ENT/OMFS/anesthesia per local workflow. IV antibiotics with anaerobic and oral flora coverage. CT face/neck with contrast only if stable enough and after airway risk is addressed. Admit or transfer for airway/source control.
Major oral bleeding / resuscitation pathway
Suction and airway plan — blood in the mouth can become an airway problem. Two IVs, CBC, PT/INR, PTT, fibrinogen if severe, type/screen or crossmatch. Direct socket pressure while preparing local control. OMFS/dentistry consult; ENT/anesthesia if airway concern. Consider reversal only for life-threatening bleeding or failed local control.
Local hemostatic escalation
Anesthetize, irrigate lightly, pack the socket (Gelfoam, Surgicel, collagen sponge, or topical thrombin), add TXA-soaked gauze under firm bite pressure, figure-of-8 suture if trained, recheck after another 20–30 minutes of uninterrupted pressure. Do not reflexively reverse anticoagulation for a controllable socket bleed.
Anticoagulated / antiplatelet patient
Most dental extraction bleeding is controlled locally. Stopping anticoagulation can create stroke/VTE/stent risk — avoid casual reversal or medication interruption. Reverse only for life-threatening, uncontrolled bleeding after local measures or if another major bleeding source exists.
Alveolar osteitis / dry socket
Analgesia (NSAID plus acetaminophen when safe, dental block can help). Gently irrigate loose debris with saline — do not aggressively curette. Dental/OMFS follow-up for medicated dressing. Antibiotics are not routine unless cellulitis, fever, purulence with spread, immunocompromise, or deep infection concern.
Localized / early spreading infection
Manage as a source-control problem — look for fluctuance or retained debris; drainage/source control is definitive. Antibiotics are appropriate when systemic involvement, fever/malaise, spreading cellulitis, immunocompromise, or lack of timely dental care creates risk. Urgent dental/OMFS follow-up is still required. Avoid antibiotics for pain alone or uncomplicated dry socket.
Oroantral communication / sinus connection
Do not aggressively probe the socket or forcefully irrigate through the opening. Sinus precautions: no nose blowing, no straw, sneeze with mouth open, avoid smoking, avoid vigorous rinsing. Analgesia and infection assessment. Urgent dental/OMFS follow-up for closure decision.
Expected post-extraction course
Bite pressure for recurrent mild oozing. Avoid smoking, straws, vigorous rinsing, spitting, and heavy exertion during the early clot-stabilization window. Soft foods, hydration, head elevation, NSAID plus acetaminophen when safe. Dental follow-up if pain worsens after day 2–3 or function declines.

Post-extraction bleeding

Sit upright, suction, good light; remove loose clot only if it prevents seeing the socket. Roll damp gauze small enough to sit directly in the socket — the gauze is a pressure plug, not a sponge. If available, use lidocaine with epinephrine by local infiltration or block for anesthesia and vasoconstriction.

  1. Identify whether blood is from socket, gingival tear, laceration, or multiple sites
  2. Seat a small damp gauze plug directly over the socket
  3. Have patient bite continuously for 30–60 minutes — do not keep checking every few minutes
  4. If pressure fails, move to local hemostatic packing

After sustained pressure, sort into major bleed/resuscitation, persistent local bleeding needing hemostatic escalation, or bleeding now controlled.

  1. Anesthetize with lidocaine plus epinephrine if safe and available
  2. Irrigate lightly enough to see the site — avoid blasting out fresh clot
  3. Pack the socket with Gelfoam, Surgicel/oxidized cellulose, collagen sponge, or topical thrombin on gelatin sponge if available
  4. Add TXA-soaked gauze/dental roll held under firm bite pressure
  5. Figure-of-8 suture over the socket if trained/equipped
  6. Recheck after another 20–30 minutes of uninterrupted pressure
  1. Suction and airway plan — blood in the mouth can become an airway problem
  2. Two IVs, CBC, PT/INR, PTT, fibrinogen if severe, type/screen or crossmatch
  3. Direct socket pressure while preparing local control
  4. OMFS/dentistry consult; ENT/anesthesia if airway concern
  5. Consider reversal only for life-threatening bleeding or failed local control

Anticoagulated / antiplatelet / bleeding-risk patients

Check anticoagulants, antiplatelets, liver disease, thrombocytopenia, uremia, hemophilia/vWD, and recent chemotherapy. Bleeding-risk medications increase monitoring needs but do not automatically require reversal.

Most dental extraction bleeding is controlled locally; stopping anticoagulation can create stroke/VTE/stent risk. Warfarin: check INR if bleeding is persistent, excessive, or status unknown. DOAC: note last dose, renal function, and interacting drugs. DAPT/recent stent: do not stop without cardiology/prescribing-team discussion. Known hemophilia/vWD or severe thrombocytopenia: involve hematology early — antifibrinolytics are often useful for oral mucosal bleeding (systemic TXA adjunct dosed 10–15 mg/kg IV or PO q8–12h), but avoid with hematuria.

Hematuria = contraindication
Systemic tranexamic acid is contraindicated with active hematuria in bleeding-disorder patients (risk of renal thrombi) — check UA before starting.

Alveolar osteitis / dry socket

Typical pattern: severe throbbing pain 2–5 days after extraction, radiating to ear/temple, bad taste/odor, empty socket or exposed bone, minimal swelling, usually afebrile. Dry socket is painful but is not the same as a spreading odontogenic infection.

  1. Analgesia: NSAID plus acetaminophen when safe; dental block can help
  2. Gently irrigate loose debris with saline — do not aggressively curette the socket
  3. Dental/OMFS follow-up for medicated dressing if available
Antibiotics are not routine
Antibiotics are not routine for dry socket unless there is cellulitis, fever, purulence with spread, immunocompromise, or deep infection concern.

Infection — localized vs. deep-space

Separate localized socket/vestibular infection from deep-space infection. Deep-space infection is an airway problem; dry socket is pain without spread.

  • Floor-of-mouth firmness/elevation, tongue elevation
  • Drooling, muffled voice, dysphagia, odynophagia, stridor
  • Severe trismus, submandibular swelling
  • Toxic appearance, rapidly progressive swelling
  1. Sit upright, continuous reassessment, airway-capable team early
  2. ENT/OMFS/anesthesia depending on local workflow
  3. IV antibiotics with anaerobic and oral flora coverage per local protocol
  4. CT face/neck with IV contrast only if stable enough and after airway risk is addressed
  5. Admit or transfer for airway/source control

Open Deep Neck Infection when the phenotype fits.

Manage as a source-control problem: analgesia and oral intake assessment; look for fluctuance or retained debris (drainage/source control is definitive). Antibiotics are appropriate when systemic involvement, fever/malaise, spreading cellulitis, immunocompromise, or lack of timely dental source care creates risk — common outpatient adult choices when indicated include amoxicillin or penicillin VK, with broader coverage or clindamycin as allergy-profile alternatives depending on severity and local policy. Urgent dental/OMFS follow-up is still required. Avoid antibiotics for pain alone, uncomplicated dry socket, or expected post-op soreness.

Oroantral communication

Suspect after maxillary premolar/molar extraction when the patient reports fluid from mouth to nose, air/bubbling through the socket, whistling, unilateral sinus symptoms, or a visible opening into the sinus.

  1. Do not aggressively probe the socket
  2. Avoid forceful irrigation through the opening
  3. Sinus precautions: no nose blowing, no straw, sneeze with mouth open, avoid smoking, avoid vigorous rinsing
  4. Analgesia and infection assessment
  5. Urgent dental/OMFS follow-up for closure decision

Expected post-extraction course

Reassuring pattern: mild oozing, soreness, swelling that is not rapidly progressive, no fever, no trismus/dysphagia, no deep-space signs, pain improving or controlled with nonopioid therapy.

  • Bite pressure for recurrent mild oozing
  • Avoid smoking, straws, vigorous rinsing, spitting, and heavy exertion during the early clot-stabilization window
  • Soft foods, hydration, head elevation
  • NSAID plus acetaminophen when safe
  • Clear dental follow-up if pain worsens after day 2–3 or function declines
Return precautions
Return for infection, airway symptoms, uncontrolled bleeding, or inability to drink.

Medication reference (general guidance — not tied to a single flow step)

This node is not wired to a specific decision branch in the source consult — it is general medication guidance referenced across the bleeding, dry socket, and infection pathways. NSAID plus acetaminophen is first-line for acute dental pain after extraction when safe (avoid NSAIDs when contraindicated: major renal disease, active GI bleed/high-risk anticoagulation, late pregnancy, severe allergy). Antibiotics: do not use for dry socket or pain alone — use for systemic involvement, spreading cellulitis, immunocompromise, or selected abscess/infection cases when timely dental source care is not available. Antifibrinolytics (TXA) are useful local adjuncts for oral bleeding in anticoagulated or mucosal-bleeding contexts — avoid swallowing large amounts, and avoid with active hematuria in bleeding-disorder patients. Opioids: reserve for narrow rescue situations after nonopioid and block/local care strategy.

Disposition depends on airway risk, bleeding control, systemic infection, pain control, oral intake, and follow-up reliability.

Escalate now for: airway concern, Ludwig/deep neck infection, sepsis, rapidly progressive facial/neck swelling, orbital involvement, uncontrolled hemorrhage, severe coagulopathy, inability to tolerate PO, failed outpatient therapy, or need for operative source control. Document airway findings, bleeding control steps, anticoagulant status, infection extent, imaging/labs, and consultant plan.

  • What was treated today
  • Exact dental follow-up destination and timing
  • Medication schedule and why antibiotics were or were not used
  • Bleeding plan: bite on damp gauze for 30–60 minutes without checking; return if repeated pressure fails
  • Return precautions: fever, worse swelling, neck/floor-of-mouth swelling, trouble swallowing, drooling, voice change, breathing issue, uncontrolled bleeding, eye swelling/vision change, inability to drink

Discharge is reasonable when bleeding is controlled, no deep infection signs, no systemic toxicity, pain is controlled enough for oral intake, and the patient understands return precautions. Instructions: protect the clot, use scheduled nonopioid analgesia if safe, avoid smoking/straws/vigorous rinsing/spitting early, return for uncontrolled bleeding or spreading infection symptoms, and contact dental/oral surgery if pain worsens after day 2–3.

Airway/deep infection, uncontrolled bleeding, unstable vitals, orbital/neck spread, PO intolerance, immunocompromised with significant infection | Admit, transfer, or emergent specialty pathway

Bleeding controlled, localized infection/dry socket/OAC concern, stable but needs dental source care | Urgent dental/OMFS follow-up

Expected recovery, controlled symptoms, no red flags, follow-up available | Routine discharge

Escalate now for: airway concern, Ludwig/deep neck infection, sepsis, rapidly progressive facial/neck swelling, orbital involvement, uncontrolled hemorrhage, severe coagulopathy, inability to tolerate PO, failed outpatient therapy, or need for operative source control. Document airway findings, bleeding control steps, anticoagulant status, infection extent, imaging/labs, and consultant plan.

  • What was treated today
  • Exact dental follow-up destination and timing
  • Medication schedule and why antibiotics were or were not used
  • Bleeding plan: bite on damp gauze for 30–60 minutes without checking; return if repeated pressure fails
  • Return precautions: fever, worse swelling, neck/floor-of-mouth swelling, trouble swallowing, drooling, voice change, breathing issue, uncontrolled bleeding, eye swelling/vision change, inability to drink

Discharge is reasonable when bleeding is controlled, no deep infection signs, no systemic toxicity, pain is controlled enough for oral intake, and the patient understands return precautions. Instructions: protect the clot, use scheduled nonopioid analgesia if safe, avoid smoking/straws/vigorous rinsing/spitting early, return for uncontrolled bleeding or spreading infection symptoms, and contact dental/oral surgery if pain worsens after day 2–3.
