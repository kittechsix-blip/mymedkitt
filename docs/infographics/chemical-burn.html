Chemical Eye Burn — Ocular Chemical Injury

ED patients with ocular chemical (alkali or acid) exposure. Excludes suspected globe rupture — that population must be shielded and sent for immediate ophthalmology consultation without irrigation.

⚠ IRRIGATE BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE — do not delay for history, visual acuity, or pH testing
⚠ Alkali burns are WORSE than acid — lipophilic penetration can reach the anterior chamber in <15 seconds
⚠ Cement/lime exposure — sweep the fornices for particulate matter that continues releasing alkali
⚠ Target pH 7.0-7.4 — wait 5-10 minutes after stopping irrigation before checking pH (reservoir effect)
⚠ Prednisolone 1% MUST be tapered by day 10-14 — risk of corneal perforation if continued
⚠ Grade III-IV injury (opaque cornea, >50% limbal ischemia) — STAT ophthalmology, admission required
⚠ Alkali burns require 30 minutes to 2+ hours of irrigation; acid burns 15-30 minutes
⚠ Limbal ischemia is the KEY prognostic factor — document clock hours involved
⚠ Exception: suspected globe rupture — do NOT irrigate; shield the eye and consult ophthalmology immediately

Recognition

True ocular emergency — IRRIGATE BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE. Alkali causes lipophilic penetration and liquefactive necrosis (worse prognosis); acid causes coagulative necrosis with a protein barrier that limits penetration. Alkali can reach the anterior chamber in under 15 seconds — time is cornea.

Alkali (More Severe)Acid
Ammonia (cleaners)Sulfuric acid (batteries)
Sodium hydroxide (drain cleaner)Hydrochloric acid (pool)
Calcium hydroxide (cement/lime)Hydrofluoric acid*
Potassium hydroxide (fertilizer)Acetic acid
Hydrofluoric acid behaves like alkali
Deep tissue penetration despite being an acid — treat as alkali. Cement/lime particles continue releasing alkali and must be swept from the fornices.
  1. Do NOT delay for history, visual acuity, slit lamp exam, pH testing, or ophthalmology consult
  2. At scene: irrigate with any non-caustic fluid (tap water, saline, fountain water); continue during transport
  3. In ED: topical anesthetic (proparacaine)
  4. Sweep fornices for particulate matter
  5. Begin continuous irrigation
  6. Use a Morgan lens if available
Exception: suspected globe rupture
Do NOT irrigate. Shield the eye and consult ophthalmology immediately.

Identify the agent type if possible — alkali, acid, or hydrofluoric acid (which behaves like alkali). If unknown, treat as alkali — the worst-case, deepest-penetrating assumption.

Irrigation Protocol

  1. Diphoterine (amphoteric, if available)
  2. Balanced salt solution (BSS)
  3. Lactated Ringer's (LR) — preferred over NS
  4. Normal saline (NS) — pH can be 4.5-7.0
  5. Sterile water
  6. Tap water (acceptable at scene)
Avoid hypotonic solutions
Hypotonic solutions increase chemical penetration into the tissue.

  2. Fill lens with irrigation fluid
  3. Have patient look down; insert lens under upper lid
  4. Have patient look up; tuck lens under lower lid
  5. Connect to IV tubing with fluid running
  6. Flow wide open initially — 250-500 mL over 15-20 min, then adjust to tolerance
Morgan lens contraindications
Suspected globe rupture, severe corneal laceration, or an uncooperative patient (relative). Alternative: nasal cannula over the bridge of the nose with tubing directed into each eye. LR preferred over NS — low-pH NS causes discomfort.
  1. Apply topical anesthetic
  2. Double-evert the upper lid
  3. Use a moist cotton swab to sweep the upper fornix systematically
  4. Repeat for the lower fornix
  5. Check lid margins and puncta
  6. Recheck fornices q15-30 min — particles migrate from upper to lower fornix with irrigation
Critical for cement/lime/plaster exposures
Particulate alkali continues releasing hydroxide until removed — look for white calcium hydroxide particles, cement fragments, plaster debris. Remove ALL visible particles. If cement is adherent to the cornea, do NOT force removal — ophthalmology will handle it.
  1. Check pH of BOTH eyes before irrigation (baseline)
  2. After irrigation, STOP flow
  3. Wait 5-10 minutes (reservoir effect)
  4. Touch narrow-range (5.0-8.0) litmus paper to the inferior fornix — NOT the cornea
  5. Read pH; stable pH = same reading on recheck 15-30 minutes later
Reservoir effect
If pH rises after stopping irrigation, chemical is still present in the tissue — resume irrigation.
  1. Resume irrigation for an additional 15-30 minutes
  2. Re-sweep fornices for retained particles
  3. Ensure adequate volume and flow rate
  4. Recheck pH after a 5-10 minute pause
Refractory pH — consider escalation
May indicate deep tissue penetration or retained particulate. Escalate to ophthalmology if pH remains abnormal after 2L+ irrigation, particles cannot be visualized/removed, or corneal opacification is visible. Continuous irrigation for 2+ hours may be needed for severe alkali burns.

Classification

  • Visual acuity in each eye (pinhole if reduced)
  • External: lid burns, periorbital skin, forniceal shortening (early symblepharon)
  • Cornea: fluorescein epithelial defect, stromal haze, clarity
  • Limbus: perilimbal ischemia (KEY prognostic factor) and clock hours involved
  • Conjunctiva: chemosis, injection, ischemia/necrosis, percentage involvement
  • Anterior chamber: cell/flare, hyphema
  • IOP: applanation or Tono-Pen (may be elevated acutely)
  • Lens: clarity (cataract from chemical penetration)

Management Algorithm

Chemical Eye Burn — Alkali reaches the anterior chamber in <15 seconds — irrigate before anything else.

Begin irrigation immediately — Do not delay for history, VA, pH, or slit lamp. Exception: suspected globe rupture — shield, do not irrigate.

Agent type? — Alkali, acid, or hydrofluoric acid (behaves like alkali). If unknown, treat as alkali.

Irrigation protocol (fluid, volume, duration) — LR preferred over NS. Acid 15-30 min; alkali 30 min to 2+ hours.

Morgan lens (hands-free irrigation) — Contraindicated if suspected globe rupture or severe corneal laceration.

Sweep fornices for particulate matter — Critical for cement/lime — particulate alkali keeps releasing hydroxide until removed.

Check pH (wait 5-10 min after stopping) — Target 7.0-7.4. Reservoir effect — chemical still present if pH rises after stopping.

pH normalized and stable? — Must be stable on two readings 30 minutes apart before proceeding to exam.

Continue irrigation, re-sweep, recheck pH — May need 2+ hours for severe alkali. Escalate to ophthalmology if refractory.

Post-irrigation exam — VA, fluorescein, limbal ischemia clock hours, anterior chamber, IOP, lens clarity.

Roper-Hall classification — Limbal ischemia is the key prognostic factor.

Dua classification (alternative) — More granular 6-grade alternative to Roper-Hall — either is acceptable.

Injury grade? — Grade III-IV = opaque cornea / >50% limbal ischemia — treat as one severe tier.

Grade I treatment — Cycloplegic, antibiotic ointment, prednisolone QID × 7 days.

Grade II treatment — Aggressive steroid taper, ascorbate/citrate hourly, doxycycline.

Grade III-IV treatment — Prednisolone Q1H around the clock — MUST taper by day 10-14. Consider AMT/Prokera.

Ophthalmology consultation — STAT for Grade III-IV, all alkali, limbal ischemia, vision loss, IOP elevation, or bilateral involvement.

Steroid taper timing — Beneficial days 0-10; rapid taper days 10-14; avoid after day 14 — perforation risk.

Disposition? — Grade III-IV admit; Grade II admit vs close outpatient; Grade I discharge.
