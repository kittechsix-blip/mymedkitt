Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO)

Adults ≥18, ED/inpatient diagnostic-differential framework for Classic, Nosocomial, Neutropenic, and HIV-associated FUO. Excludes pediatric FUO and pregnancy-specific FUO workups (not addressed in the source consult).

⚠ Malaria is a medical emergency in any traveler to an endemic area within the past year — P. falciparum can progress to death within 24-36 hours in a malaria-naive patient. Chemoprophylaxis does NOT exclude malaria. Obtain thick and thin blood smears STAT; treat empirically if a smear cannot be obtained within 2 hours, or if there is high clinical suspicion with hemodynamic instability or severe disease features.
⚠ Neutropenic fever (ANC <500) is a medical emergency — ALL patients require hospital admission and empiric anti-pseudomonal antibiotics (cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, or meropenem) started immediately; do not observe pending workup.
⚠ Giant cell arteritis with visual symptoms: start corticosteroids immediately — do not delay treatment for temporal artery biopsy confirmation; vision loss can be permanent.
⚠ Do NOT give empiric corticosteroids for undiagnosed FUO until infection has been excluded — steroids can unmask or worsen an occult infection.
⚠ Travel history must be obtained in every FUO evaluation — malaria must be ruled out in any traveler to a malaria-endemic area within the past year, regardless of chemoprophylaxis use or timing.

1 · Definition & category

Classic FUO (Petersdorf 1961, modified Durack 1991): Temperature ≥38.3°C (101°F) on multiple occasions, duration ≥3 weeks, diagnosis uncertain after initial evaluation.[1][2]

Four categories, each with its own definition and differential:
• Classic — immunocompetent, outpatient or early admission
• Nosocomial — hospitalized ≥24h before fever onset, fever ≥3 days
• Neutropenic — ANC <500, fever ≥3 days despite antibiotics
• HIV-associated — confirmed HIV, fever ≥4 weeks

Nosocomial FUO: fever developing ≥3 days after hospital admission in a patient admitted without fever.

Neutropenic FUO: ANC <500 (or expected to fall below 500), fever ≥38.3°C single or ≥38°C sustained, fever ≥3 days despite appropriate antibiotics.

HIV-associated FUO: confirmed HIV, fever ≥38.3°C, duration ≥4 weeks outpatient or ≥3 days inpatient.

▶ What to do now

Neutropenic fever — admit + empiric antibiotics NOW
Admit. Start empiric anti-pseudomonal therapy immediately: Cefepime, Piperacillin-tazobactam, or Meropenem. Add vancomycin if MRSA risk, severe mucositis, catheter infection, or skin/soft-tissue infection. Add an antifungal if fever persists 4-7 days on antibiotics.
Malaria-endemic travel — rule out malaria NOW
Thick and thin blood smears STAT (gold standard); RDT if smear delayed; repeat q12-24h ×3 if initial negative and suspicion remains high. Treat empirically if unable to obtain a smear within 2 hours, or if high suspicion + hemodynamic instability or severe features. Chemoprophylaxis does NOT exclude malaria.
Nosocomial FUO — review every line, catheter, and drug
Culture all lines (line AND peripheral), obtain C. diff toxin, CXR, UA + culture, and lower-extremity Doppler if DVT suspected. Remove or replace unnecessary lines/catheters and discontinue unnecessary medications; treat with culture-directed antibiotics.
HIV-associated FUO — CD4 drives the differential
Check CD4 count and HIV viral load. Admit and obtain mandatory ID consultation if CD4 <200 or severely ill. Below CD4 <200 add PCP; below CD4 <50 add MAC and CMV disease. Optimize/initiate ART with ID guidance to avoid IRIS.
Other endemic travel — incubation period guides testing
CBC, CMP/LFTs, blood cultures ×2, thick/thin smears, UA; then directed serologies by incubation period (short <2wk: dengue/enteric; medium 2-6wk: typhoid/malaria/hepatitis; long >6wk: TB/leishmania/abscess/brucellosis).
Classic FUO, no travel — start the standard workup
Comprehensive history (all medications/OTCs/supplements, exposures, sexual/HIV risk, family history, devices) plus initial labs: CBC, CMP/LFTs, ESR/CRP, blood cultures ×2-3, UA, LDH, ferritin, HIV serology, ANA/RF; initial imaging CXR + abdominal US/CT.

5 · Advanced diagnostics

When initial workup is non-diagnostic: FDG-PET/CT has a 50-70% diagnostic yield in FUO — best for occult infection, malignancy, vasculitis; consider early if initial workup negative.[10] Also: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast, MRI spine/pelvis, echocardiogram (TTE→TEE if negative). Tissue diagnosis: bone marrow biopsy, temporal artery biopsy (age >50, GCA suspected), lymph node biopsy, liver biopsy. Serologies/cultures: TB (quantiferon, sputum AFB), fungal (histo/blasto/cocci antigens+serologies), Q fever/Bartonella/Brucella serologies, CMV/EBV serologies.

6 · Specific etiologies & disposition

Across large FUO cohorts, the etiology breaks down roughly as infection (~30%), malignancy (~20%), autoimmune (~20%), undiagnosed (~30%).[1]

Infectious etiology
Bacterial: tuberculosis (most common infectious cause worldwide), endocarditis (esp. culture-negative), intra-abdominal abscess, osteomyelitis, prostatitis (elderly men), dental abscess. Viral: EBV/CMV, HIV. Fungal: histoplasmosis/coccidioidomycosis, disseminated candidiasis. Parasitic: malaria, visceral leishmaniasis, toxoplasmosis. Management: targeted antimicrobial therapy, ID consultation for complex/unusual infections, source control (abscess drainage, valve surgery).
Malignancy-related FUO
Hematologic (most common): lymphoma, leukemia (AML/CLL), Castleman disease, MDS. Solid tumors: renal cell carcinoma (classic FUO cause), hepatocellular carcinoma, ovarian cancer, atrial myxoma, colon cancer with liver metastases. Mechanism: tumor necrosis, cytokine production, superimposed infection, paraneoplastic syndrome. Management: oncology referral for staging/treatment; NSAIDs or acetaminophen for symptomatic control; naproxen test (fever responds more to naproxen than acetaminophen in neoplastic fever — not definitive).
Autoimmune / inflammatory FUO
Vasculitis: Giant Cell Arteritis — age >50, headache, jaw claudication, visual changes; ESR often >100, temporal artery biopsy diagnostic; start steroids immediately if vision threatened. Also polyarteritis nodosa, ANCA-associated vasculitis. Connective tissue: adult Still disease (quotidian fever, salmon rash, arthritis, ferritin >10,000), SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory myopathy. Granulomatous: sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Autoinflammatory: familial Mediterranean fever, TRAPS, Schnitzler syndrome. Management: rheumatology consultation; corticosteroids often first-line; start immediately for GCA with visual symptoms.
Undiagnosed FUO (~30%)
Generally favorable prognosis if stable; many resolve spontaneously over weeks to months; ~50% eventually diagnosed on long-term follow-up. Management: watchful waiting (preferred if stable) with close outpatient follow-up and serial inflammatory markers; empiric NSAID trial for possible autoimmune/inflammatory cause; corticosteroids only after infection excluded, and only with high suspicion for GCA or Still disease; antibiotics generally NOT recommended empirically unless clinical deterioration; repeat FDG-PET/CT in 4-6 weeks if initial negative. Red flags that escalate workup: clinical deterioration, weight loss >10%, new localizing signs, severe cytopenias.

7 · Special populations

Common causes: line-related infection (central lines, peripheral IVs), surgical site infection, C. difficile colitis, drug fever (antibiotics very common), UTI (especially with Foley), pneumonia (HAP/VAP), DVT/PE, sinusitis (NG tube/intubation), decubitus ulcer infection.

Workup: review all lines/catheters/surgical sites; blood cultures from line AND peripheral; C. diff toxin assay; CXR; UA + culture; lower-extremity Doppler if DVT suspected; CT sinuses if intubated/NG tube.

Management: remove or replace lines/catheters if possible; targeted antibiotics based on cultures; stop unnecessary medications.

High-risk features: profound neutropenia (ANC <100), prolonged neutropenia (>7 days), hemodynamic instability, mucositis, pneumonia, new abdominal pain.

Common pathogens: gram-positive (staph, strep, enterococcus), gram-negative (pseudomonas, E. coli, klebsiella), fungal (candida, aspergillus — prolonged neutropenia).

Escalation: add vancomycin if clinical deterioration, MRSA risk, or catheter concern; add antifungal (echinocandin or amphotericin) if fever persists 4-7 days despite antibiotics; consider CT chest for pulmonary aspergillosis; consider G-CSF for prolonged neutropenia.

Duration: continue antibiotics until ANC >500 AND afebrile ≥48h; if source identified, treat for the appropriate duration.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls6 items
Do NOT rely on chemoprophylaxis to exclude malaria — breakthrough infections occur; obtain thick/thin smears in any traveler to an endemic area regardless of prophylaxis history.
Do NOT give empiric corticosteroids for undiagnosed FUO until infection has been excluded — steroids can unmask or worsen an occult infection.
Do NOT delay steroids for suspected GCA with visual symptoms waiting on temporal artery biopsy confirmation — vision loss can be permanent.
Do NOT re-challenge a patient with a suspected culprit drug to confirm drug fever unless absolutely necessary — the diagnosis is one of exclusion plus defervescence after discontinuation.
Do NOT observe neutropenic fever (ANC <500) pending workup — admit and start empiric anti-pseudomonal antibiotics immediately; this is a medical emergency.
Do NOT skip excisional lymph node biopsy when accessible adenopathy is present — tissue is the issue; FNA alone is inferior for lymphoma/granulomatous disease.

Travel-associated workup (by incubation period) — Incubation-guided differential: short <2wk (dengue, chikungunya, zika, travelers diarrhea/enteric pathogens, influenza/COVID, rickettsial); medium 2-6wk (typhoid/paratyphoid, malaria esp. vivax/ovale, hepatitis A/E, acute HIV seroconversion, leptospirosis); long >6wk (TB, visceral leishmaniasis, amoebic liver abscess, brucellosis). Labs: CBC+diff, CMP/LFTs, blood cx ×2, thick/thin smears, UA+culture. Directed testing: dengue NS1/serology, typhoid blood culture, hepatitis panel, rickettsial serology, leptospirosis IgM/PCR, brucellosis blood culture+serology. Imaging: CXR, abdominal US/CT. Consult ID/travel medicine if severe, hemorrhagic symptoms, diagnostic uncertainty, or exotic pathogen suspected.

Travel workup diagnostic? — If a specific pathogen or source is identified, treat accordingly; if negative and fever persists, proceed to standard non-travel FUO workup.

Travel-associated diagnosis established → targeted treatment — Malaria: artemether-lumefantrine. Dengue: supportive care, monitor warning signs. Typhoid: fluoroquinolone, azithromycin, or ceftriaxone. Rickettsial: doxycycline 100 mg PO BID. Leptospirosis: doxycycline or ceftriaxone. Brucellosis: doxycycline + gentamicin or rifampin. Admit if unstable/severe/needs IV therapy; outpatient if mild with reliable follow-up; ID consult for complex/exotic pathogens.

Standard initial FUO workup (history + labs + imaging) — Comprehensive history: duration/pattern of fever, ALL medications (incl. OTC/supplements/herbals), occupational/animal exposures, sexual history/HIV risk, family history (e.g. familial Mediterranean fever), prior surgeries/implants/devices. Initial labs: CBC+diff, CMP/LFTs, ESR/CRP, blood cultures ×2-3, UA+culture, LDH, ferritin, HIV serology, ANA/RF if autoimmune suspected. Initial imaging: CXR, abdominal US/CT.

Initial workup result? — Diagnosis found → treat; abnormal clues but no diagnosis → clue-directed workup; no localizing signs and workup negative → advanced diagnostics.

Which clue is present? — Organ-system clues (elevated ESR/CRP, lymphadenopathy/splenomegaly, hepatic abnormalities, cardiac murmur/embolic phenomena, rash/skin findings) all funnel to directed organ-system workup; a suspicious medication history routes to drug-fever evaluation.

Organ-system-directed workup (ESR/CRP, adenopathy, liver, endocarditis, rash) — ESR >100: GCA (age >50), malignancy (lymphoma/RCC), endocarditis, osteomyelitis, TB, adult Still (ferritin >10,000/HLH) — temporal artery biopsy if >50yo, ask about PMR symptoms (proximal muscle pain/stiffness) if >50yo, echo, CT C/A/P, bone scan/MRI, quantiferon/PPD. Adenopathy/splenomegaly: lymphoma, TB, Castleman, Kikuchi, sarcoid, mono (EBV/CMV), HIV, SLE — excisional lymph node biopsy is the key diagnostic step. Hepatic abnormalities: pyogenic/amoebic abscess, granulomatous hepatitis, HCC, DILI, viral hepatitis, Q fever, brucellosis — CT/MRI + hepatitis panel + serologies, biopsy if lesion accessible; pyogenic abscess = drain + antibiotics, amoebic = metronidazole ± aspiration. Murmur/embolic phenomena: infective endocarditis (modified/2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria) — blood cultures ×3 separate sites, TTE first then TEE if negative + high suspicion or prosthetic valve, ESR/CRP/RF, urinalysis (microscopic hematuria); culture-negative = HACEK/Bartonella/Coxiella/Brucella/fungal. Rash: maculopapular = drug/viral/Still; petechial/purpuric = endocarditis/meningococcemia/RMSF/vasculitis; nodular = sarcoid/TB/IBD/fungal — skin biopsy is often diagnostic.

Drug fever evaluation — Accounts for 3-7% of FUO. Culprits: beta-lactams (most common), sulfonamides, vancomycin, nitrofurantoin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, procainamide, quinidine, hydralazine, allopurinol, heparin, interferon, biologics. Characteristics: onset days-weeks after starting drug, patient appears paradoxically well, relative bradycardia common, eosinophilia ~20%, rash ~20%. Diagnosis of exclusion; fever typically resolves 48-72h after discontinuation (up to 5-7 days for some drugs).

Suspected drug that can be safely discontinued? — If yes, stop and observe 48-72h; if no clear culprit or cannot stop, continue FUO workup.

Discontinue drug, observe 48-72h — Fever typically resolves within 48-72h (up to 5-7 days for some drugs). If fever persists >72h, unlikely drug fever — resume workup. If resolves, document drug allergy/intolerance; do not re-challenge unless absolutely necessary.

Advanced diagnostics — FDG-PET/CT: 50-70% diagnostic yield, best for occult infection/malignancy/vasculitis. CT C/A/P with contrast (if not done), MRI spine/pelvis (osteomyelitis/abscess), echocardiogram (TTE→TEE if negative). Tissue diagnosis: bone marrow biopsy, temporal artery biopsy (if >50yo, GCA suspected), lymph node biopsy, liver biopsy. Serologies/cultures: TB quantiferon/sputum AFB, fungal antigens/serologies (histo/blasto/cocci), Q fever/Bartonella/Brucella serologies, CMV/EBV serologies.

Category of disease identified (or most likely)? — Infection, malignancy, autoimmune/inflammatory, or still undiagnosed — each routes to its own definitive management pathway below.

Targeted treatment by etiology + subspecialty referral — Infection (~30%): targeted antimicrobials + ID consult + source control (TB most common worldwide, endocarditis, intra-abdominal abscess, osteomyelitis, prostatitis, EBV/CMV, HIV, fungal, malaria, leishmaniasis, toxoplasmosis). Malignancy (~20%): oncology referral + staging; heme (lymphoma/leukemia/Castleman/MDS) most common, solid tumors (RCC classic, HCC, ovarian, atrial myxoma, colon+liver mets); NSAIDs/acetaminophen for symptomatic fever control. Autoimmune/inflammatory (~20%): rheumatology consult; GCA (start steroids immediately if vision threatened), adult Still (ferritin >10,000), SLE, RA, vasculitis, sarcoidosis, autoinflammatory syndromes — steroids often first-line. Still undiagnosed (~30%): watchful waiting if stable (favorable prognosis, ~50% eventually diagnosed on follow-up); empiric NSAID trial; corticosteroids ONLY after infection excluded; antibiotics generally not recommended empirically; repeat FDG-PET/CT in 4-6 weeks; red flags (deterioration, weight loss >10%, new localizing signs, severe cytopenias) escalate workup. If diagnosis was established at the initial (non-clue, non-advanced) workup, ensure clinical response, repeat labs, and monitor for treatment complications.

3 · Travel-associated workup

P. falciparum can progress to death within 24-36 hours in a malaria-naive patient. Immediate workup: thick and thin blood smears (gold standard), rapid diagnostic test if smear delayed, repeat smears q12-24h ×3 if initial negative and suspicion high. Empiric treatment if unable to obtain a smear within 2 hours, or high clinical suspicion with hemodynamic instability or severe disease features present. Chemoprophylaxis does NOT exclude malaria — breakthrough infections occur.

Medium (2-6 weeks) | Typhoid/paratyphoid fever; malaria (esp. P. vivax, P. ovale); hepatitis A, E; acute HIV seroconversion; leptospirosis

Long (>6 weeks) | Tuberculosis; visceral leishmaniasis; amoebic liver abscess; brucellosis

  • CMP, LFTs
  • Blood cultures ×2
  • Thick and thin blood smears for malaria
  • Urinalysis + urine culture
  • Directed serologies by suspected pathogen (dengue NS1/IgM-IgG, typhoid blood culture, hepatitis panel, rickettsial serology, leptospirosis IgM/PCR, brucellosis blood culture + serology)
  • CXR; abdominal US or CT

Treatment by pathogen: Malaria → artemether-lumefantrine. Dengue → supportive care, monitor warning signs. Typhoid → fluoroquinolone, azithromycin, or ceftriaxone. Rickettsial → doxycycline (see dose below). Leptospirosis → doxycycline or ceftriaxone. Brucellosis → doxycycline + gentamicin or rifampin.

Disposition: admit if hemodynamically unstable, severe disease, or requires IV therapy; outpatient if mild with reliable follow-up; ID consult for complex or exotic pathogens.

5 · Advanced diagnostics

When initial workup is non-diagnostic: FDG-PET/CT has a 50-70% diagnostic yield in FUO — best for occult infection, malignancy, vasculitis; consider early if initial workup negative.[10] Also: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast, MRI spine/pelvis, echocardiogram (TTE→TEE if negative). Tissue diagnosis: bone marrow biopsy, temporal artery biopsy (age >50, GCA suspected), lymph node biopsy, liver biopsy. Serologies/cultures: TB (quantiferon, sputum AFB), fungal (histo/blasto/cocci antigens+serologies), Q fever/Bartonella/Brucella serologies, CMV/EBV serologies.

6 · Specific etiologies & disposition

Across large FUO cohorts, the etiology breaks down roughly as infection (~30%), malignancy (~20%), autoimmune (~20%), undiagnosed (~30%).[1]

Bacterial: tuberculosis (most common infectious cause worldwide), endocarditis (esp. culture-negative), intra-abdominal abscess, osteomyelitis, prostatitis (elderly men), dental abscess. Viral: EBV/CMV, HIV. Fungal: histoplasmosis/coccidioidomycosis, disseminated candidiasis. Parasitic: malaria, visceral leishmaniasis, toxoplasmosis. Management: targeted antimicrobial therapy, ID consultation for complex/unusual infections, source control (abscess drainage, valve surgery).

Hematologic (most common): lymphoma, leukemia (AML/CLL), Castleman disease, MDS. Solid tumors: renal cell carcinoma (classic FUO cause), hepatocellular carcinoma, ovarian cancer, atrial myxoma, colon cancer with liver metastases. Mechanism: tumor necrosis, cytokine production, superimposed infection, paraneoplastic syndrome. Management: oncology referral for staging/treatment; NSAIDs or acetaminophen for symptomatic control; naproxen test (fever responds more to naproxen than acetaminophen in neoplastic fever — not definitive).

Vasculitis: Giant Cell Arteritis — age >50, headache, jaw claudication, visual changes; ESR often >100, temporal artery biopsy diagnostic; start steroids immediately if vision threatened. Also polyarteritis nodosa, ANCA-associated vasculitis. Connective tissue: adult Still disease (quotidian fever, salmon rash, arthritis, ferritin >10,000), SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory myopathy. Granulomatous: sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Autoinflammatory: familial Mediterranean fever, TRAPS, Schnitzler syndrome. Management: rheumatology consultation; corticosteroids often first-line; start immediately for GCA with visual symptoms.

Generally favorable prognosis if stable; many resolve spontaneously over weeks to months; ~50% eventually diagnosed on long-term follow-up. Management: watchful waiting (preferred if stable) with close outpatient follow-up and serial inflammatory markers; empiric NSAID trial for possible autoimmune/inflammatory cause; corticosteroids only after infection excluded, and only with high suspicion for GCA or Still disease; antibiotics generally NOT recommended empirically unless clinical deterioration; repeat FDG-PET/CT in 4-6 weeks if initial negative. Red flags that escalate workup: clinical deterioration, weight loss >10%, new localizing signs, severe cytopenias.

7 · Special populations

Common causes: line-related infection (central lines, peripheral IVs), surgical site infection, C. difficile colitis, drug fever (antibiotics very common), UTI (especially with Foley), pneumonia (HAP/VAP), DVT/PE, sinusitis (NG tube/intubation), decubitus ulcer infection.

Workup: review all lines/catheters/surgical sites; blood cultures from line AND peripheral; C. diff toxin assay; CXR; UA + culture; lower-extremity Doppler if DVT suspected; CT sinuses if intubated/NG tube.

Management: remove or replace lines/catheters if possible; targeted antibiotics based on cultures; stop unnecessary medications.

High-risk features: profound neutropenia (ANC <100), prolonged neutropenia (>7 days), hemodynamic instability, mucositis, pneumonia, new abdominal pain.

Common pathogens: gram-positive (staph, strep, enterococcus), gram-negative (pseudomonas, E. coli, klebsiella), fungal (candida, aspergillus — prolonged neutropenia).

Escalation: add vancomycin if clinical deterioration, MRSA risk, or catheter concern; add antifungal (echinocandin or amphotericin) if fever persists 4-7 days despite antibiotics; consider CT chest for pulmonary aspergillosis; consider G-CSF for prolonged neutropenia.

Duration: continue antibiotics until ANC >500 AND afebrile ≥48h; if source identified, treat for the appropriate duration.

CD4 count | Differential

CD4 >200 | Similar to immunocompetent hosts — bacterial infections, lymphoma

CD4 <200 | Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP); TB (any CD4); lymphoma; Kaposi sarcoma

CD4 <50 | Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC); disseminated histoplasmosis/coccidioidomycosis; CMV disease

Empiric therapies: PCP suspected → TMP-SMX. MAC suspected → azithromycin + ethambutol. TB suspected → RIPE therapy. Fungal suspected → amphotericin B or itraconazole. Always: optimize or initiate ART with ID guidance (avoid IRIS); prophylaxis for opportunistic infections based on CD4; close outpatient follow-up if discharged.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT rely on chemoprophylaxis to exclude malaria — breakthrough infections occur; obtain thick/thin smears in any traveler to an endemic area regardless of prophylaxis history.

Do NOT give empiric corticosteroids for undiagnosed FUO until infection has been excluded — steroids can unmask or worsen an occult infection.

Do NOT delay steroids for suspected GCA with visual symptoms waiting on temporal artery biopsy confirmation — vision loss can be permanent.

Do NOT re-challenge a patient with a suspected culprit drug to confirm drug fever unless absolutely necessary — the diagnosis is one of exclusion plus defervescence after discontinuation.

Do NOT observe neutropenic fever (ANC <500) pending workup — admit and start empiric anti-pseudomonal antibiotics immediately; this is a medical emergency.

Do NOT skip excisional lymph node biopsy when accessible adenopathy is present — tissue is the issue; FNA alone is inferior for lymphoma/granulomatous disease.
