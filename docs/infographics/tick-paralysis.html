Tick Paralysis: Find It, Pull It, Watch the Airway

All ages presenting with acute ascending flaccid paralysis in the ED — classic population is a young child (often a girl with long thick hair) with an occult scalp tick. Diagnosis is clinical; there is no confirmatory lab test. Excludes patients with an already-confirmed alternative diagnosis (Guillain-Barré, botulism, myasthenia gravis, transverse myelitis) or hypokalemic periodic paralysis on labs.

⚠ Full head-to-toe skin search is the diagnostic test. There is no confirmatory lab — search scalp/hairline, behind the ears and ear canal, axillae, groin/perineum/gluteal cleft, umbilicus, interdigital spaces, and the neck/spine. If one tick is found, keep looking — multiple ticks can be present.
⚠ Remove properly: fine forceps at the skin, straight-up steady pull. Do NOT twist, crush, or squeeze the tick body, and do NOT use folklore methods (petroleum jelly, nail polish, matches, heat) — these can trigger more toxin release.
⚠ Respiratory failure is the cause of death. Serial bulbar and ventilatory monitoring is the priority while searching for the tick. Pulse oximetry is a late, poor indicator — a normal SpO2 should NOT be reassuring.
⚠ Do NOT give IVIG or plasmapheresis — they do not work in tick paralysis, unlike GBS. Removing the tick is the treatment.
⚠ Australian Ixodes holocyclus tick paralysis can WORSEN after removal, peaking in the first 12-48 h — admit and observe; involve toxicology regarding tick antitoxin (TAS), which carries non-standardized dosing and allergy/serum-sickness risk.

1 · Recognize the GBS mimic

A toxin, not an infection
Tick paralysis is acute ascending flaccid paralysis caused by a neurotoxin in the saliva of an engorged feeding female tick — there is no pathogen (distinct from Lyme/RMSF). The toxin is only secreted late in feeding, so symptoms appear after 4-7 days of attachment. The patient rarely reports a known bite.

Classic patient: a young child, often a girl with long thick hair hiding a scalp tick. Pacific Northwest, Rocky Mountains, Southeast US (Dermacentor); eastern Australia (Ixodes holocyclus — far more severe).

The syndrome: prodrome of anorexia, lethargy, irritability; ataxia frequently precedes weakness (evaluate for tick paralysis in any child with acute ataxia); acute symmetric ascending flaccid paralysis (legs → trunk → arms) over 24-48 h; areflexia/hyporeflexia; bulbar and respiratory involvement late.

Sparing features that point AWAY from a cord/brain lesion: sensation SPARED, mental status NORMAL, afebrile.
