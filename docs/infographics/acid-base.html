Acid-Base: A Stepwise Bedside Read

Adults ≥18 in the ED/ICU interpreting an ABG/VBG + BMP. A diagnostic algorithm, not a substitute for clinical judgment. Excludes neonates and isolated toxicologic ingestions managed by protocol.

⚠ Stepwise approach: pH → PCO2 compensation → Anion gap → Delta-delta → Osmolar gap
⚠ Anion gap = Na - (Cl + HCO3), normal 8-12. Elevated AG >12 = GOLDMARK mnemonic
⚠ Delta-delta ratio = ΔAG / ΔHCO3. Ratio <1 = non-AG acidosis, 1-2 = pure AG acidosis, >2 = concurrent metabolic alkalosis
⚠ Winter formula for respiratory compensation: Expected PCO2 = 1.5(HCO3) + 8 ± 2
⚠ Osmolar gap >10 suggests toxic alcohol (methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol)
⚠ Urine anion gap for non-AG metabolic acidosis: UAG = (Na + K) - Cl. Positive = RTA, Negative = GI losses

1 · Assess & choose your approach

Order the full panel first: ABG or VBG, BMP (Na, Cl, HCO₃, BUN, Cr, glucose), lactate, albumin, β-hydroxybutyrate, serum osmolality. For most ED work-ups a VBG is sufficient — venous pH runs about 0.04 lower than arterial.[14]

  1. Interpret the pH: acidemia (<7.35), alkalemia (>7.45), or normal.
  2. Check the pCO₂ compensation with the six rules.
  3. Compute the anion gap and correct it for albumin.
  4. Run the delta-delta ratio to unmask a second metabolic process.
  5. Add the osmolar gap when unmeasured anions are present.
A normal pH does NOT exclude a disorder
Opposing processes cancel: AG acidosis + metabolic alkalosis, or respiratory acidosis + metabolic alkalosis, both land near 7.40. Always check the anion gap, base excess, and lactate even when the pH is normal — an elevated lactate with a normal pH is ominous.[4]

—Anion Gap = Na − (Cl + HCO₃)

Normal AG: 8–12 mEq/L (as low as 6 ± 3 on modern analyzers).[7]

—Albumin-corrected AG

Corrected AG = AG + 2.5 × (4.2 − measured albumin in g/dL). Low albumin falsely narrows the AG and can mask an anion-gap acidosis in the critically ill.[8]

—Delta ratio (ΔAG / ΔHCO₃)

Delta Ratio = ΔAG / ΔHCO₃ = (AG − 12) / (24 − HCO₃). In a pure AG acidosis this equals ~1; deviation reveals a second process.[7]

Delta ratio < 1 — concurrent non-AG acidosis
HCO₃ has fallen MORE than the gap rose. Check the urine anion gap (UNa + UK − UCl): negative = GI loss, positive = RTA.
Delta ratio 1–2 — pure AG acidosis
The gap and bicarbonate moved together. Apply Winter's formula next to confirm respiratory compensation.
Delta ratio > 2 — concurrent metabolic alkalosis
The AG rose MORE than bicarbonate fell — a baseline elevated HCO₃ is offsetting the acidosis (e.g., AG acidosis plus vomiting).
—Winter's expected pCO₂ (± 2)

Expected pCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃ + 8 ± 2. Measured pCO₂ above this range = concurrent respiratory acidosis; below it = respiratory alkalosis (classic salicylate pattern).[6][2]

—Osmolar gap

Osmolar gap = Measured Osm − Calculated Osm, where calculated Osm = 2 × Na + Glucose/18 + BUN/2.8. A gap >10 suggests methanol, ethylene glycol, or isopropanol; >50 is almost certainly a toxic alcohol.

4 · Metabolic acidosis workup

High anion gap (HAGMA) differentialGOLDMARK

Glycols (ethylene, propylene) · Oxoproline · L-lactate · D-lactate · Methanol · Aspirin · Renal failure (uremia) · Ketoacidosis (DKA, AKA, starvation). Toxic alcohols and salicylates raise both the gap and the osmolar gap.[15]

Lactate > 4 mmol/L is critical
Think sepsis, shock, and mesenteric ischemia. Failure to clear with fluids, plus hemodynamic instability, drives ICU admission.[5]
Non-anion gap (NAGMA) — urine anion gapRTA vs GI

Urine anion gap = UNa + UK − UCl. Negative (<0) → GI bicarbonate loss (diarrhea). Positive (>0) → renal tubular acidosis; differentiate by urine pH.

Large-volume normal saline (SID 0) itself causes a hyperchloremic acidosis; balanced crystalloids reduce this.[13]

5 · Stewart approach (SID / SIG)

—Strong Ion Difference (Na − Cl)

SID = Na − Cl (simplified), normal ~35 mEq/L. The Na-Cl difference is the predominant determinant of metabolic acid-base status: a reduced SID drives acidosis (strong anions squeeze out bicarbonate), an increased SID drives alkalosis.[11]

Strong Ion Gap (unmeasured anions)SIG > 2

SIG > 2 flags unmeasured anions beyond what Na-Cl, lactate, and albumin explain — uremia, DKA/AKA ketones, toxic alcohols, salicylates, D-lactate. A negative SIG points to unmeasured cations (hypercalcemia, hypermagnesemia, paraproteins/myeloma) or lab artifact.[10]

6 · Treatment & disposition

Treat the cause, not the number
Correct the underlying process. Sodium Bicarbonate is reserved for specific indications — it is NOT routine in DKA, lactic acidosis, or cardiac arrest.[3][16]

Bicarbonate indications: pH ≤ 7.20 with moderate-to-severe AKI (BICAR-ICU: NNT 6 for 28-day mortality)[12] · TCA overdose (sodium-channel blockade) · salicylate toxicity (urinary alkalinization) · life-threatening hyperkalemia. The pH < 6.9 threshold is ADA guidance specific to DKA.[1]

AKI toggled on → bicarbonate is on the table
With moderate-to-severe AKI and pH ≤ 7.20, isotonic bicarbonate reduced the need for dialysis and 28-day mortality in the AKIN 2–3 subgroup (BICAR-ICU).[12]
