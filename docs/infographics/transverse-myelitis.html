Acute Transverse Myelitis: Exclude Compression First

Adults and children in the ED with suspected acute myelopathy (bilateral weakness + sensory level ± sphincter dysfunction). Excludes chronic/slowly progressive myelopathy (>21 days to nadir) and hyperacute (seconds-minutes) cord syndromes, which point away from transverse myelitis toward degenerative or vascular causes respectively.

⚠ ROOT GATE: order emergent MRI of the spinal cord WITH and WITHOUT gadolinium — image the clinically suspected level PLUS the whole cord — BEFORE the LP. Do not delay imaging for the LP.
⚠ Establish a sensory level and always check a post-void residual — red flags for compression (fever/IVDU, anticoagulation, known cancer, saddle anesthesia) demand emergent MRI now.
⚠ Compressive myelopathy (epidural abscess, hematoma, tumor/metastasis, disc) is a neurosurgical emergency, NOT transverse myelitis — do not give high-dose steroids for presumed inflammation until compression and infection are addressed.
⚠ If epidural abscess or active CNS infection (e.g., HSV myelitis) is plausible, treat/cover the infection and discuss steroid timing with neurology/ID first — immunosuppressing an unrecognized infection can be harmful.
⚠ Do NOT high-dose steroid a hyperacute spinal cord infarct — tempo (nadir <12 h, no early enhancement) separates it from transverse myelitis.

1 · Recognize the cord syndrome

The triad that says "this is the cord"
Transverse myelitis (TM) is acute inflammation of the spinal cord producing motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction below the lesion. Bilateral motor weakness (para-/quadriparesis) + a sensory level (a horizontal line below which sensation changes) + sphincter/autonomic dysfunction (urinary retention is early and common).

Tempo: progresses over hours to 21 days, nadir typically 4 hours to a few days — faster than GBS climbs, but unlike stroke it is not maximal at onset.

The ED job is NOT to confirm inflammation
The ED job is to EXCLUDE COMPRESSION. A compressive myelopathy (epidural abscess, hematoma, tumor, disc) looks identical and is a neurosurgical emergency. Get the MRI first.
  • Sensory level — test pinprick/temperature ascending up the trunk until it normalizes; that dermatome localizes the lesion (thoracic most common).
  • Weakness — bilateral, often initially flaccid (spinal shock) then spastic with hyperreflexia + Babinski over days.
  • Bladder/bowel — urinary retention or incontinence; always check a post-void residual.
  • Back pain at the level in about one third.
Red flags for structural compression — MRI now
Severe focal back pain + fever/immunosuppression/IVDU → epidural abscess. Anticoagulation or recent procedure → epidural hematoma. Known cancer + weight loss → metastatic compression. Saddle anesthesia + retention + areflexia → cauda equina. Every one of these is a neurosurgical clock — the next step is the same for all: image the cord.

2 · Root gate — emergent MRI to exclude compression

This is the decision the whole consult hinges on
Transverse myelitis is a diagnosis of exclusion — you cannot call cord dysfunction "inflammatory" until a surgically correctable compressive lesion has been ruled out. Order MRI of the spinal cord WITH and WITHOUT gadolinium.

Image the clinically suspected level plus the whole cord — multifocal disease and skip lesions are common, and a cervical lesion can present with a thoracic-feeling level. If MRI is unavailable, CT myelography is the fallback to exclude compression. Do NOT delay imaging for the LP. Hunting: epidural abscess, hematoma, tumor/metastasis, herniated disc, or severe stenosis.

3 · Inflammatory workup (after compression excluded)

  • CSF: cell count + differential (pleocytosis supports inflammation; >50 lymphocytes seen in MS/idiopathic, neutrophils suggest infection).
  • CSF: protein (often elevated); oligoclonal bands + IgG index (positive favors MS).
  • CSF: infectious studies as indicated — HSV/VZV PCR, enterovirus, VDRL.
  • Serum: AQP4-IgG (NMOSD; think longitudinally extensive lesion, ≥3 vertebral segments) and MOG-IgG (MOGAD).
  • Serum: B12, folate, copper, HIV, RPR, ANA/dsDNA, SS-A/SS-B, ESR/CRP.
  • MRI brain — screen for demyelinating lesions (MS conversion risk).

AQP4/MOG status changes acute therapy (lower threshold for PLEX) and the long-term immunosuppression plan.

7 · Disposition & prognosis

Admit every acute myelopathy
Neurology consult for all suspected TM; neurosurgery if any compressive component. ICU/step-down if: cervical lesion with respiratory compromise, rapid ascending progression, or autonomic instability. High cervical lesions (C3-5) can impair the diaphragm — monitor respiratory function.
  • Continue IV steroids; arrange PLEX if refractory.
  • Bladder scan + catheter for retention; bowel regimen.
  • DVT prophylaxis, pressure-injury bundle, early rehab consult.
  • Discharge planning: rehab placement, outpatient neurology for disease-modifying therapy, repeat MRI/antibody follow-up.
