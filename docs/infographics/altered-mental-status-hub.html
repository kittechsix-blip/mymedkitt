Altered Mental Status: Sick-Check First, Then Hunt the Reversible Killer

Adults with undifferentiated altered mental status in the ED. This is a navigational triage hub, not a definitive protocol — each lane opens its own myMedKitt consult. Excludes primary pediatric presentations and post-cardiac-arrest coma (see dedicated pathways). Never label a presentation psychiatric until delirium and medical mimics are excluded.

⚠ Fingerstick glucose immediately in every AMS patient. Treat hypoglycemia before anything else — do not wait for the lab glucose. Severe hypoglycemia can mimic stroke; treat the sugar before locking in the stroke lane.
⚠ Insulin before knowing potassium can kill. In DKA/HHS, check potassium before the insulin decision.
⚠ Naloxone is titrated to adequate respirations, not full wakefulness. A patient may breathe better after naloxone yet stay deeply sedated from xylazine/medetomidine, alcohol, benzodiazepines, or clonidine — support ventilation and prepare the airway.
⚠ Empiric CNS antibiotics/acyclovir must not wait for CT/LP when meningitis/encephalitis is plausible. Elderly septic patients may present as isolated AMS without fever.
⚠ "Dementia" does not explain an acute change. Acute change is delirium until proven otherwise. Persistent AMS stays medical until proven otherwise.
⚠ Severe symptomatic hyponatremia (seizure/coma physiology) needs hypertonic saline per the sodium protocol while the cause is worked up.

1 · Sick check first (first 60 seconds)

First 60 seconds — before any workup
Airway and breathing (GCS, gag, oxygenation, ventilation) · fingerstick glucose now · full vitals including core temperature · pupils, skin, and tox clues · rapid trauma survey · quick focal neuro exam. Every AMS patient gets a bedside glucose before anything else.[1]
5 DO NOT MISS
1. Hypoglycemia — fingerstick now; treat before anything else.
2. Hypoxia / hypercarbia — airway, oxygenation, ventilation.
3. CNS catastrophe (stroke, bleed, status epilepticus) — focal deficit, gaze, subtle seizure.
4. CNS infection (meningitis/encephalitis) — fever + altered; empiric coverage early.
5. Toxidrome / withdrawal (opioid, sedative, salicylate, CO, serotonin/NMS) — vitals, pupils, skin, gaps.[1]

Critical actions: get a fingerstick glucose immediately in every AMS patient; treat hypoglycemia, opioid hypoventilation, and sepsis/stroke threats in parallel, not in series; and do not label the patient psychiatric until delirium and medical mimics are addressed.[3]

2 · What to do right now

Toggle the sick-check finding above and the single highest-priority lane appears below — hypoglycemia overrides everything, then opioid hypoventilation, stroke, seizure, CNS infection, and toxidrome in that order. Tapping the matching box in the algorithm (section 6) focuses that lane's content.

Hypoglycemia — treat immediately
Do not wait for the lab glucose. Next 5 minutes: IV dextrose if access; glucagon if no access, but recognize vomiting/aspiration risk. Give thiamine when Wernicke risk exists, but do not delay dextrose for severe hypoglycemia. Recheck glucose and mental status, then identify cause: insulin/sulfonylurea, sepsis, renal failure, adrenal insufficiency, liver disease, poor intake.[2]
Opioid / sedative respiratory depression
Next 5 minutes: support ventilation first. Naloxone titrated to adequate respirations, not full wakefulness, when opioid toxicity is plausible. Prepare the airway if persistent hypoventilation, aspiration, or mixed sedative toxicity. Pitfall: a patient may breathe better after naloxone but stay sedated from xylazine/medetomidine, alcohol, benzodiazepines, or clonidine — open Xylazine Toxicity if deeply sedated after ventilation improves.[6]
Stroke / ICH / SAH
Next 5 minutes: glucose already checked, stroke alert if within or near any treatment window, non-contrast CT head, CTA/perfusion per local protocol for LVO/window selection, BP management by diagnosis, and reverse anticoagulation early if ICH is suspected in an anticoagulated patient. Pitfall: severe hypoglycemia can mimic stroke — treat glucose first. Open Acute Ischemic Stroke, ICH, or SAH.[4][5]
Seizure / nonconvulsive status
Next 5 minutes: time the seizure; give a benzodiazepine if ongoing ≥5 min or recurrent without return to baseline; check glucose and sodium; oxygen/suction/positioning; second-line antiseizure medication if benzodiazepine-refractory. If AMS persists unexplained after a convulsive seizure, suspect nonconvulsive status and get the EEG pathway moving. Open Status Epilepticus.[7]
Sepsis / CNS infection
Next 5 minutes: cultures if they do not delay therapy, lactate, broad antibiotics for sepsis physiology, and empiric CNS antibiotics/acyclovir when meningitis/encephalitis is plausible. CT before LP when indicated, but empiric treatment must not wait for CT/LP. Pitfall: elderly septic patients may present as isolated AMS without fever. Open Sepsis Management or Meningitis / Encephalitis.[8]
Toxicologic AMS
Next 5 minutes: ECG for QRS/QTc, glucose, VBG/ABG, lactate, BMP, osm gap/anion gap when relevant, acetaminophen/salicylate levels if ingestion is possible, and poison center early. Pitfall: the urine drug screen rarely explains the immediate resuscitation problem — the ECG and acid-base do. Open the matching tox consult: TCA Toxidrome, Salicylate, Toxic Alcohols, CO Toxicity, or Alcohol Withdrawal.[1]
No single killer declares itself → run the default AMS bundle
When no one reversible cause fits, do not stall — start the default rescue bundle in section 4, then reassess. You can return to a specific lane the moment a finding declares itself.[1]

4 · Default rescue bundle + reassess

Default AMS bundle when no single cause declares itself — start it in parallel while you keep hunting:

  • Airway/ventilation: oxygen, ETCO2, suction, aspiration precautions.
  • Glucose now and repeat after treatment.
  • Vitals including core temperature if hypothermia/hyperthermia possible.
  • ECG.
  • CBC/CMP, VBG/ABG, lactate when ill; UA/CXR only when clinically pointed.
  • Tox labs when ingestion possible: acetaminophen, salicylate, ethanol, osm gap/anion gap.
  • CT head for trauma, focal neuro, anticoagulation, severe headache, seizure, persistent unexplained AMS, immunocompromise, or concern for bleed/mass.
  • Collateral: last known normal, medications, and the story.
Reassess after every reversible fix
Recheck the patient after each intervention. Persistent AMS stays medical until proven otherwise. Still altered or worsening → return to the exclusions. Improving but not baseline, or cause unclear → move to imaging/EEG/LP. Back to baseline with a clear reversible cause → disposition.[1]

CT/CTA/LP/EEG/MRI are selected by red flags and persistent unexplained AMS — not ordered reflexively.
