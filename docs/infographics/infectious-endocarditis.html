Infective Endocarditis: Recognize, Culture, Echo, Classify, Treat

Adults ≥18 with suspected infective endocarditis (native or prosthetic valve, CIED/VAD, right-sided/IVDU-associated) in the ED. Excludes pediatric IE, isolated infective myocarditis/pericarditis, and noninfectious valve disease (Libman-Sacks, marantic/NBTE) which need separate pathways.

⚠ Unstable or complicated IE (shock, acute heart failure, new heart block/PR prolongation, embolic stroke/ICH, or prosthetic valve/device concern) needs simultaneous resuscitation, cultures, empiric antibiotics, STAT echo, and immediate ID + cardiology + CT surgery activation — do NOT wait for Duke criteria to become definite.
⚠ Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics if this will not delay resuscitation; in shock, do NOT delay antibiotics for perfect culture timing.
⚠ A negative TTE does not exclude IE when suspicion remains high — obtain TEE and repeat imaging. Do NOT discharge high-suspicion IE solely because TTE is negative.
⚠ POCUS can identify a large vegetation or severe regurgitation, but it cannot rule out IE — it must never delay cultures, antibiotics, or formal echo.
⚠ Do NOT give thrombolytics for presumed ischemic stroke when IE is suspected — evaluate for mycotic aneurysm or hemorrhage first, and do not anticoagulate solely for IE.
⚠ Persistent S. aureus bacteremia is never a benign contaminant — prosthetic material, intracardiac device, dialysis access, or injection drug use should lower the threshold for TEE and source control.
⚠ Surgical triggers — acute heart failure, periannular abscess/pseudoaneurysm/fistula/dehiscence, new AV block, persistent bacteremia/fever despite therapy, fungal or highly resistant organism, or recurrent emboli/large mobile vegetation — need early CT surgery consultation; CIED endocarditis usually requires complete device/lead extraction for cure.

1 · Recognize & risk-stratify

Suspect IE in fever, bacteremia, or sepsis without a source plus a new murmur, embolic phenomena, back pain, stroke/ICH, renal/splenic infarct, septic pulmonary emboli, or glomerulonephritis — especially with a risk substrate.[1][2][3]

High-risk substrates
Prosthetic valve, prior IE, transcatheter valve/valve repair, ventricular assist device, congenital cyanotic heart disease, cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED), hemodialysis access, injection drug use, indwelling vascular catheter, immunocompromise, or recent invasive procedure.[1][2]
  1. Stabilize ABCs and sepsis physiology.
  2. Draw blood cultures before antibiotics if feasible.
  3. Start empiric antibiotics immediately if unstable.
  4. Order TTE; obtain TEE when suspicion is high or prosthetic/device disease is possible.
  5. Involve ID, cardiology, and CT surgery early for complicated disease.

Unstable or complicated IE — act now
Do these in parallel: sepsis resuscitation + ICU-level monitoring; 2 sets, preferably 3 sets of blood cultures immediately if this will not delay antibiotics; start empiric Vancomycin + Cefepime; STAT ECG (new AV block/PR prolongation suggests periannular abscess); TTE now, TEE if prosthetic/device disease, poor TTE, aortic valve concern, abscess concern, or high suspicion; call ID + cardiology now, CT surgery early. Do NOT wait for Duke criteria to become definite when the patient is unstable or has a surgical complication.[1][3]

Stable suspected IE — recognition pattern

2 · Cultures, Duke-ISCVID & echo

Before antibiotics if feasible, obtain 2 sets, preferably 3 sets of blood cultures from separate peripheral venipunctures — do not wait for fever spikes; underfilled bottles reduce yield. If unstable, draw cultures immediately but do NOT delay antibiotics for ideal timing. After antibiotics start, repeat blood cultures every 24-48 hours until clearance, especially for S. aureus, enterococcus, Candida, prosthetic/device infection, or persistent fever. Draw peripheral cultures plus line cultures if a catheter source is possible — do not rely on line-only cultures for IE diagnosis.[1][2][3]

Classification2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria
Definite clinical IE2 major criteria, or 1 major + 3 minor criteria, or 5 minor criteria
Possible IE1 major + 1 minor, or 3 minor criteria

Criteria support diagnosis and research classification. They do NOT replace resuscitation, source control, or specialist consultation when the patient is unstable.[2]

Positive echo / direct evidence — document valve involved (native vs prosthetic, left vs right), vegetation size/mobility, new or severe regurgitation, ventricular function, abscess/pseudoaneurysm/fistula/perforation/dehiscence, CIED lead vegetation or pocket infection, and embolic/metastatic complications. Admission; complicated disease needs an endocarditis-team center with cardiac surgery availability.[1][3]

Negative TTE, high suspicion
A negative TTE does not exclude IE when suspicion remains high. Obtain TEE for prosthetic valve/CIED/VAD, S. aureus or persistent bacteremia, poor TTE windows, aortic valve/root concern, new AV block, embolic disease/stroke/ICH, or septic pulmonary emboli. If initial imaging is negative but suspicion persists, repeat echo in several days and reassess for CT/PET-CT with cardiology/ID guidance. Do NOT discharge high-suspicion IE solely because TTE is negative.[1][3]
POCUS recognition
POCUS/TTE can identify large mobile valve-attached masses, new severe regurgitation, gross valve destruction, right-sided vegetations, RV strain from septic pulmonary emboli, or heart-failure physiology. Limitations: POCUS cannot rule out IE. Small vegetations, prosthetic valves, aortic root abscess, and device infection often require TEE and/or advanced imaging — it must not delay cultures, antibiotics for unstable patients, or formal echo.[1][4]

3 · Anatomic/hardware context & empiric therapy

Set Valve / hardware context above (native / prosthetic / right-sided) — the matching empiric plan appears below when the patient is not already unstable.

Native left-sided — cultures first if stable
Obtain cultures first and discuss empiric-antibiotic timing with ID if IE is strongly suspected but the patient is not septic — culture yield matters. Once the organism is identified, narrow promptly; avoid prolonged broad-spectrum therapy without microbiologic or imaging direction. Do NOT add anticoagulation solely for IE.[1][3]
Prosthetic valve / CIED / VAD — treat as complicated
Treat as complicated IE until proven otherwise: ID + cardiology early, CT surgery or electrophysiology/device team depending on substrate; TEE is usually required, with cardiac CT/PET-CT/WBC SPECT when echo is nondiagnostic; obtain cultures before antibiotics if feasible and repeat until clearance. Empiric therapy usually requires MRSA, coagulase-negative staphylococci, enterococci, and gram-negative coverage pending cultures and local guidance. CIED endocarditis generally requires complete device/lead extraction for cure — suppressive antibiotics alone are usually failure-prone. Rifampin is an adjunct only after bacteremia is controlled and susceptibilities are known; avoid empiric monotherapy.[1][3]
Right-sided / IVDU / dialysis
Common pattern: tricuspid IE, S. aureus, injection drug use, hemodialysis catheter/access, septic pulmonary emboli — look for pleuritic chest pain, hypoxia, hemoptysis, cavitary nodules, vertebral osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, or catheter infection. TTE often sees tricuspid vegetations, but TEE may still be needed for pulmonary valve, device/catheter involvement, poor windows, or persistent bacteremia. Vancomycin-centered empiric therapy when MRSA risk is high; remove infected catheters when feasible. Surgical/percutaneous consultation for persistent bacteremia, recurrent septic pulmonary emboli, severe TR/right heart failure, fungal infection, or medical-therapy failure.[1][3]

Culture-negative IE
Common causes: prior antibiotics, fastidious/intracellular organisms, fungi, or noninfectious mimics. 5 · Complications & disposition

Surgery / source control
Call CT surgery early for: acute heart failure from valve regurgitation/obstruction; periannular abscess, pseudoaneurysm, fistula, prosthetic dehiscence, or new AV block; persistent bacteremia/fever despite appropriate therapy; fungal/Candida/highly resistant organism or uncontrolled infection; recurrent emboli or large mobile vegetation; prosthetic valve IE with complication. Right-sided disease: consult for persistent bacteremia, large vegetations with recurrent septic pulmonary emboli, severe TR/right-heart failure, fungal infection, or medical-therapy failure. CIED: complete device/lead extraction is usually required — antibiotics alone are usually inadequate.[1][3]
Neurologic complications
IE can cause ischemic stroke, ICH/SAH, meningitis/encephalitis-like presentations, seizure, brain abscess, and mycotic aneurysm. CT/CTA head/neck for focal deficit, severe headache, or ICH/SAH concern; MRI brain for embolic burden. Do not give thrombolytics for presumed ischemic stroke when IE is suspected — hemorrhage and mycotic aneurysm risk are major concerns. Do not anticoagulate solely for IE; handle existing anticoagulation with neurology/cardiology/ID. Alert CT surgery/neuro teams — neurologic complications affect valve-surgery timing. Red flags for mycotic aneurysm: ICH/SAH, thunderclap headache, persistent bacteremia with neuro symptoms, multifocal embolic infarcts.[1][3]
  • Most suspected IE patients require admission.
  • ICU/monitored setting: shock, respiratory failure, acute heart failure, high-grade AV block/new conduction disease, severe valve dysfunction, neurologic complication, persistent bacteremia, prosthetic/device infection, dialysis/IVDU with septic emboli, or need for urgent surgery.
  • Transfer to an endocarditis-capable center: complicated IE needing CT surgery, prosthetic valve complication, CIED/VAD infection, abscess, uncontrolled infection, recurrent emboli, or advanced imaging unavailable.
  • Handoff must include culture times/sites, antibiotics and timing, echo findings/plan, valve/device history, organism/susceptibility if known, embolic/metastatic infection screen, renal dosing needs, anticoagulant/antiplatelet status, and consulted teams.

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT wait for Duke criteria to become definite** when the patient is unstable or has a surgical complication.

**Do NOT discharge high-suspicion IE solely because TTE is negative** — obtain TEE.

**Do NOT rely on POCUS to rule out IE**, and do not let it delay cultures, antibiotics, or formal echo.

**Do NOT anticoagulate solely for IE.**

**Do NOT give thrombolytics** for presumed ischemic stroke when IE is suspected.

**Do NOT use rifampin as empiric monotherapy, or gentamicin reflexively**, in native-valve S. aureus IE.

**Do NOT rely on suppressive antibiotics alone for CIED endocarditis** — device/lead extraction is usually required for cure.

**Do NOT rely on line-only cultures** for the diagnosis of IE.
