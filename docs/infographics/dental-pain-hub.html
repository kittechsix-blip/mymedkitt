Dental Pain Hub — ED Triage and Management

ED patients with dental or facial pain. Covers sick-check triage across airway/deep-space infection, spreading systemic infection, dental trauma, localized abscess, pulpitis, pericoronitis, dry socket, and non-dental mimics, through analgesia, selective imaging, and disposition. ADA analgesia and antibiotic guidance referenced here applies to adolescents and adults; definitive tooth-preservation and avulsion procedures are deferred to the linked Dental Trauma and Dental Avulsion trees; airway-risk deep-space infection requiring ENT/OMFS involvement is deferred to the linked Deep Neck Infection tree.

⚠ Dental pain plus airway, floor-of-mouth, trismus, dysphagia, or systemic toxicity is deep-space infection until proven otherwise
⚠ Uncomplicated toothache/pulpitis usually needs analgesia and dental care, not automatic antibiotics
⚠ NSAID plus acetaminophen is first-line analgesia for most acute dental pain when safe
⚠ Cardiac ischemia can rarely refer pain to the jaw/teeth — consider ACS in patients with exertional symptoms, dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, or cardiac risk factors

Sick check first

Dental pain is usually a definitive-dental-care problem. The ED job is to find airway/deep infection, control pain, avoid unnecessary antibiotics/opioids, and secure follow-up.

  1. Ludwig angina / deep space infection — floor-of-mouth swelling, drooling, airway threat.
  2. Cavernous sinus thrombosis — spreading facial/orbital infection, cranial nerve signs.
  3. Necrotizing fasciitis of the neck — pain out of proportion, crepitus, toxicity.
  4. Cardiac referred pain (ACS) — jaw/tooth pain without dental source, risk factors.
  5. Post-extraction hemorrhage / odontogenic sepsis — uncontrolled bleeding or systemic toxicity.
  • Airway: voice, drooling, stridor, tongue/floor-of-mouth elevation, neck swelling.
  • Infection: fever, rigors, facial cellulitis, trismus, dysphagia, immunocompromise, diabetes, toxicity.
  • Trauma: avulsion, fracture, luxation, alveolar injury, missing tooth fragments.
  • Pain source: caries/pulpitis, periapical abscess, periodontal abscess, pericoronitis, post-extraction dry socket, sinus/ear/TMJ mimic.
  • Analgesia early: NSAID plus acetaminophen when safe; consider dental block.

Management algorithm

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for complete node text, and Deep Neck Infection, Dental Trauma, Dental Avulsion, Chest Pain Hub, Trigeminal Neuralgia, and Ear Infection for the linked pathways.

Airway-threat / deep-space odontogenic infection

Open Deep Neck Infection. Ludwig angina and deep neck infection can deteriorate quickly.

  1. Sit upright.
  2. Airway equipment and experienced help early.
  3. ENT/OMFS/anesthesia as local resources allow.
  4. IV access; start broad IV antibiotics.
  5. CT neck/face with contrast only when stable enough and after airway risk is addressed.
Danger signs
Drooling, stridor, muffled voice, dysphagia, tongue elevation, firm floor of mouth, marked trismus, toxic appearance, rapidly progressive swelling. Airway plan comes before scanner plan.

Spreading infection / systemic involvement

Treat as infection with source-control needs
Find the source tooth, assess spread, give analgesia, start antibiotics when systemic involvement or spreading cellulitis is present, and arrange urgent dental/OMFS follow-up or admission. Immunocompromised, diabetic, toxic, trismus/dysphagia, or rapidly progressive swelling patients need a lower threshold for imaging, IV antibiotics, and admission.
ED pitfall
Antibiotics alone do not fix the source. The patient needs drainage, extraction, root canal, or dental procedure when indicated.

Dental trauma / missing tooth fragment

Open Dental Trauma or Dental Avulsion. Trauma follows time-sensitive tooth-preservation rules, especially permanent avulsion.

  • Account for all teeth/fragments.
  • Protect avulsed permanent tooth.
  • Do not reimplant primary teeth.
  • Assess alveolar fracture.
  • Tetanus when indicated.
  • Control bleeding; splint/refer per injury type.
  • If a tooth or fragment is missing and aspiration is plausible, obtain chest imaging.

Localized dental abscess

Localized swelling without systemic toxicity or airway/deep-space signs
Priorities: analgesia, consider drainage if fluctuant and within ED skill/resources, dental source-control referral, and antibiotics only when guideline-supported — systemic involvement, spreading cellulitis, immunocompromise, or when definitive dental care is not immediately available for selected abscess scenarios. Drain/source control and dental follow-up matter more than reflex antibiotics.

Common adult options when antibiotics are indicated include amoxicillin or penicillin VK; broaden/adjust for allergy, severe infection, recent antibiotics, or local protocol.

Pulpitis / uncomplicated toothache

Typical pattern
Severe tooth pain with hot/cold/sweet, caries or broken filling, no facial swelling, no fever, no trismus, no systemic signs. ADA guidance recommends against routine systemic antibiotics for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis or symptomatic apical periodontitis in immunocompetent adults when there is no systemic involvement. Treat pain and arrange definitive dental care. Analgesia plus dental care, not automatic antibiotics, for uncomplicated pulpitis.

Open Dental Trauma only if fracture/trauma is part of the story.

Pericoronitis / periodontal infection

Local care and dental follow-up first
Posterior molar pain with inflamed operculum, food trapping, bad taste, and local swelling often needs irrigation, analgesia, oral hygiene instructions, and dental follow-up. Antibiotics are reserved for systemic involvement, spreading cellulitis, immunocompromise, or significant trismus/deep-space concern. Escalate if trismus, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth findings, fever, or mandibular swelling suggests spread beyond a local process.

Alveolar osteitis / dry socket

Not spreading infection
Severe pain 2-5 days after extraction, empty socket/exposed bone, foul taste, and minimal swelling usually suggests dry socket rather than spreading infection. ED care is analgesia, gentle irrigation if debris, avoid aggressive curettage, and dental/oral surgery follow-up for medicated dressing. Antibiotics are not routine unless cellulitis, fever, immunocompromise, or deep infection signs are present.

Dental pain mimics

If the teeth do not explain the pain, widen the lens.

  • Maxillary sinusitis: upper tooth pain with sinus symptoms.
  • Otitis media/externa, mastoiditis.
  • TMJ dysfunction.
  • Trigeminal neuralgia: electric, brief, triggerable pain.
  • Herpes zoster before rash.
  • Cardiac ischemia can rarely refer to jaw/teeth, especially exertional symptoms, dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, or risk factors.

Open Chest Pain Hub, Trigeminal Neuralgia, or Ear Infection when the phenotype fits.

Rescue / initial bundle

  • Visualize all quadrants, gingiva, floor of mouth, uvula, tonsils, neck, and facial symmetry.
  • Percuss culprit tooth, check mobility, look for vestibular fluctuance, draining sinus, caries/fracture.
  • Vitals and immune risk.
  • Analgesia: NSAID plus acetaminophen when safe; consider local dental block.
  • Avoid opioid-first strategy.
  • Antibiotics only when indicated by systemic/spreading infection or selected abscess scenario without timely dental care.
  • Give a concrete dental follow-up plan.

Reassess pain, oral intake, swelling trajectory, and airway/deep-space signs before discharge.

Analgesia / dental block strategy

For most adolescents/adults, ADA guidance supports NSAIDs, alone or with acetaminophen, as first-line acute dental pain therapy when there are no contraindications.

Consider before dosing
Renal disease, GI bleed risk, anticoagulation, pregnancy, liver disease, heavy alcohol use, total acetaminophen dose, and age/frailty.

Opioids — rescue only
Reserve for narrow rescue situations after nonopioid and block strategy, with short duration and risk review.

Imaging / consult strategy

  • CT neck/face with contrast: deep-space infection, trismus/dysphagia, toxic/systemic patient, orbital involvement, rapidly progressive swelling, unclear extent.
  • Panorex/dental films: dental-service dependent, fracture/root/periapical planning when available.
  • CT face: significant trauma, alveolar/maxillofacial fracture, embedded tooth fragment.
  • CXR: missing tooth/tooth fragment with aspiration concern.

Consults: OMFS/dentistry for source control, ENT/anesthesia for airway-risk infections, ophthalmology if orbital spread.

Disposition

Disposition depends on airway, spread, systemic illness, immune risk, pain control, and dental access.

Admit / transfer / specialty
Admit, transfer, or emergently consult for airway-risk findings, Ludwig/deep neck infection, sepsis, orbital spread, rapidly progressive swelling, immunocompromise/diabetes with significant infection, inability to tolerate PO, failed outpatient therapy, or need for operative drainage/source control. Dangerous odontogenic infection needs airway-aware admission and source control.
Urgent dental / OMFS follow-up
Use this lane for localized abscess/cellulitis without airway/systemic toxicity, fracture without emergent features, or pain requiring a definitive dental procedure. Give specific follow-up timing, antibiotic rationale if prescribed, analgesic schedule, and return precautions for spread or airway symptoms. Every dental pain discharge needs a definitive dental-care destination, not just medication.
Discharge checklist
Discharge only when no airway/deep-space/systemic red flags, pain is controlled enough for PO, diagnosis is low-risk, and dental follow-up is realistic. Document no deep-space signs, analgesia plan, antibiotic indication if used, and dental follow-up instructions.
Return now for
Fever, worse swelling, neck swelling, trouble swallowing, drooling, voice change, breathing difficulty, trismus, eye swelling/vision change, inability to drink, uncontrolled pain, or rapidly worsening symptoms.
