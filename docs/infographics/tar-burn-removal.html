Tar / Asphalt Burn Removal — Dissolve, Don't Peel

ED patients with hot tar/asphalt contact burns to the skin (e.g. occupational roofing/paving exposures). Airway/inhalation injury must be screened and, if present, stabilized before tar-specific wound care begins.

⚠ Never mechanically peel, pick, or scrub adherent tar — it strips viable skin; dissolve with a petroleum-based emulsifier instead.
⚠ Never use acetone, gasoline, or other organic solvents to remove tar — they cause chemical injury.
⚠ Never apply ice to cool the burn — worsens injury; avoid hypothermia when cooling large burns.
⚠ Airway/inhalation injury and CO/cyanide concern supersede tar removal — screen before focusing on the skin.
⚠ Burn depth and TBSA cannot be judged while tar covers the skin — assess only after the tar is cleared.

Overview

Adherent hot tar traps heat and hides burn depth. The core rule: never mechanically peel adherent tar — it strips viable skin. Cool first, then dissolve with a petroleum-based emulsifier.

Dissolve, don't peel
Never mechanically peel or scrub adherent tar — reapply emulsifier and let it release atraumatically.
  • Screen for inhalation injury and CO/cyanide BEFORE wound care — airway supersedes tar removal.
  • Cool the tar (~20 min, no ice) to stop thermal injury; never apply ice to a large burn.
  • Dissolve with a petroleum-based emulsifier; NEVER use acetone, gasoline, or other organic solvents.
  • Never mechanically peel or scrub adherent tar — reapply emulsifier and let it release atraumatically.
  • Assess burn depth and TBSA only AFTER the tar is cleared; refer deep/large burns to a burn center.

Airway / Inhalation Screen

Wound care never precedes airway. Hot-tar exposures (enclosed spaces, kettle flashes) can carry inhalation injury — screen before focusing on the skin. Signs: facial burns, singed nasal hair, soot in oropharynx, hoarseness, stridor, carbonaceous sputum, enclosed-space fire.

Airway takes priority
Manage the airway and systemic injury before tar: early intubation if progressive airway edema, stridor, or deep facial burns; 100% oxygen for suspected carbon monoxide; consider cyanide toxicity in enclosed-space fires. Then resume tar dissolution once the patient is stabilized. Significant burns with airway involvement warrant burn-center transfer.

Step 1 — Cool + Analgesia

  1. Cool the tar immediately with room-temperature water to stop ongoing thermal injury and harden the tar so it stops spreading. Cool ~20 minutes; avoid ice (worsens injury) and avoid hypothermia in large burns.
  2. Provide analgesia early — tar burns are painful and dissolution takes time.
  3. Do NOT attempt to pick, peel, or scrub the tar off at this stage.
No ice
Do NOT apply ice (worsens injury) or risk hypothermia in large burns.

Dissolution takes time; provide analgesia up front and reassess. IV opioid titration for moderate-severe pain; adjunct acetaminophen/NSAID for smaller burns; consider regional/digital block for distal-extremity tar burns.

Step 3 — Apply + Dwell

  1. Apply the chosen emulsifier generously over ALL adherent tar.
  2. Cover with a non-adherent dressing and a bulky outer wrap to hold the agent against the tar.
  3. Let it dwell — tar dissolves gradually. Reapply the agent every 1-2 hours. As the tar softens it lifts off atraumatically with gentle wiping.
  4. Complete dissolution can take several hours and may extend beyond the ED visit; outpatient continuation with ongoing ointment is acceptable for stable, small burns.
Do not force, scrub, or peel
Do NOT force, scrub, or peel any tar that is still adherent — reapply the emulsifier and wait.

Burn depth CANNOT be judged while tar covers the skin. Only after the tar is cleared: estimate depth (superficial / partial / full thickness) and TBSA, and update tetanus status.
