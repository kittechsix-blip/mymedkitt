Continuous Bladder Irrigation — Clot Retention

Adults with gross hematuria and clot retention (post-TURP, anticoagulated, BPH, known bladder tumor) requiring bladder decompression and irrigation in the ED. Excludes painless urinary retention with clear urine (no blood) — that pathway uses standard catheterization and the Urinary Retention workup, not CBI.

⚠ CBI does NOT break up clots — existing clots must be MANUALLY evacuated with a Toomey syringe BEFORE starting irrigation.
⚠ Use a large-bore 3-way (hematuria) catheter, typically 22-24 Fr — a standard small Foley clogs with clots and has no irrigation channel.
⚠ During CBI, outflow must keep up with inflow. If outflow lags, STOP the inflow immediately — risk of bladder overdistension/perforation.
⚠ Do NOT force the Toomey syringe against firm resistance during manual evacuation — risk of bladder injury.
⚠ All gross hematuria with clot retention needs urologic follow-up even after CBI clears — to evaluate for malignancy and source.

Overview — Two Rules That Prevent Harm

For gross hematuria with clot retention. Two rules prevent harm: (1) CBI does NOT break up clots — you must manually evacuate existing clots first; (2) use a large-bore 3-way catheter, not a standard Foley.

Do not skip manual evacuation
CBI fluid flows around clots — it does not dissolve or break them. Starting irrigation before manual clot evacuation is the classic failure mode of this procedure.

Confirm Clot Retention vs Simple Retention

Distinguish clot retention (gross hematuria + clots obstructing outflow) from simple urinary retention (no blood). The pathway and catheter choice differ. Clot retention: blood and clots in urine, painful distended bladder, prior TURP / anticoagulation / BPH / known bladder tumor.

Not Clot Retention — Use Retention Pathway

CBI not indicated
Clear urine with painless retention is simple urinary retention. CBI is not indicated. Decompress with a standard catheter and work up the cause via Urinary Retention. If catheter placement is difficult, escalate per standard difficult-catheterization steps.

Identify + Address the Bleeding Source

While preparing the procedure, address reversible drivers. Reversal decisions are bleeding-severity dependent; severe hematuria with hemodynamic change warrants resuscitation and urgent urology.

  • Review anticoagulants/antiplatelets; correct supratherapeutic INR; consider reversal if bleeding is severe (cross-reference institutional reversal pathway)
  • Check CBC, coags, type-and-screen if bleeding is brisk or hemoglobin is dropping
  • Note prior TURP/biopsy, known bladder/prostate tumor, recent instrumentation

Step 1 — Place Large-Bore 3-Way Catheter

A standard 14-16 Fr Foley clogs with clots and has no irrigation channel. Use a large-bore 3-way (hematuria) catheter. The three lumens are: balloon inflation, drainage/outflow, and irrigation inflow. Do NOT start irrigation fluid yet — clots come out first, by hand.

22-24 Fr3-way hematuria catheter size
  1. Sterile prep; insert the 3-way catheter; confirm intravesical placement (urine/blood return) before inflating the balloon
  2. Inflate the balloon to the manufacturer's volume
  3. Confirm three lumens: balloon inflation, drainage/outflow, irrigation inflow

Step 2 — MANUAL Clot Evacuation (Mandatory First)

This is the step clinicians skip — and CBI fails without it
CBI fluid flows around clots; it does not break them. Only once the bladder is substantially clot-free do you start CBI.
~60 mL mLToomey-syringe irrigation volume per cycle
  1. Using a catheter-tip (Toomey) syringe with ~60 mL sterile saline, instill and aspirate through the drainage port
  2. Repeat aspiration/instillation, evacuating clots, until returns are clear or only lightly blood-tinged and no further clot is retrieved
  3. Be patient — this can take many syringe cycles; gentle instillation, do NOT force against firm resistance (risk of bladder injury/perforation)
  4. Only once the bladder is substantially clot-free do you start CBI
If clots will not clear
If you cannot clear clots manually or the bladder will not drain → urology now for cystoscopic clot evacuation.

Step 3 — Start + Titrate CBI

Connect sterile normal saline to the irrigation (inflow) port and open the drainage (outflow) to a closed bag. The goal is to keep clots from re-forming, NOT to break existing clots (those were removed manually). Maintain a closed, sterile system; track net fluid balance (subtract irrigant volume from total output to estimate true urine output).

  • Titrate inflow rate to keep effluent light pink to clear — faster for brisk bleeding, slower as it clears
  • Brisk/bright-red bleeding → run inflow fast initially to keep effluent dilute
  • As effluent lightens → slow the inflow stepwise; clear/light-pink and stable → maintain a low maintenance rate
  • Re-darkening effluent → increase inflow and reassess for a re-bleed/clot

Inflow vs Outflow — Watch Closely

The key safety check during CBI
Outflow must roughly match or exceed inflow. If you keep running fluid in but little comes out, the bladder is overfilling.

Outflow Lagging — STOP + Troubleshoot

Stop the irrigation inflow immediately
Continuing to instill fluid into a non-draining bladder risks overdistension and perforation. Do not restart CBI until free drainage is re-established.
  1. Manually aspirate the catheter with a Toomey syringe to clear an obstructing clot
  2. Check the catheter is not kinked and the balloon is not displaced into the urethra
  3. If outflow does not promptly recover, or bladder injury is suspected (severe pain, low return, peritoneal signs) → urology now for cystoscopy ± imaging

Disposition + Hematuria Workup

CBI running well with clearing effluent: admit for ongoing CBI and monitoring in most clot-retention cases; brisk or persistent bleeding warrants urology involvement and possible intervention (cystoscopic fulguration, intravesical agents). Continue to monitor hemoglobin, coagulation status, and net fluid balance.

Mandatory urologic follow-up
All gross hematuria with clot retention needs urologic follow-up even if it clears — to evaluate for malignancy and source.
