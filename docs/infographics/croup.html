Croup: Severity-Driven Steroid + Epinephrine Management

Children roughly 6 months-6 years in the ED with viral croup (laryngotracheobronchitis). Excludes suspected bacterial tracheitis, epiglottitis, peritonsillar abscess, and airway foreign body — each needs a different pathway.

⚠ Give a glucocorticoid to EVERY severity — Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg PO (max 16 mg) single dose reduces return visits by ~50% (NNT approximately 7). No child with croup should leave without a steroid.
⚠ Epinephrine relief is temporary (onset minutes, duration 1-2 hours). Observe a minimum of 2 hours after the last nebulized Racemic Epinephrine dose for rebound stridor before any discharge decision.
⚠ Do NOT limit epinephrine to a single dose in severe disease — repeated doses may prevent intubation; restart the 2-hour observation clock after each dose.
⚠ If intubation is needed, use an ETT 0.5-1.0 size smaller than age-predicted (subglottic narrowing) and call anesthesia/ENT for airway backup before attempting.
⚠ Keep the child calm — agitation worsens airway obstruction. Minimize interventions, use blow-by oxygen, and avoid unnecessary exam maneuvers in severe/impending-failure croup.
⚠ Rule out mimics before committing to the croup pathway — toxic appearance, drooling, high fever without a barking cough, or failure to improve with steroid + epinephrine should prompt evaluation for bacterial tracheitis, epiglottitis, peritonsillar abscess, or airway foreign body.

1 · Recognize croup

Subglottic inflammation from parainfluenza
Barking, seal-like cough + inspiratory stridor + hoarseness, usually after a 1-3 day URI prodrome with low-grade fever. Parainfluenza virus inflames the subglottic airway — the narrowest point in a young child's airway.[2]

Affects 3% of children aged 6 months to 3 years, peaking at 1-2 years. Most cases are mild and self-limited — only 1-8% require hospitalization and <3% of admitted patients require intubation.[1][2]

Mild — a single oral steroid is the whole treatment
No stridor at rest → no epinephrine. Give one dose of Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg PO (max 16 mg), observe 1-2 hours, and discharge with return precautions.[3][8]
Moderate — steroid PLUS nebulized epinephrine
Stridor at rest with mild retractions. Give Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg PO/IM (max 16 mg) and Racemic Epinephrine now. Keep the child calm — agitation worsens obstruction. Then observe a minimum of 2 hours after the epinephrine dose.[4]
Severe — aggressive dual therapy, may repeat epinephrine
Marked retractions and agitation/distress. Give dexamethasone + nebulized epinephrine as above — repeated epinephrine doses may prevent intubation and should not be artificially limited to one dose.[4] Heliox (70:30 He:O2) has limited, low-certainty evidence as an adjunct.[11] Have airway equipment at bedside; minimize agitating interventions.
Impending respiratory failure — activate the airway team
Clinical emergency. Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg IM if not already given, nebulized Racemic Epinephrine (may repeat), blow-by oxygen — do NOT agitate the child — and call anesthesia/ENT for airway backup. If intubating, use an ETT 0.5-1.0 size smaller than age-predicted (subglottic narrowing).[4]
Unable to tolerate oral
Give steroid by a non-oral route: Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg IM, or Budesonide 2 mg nebulized (onset 2-4 hours, slower than oral dex).[5][9]

5 · Observation & disposition

  • Post-dexamethasone: observe 1-2 hours. Most children improve within 2-3 hours; the ~36 hours half-life gives sustained benefit from one dose.[3]
  • Post-epinephrine: observe a minimum of 2 hours after the last dose — symptoms can rebound as the effect wears off.[4]
  • Repeat epinephrine at the same dose restarts the 2-hour clock; needing to repeat at all is itself a strong indicator for admission, and needing more than 2 total doses raises consideration for ICU-level monitoring.[4]
  • Discharge when there is no stridor at rest for at least 2 hours after the last epinephrine dose, the child tolerates fluids, and a reliable caregiver has access to emergency care.[2]
Multiple epinephrine doses
This patient has received 3 or more total nebulized epinephrine doses — consider ICU-level monitoring in addition to admission.[4]
Discharge home
No stridor at rest for at least 2 hours after the last epinephrine dose (or 1-2 hours post-dex if mild and improved), tolerating oral fluids, reliable caregiver with access to emergency care. Warn: symptoms are often worse at night; barking cough typically lasts 1-2 days, up to 5-7 days. No additional steroid doses needed.[2]
Hospital admission
Persistent stridor at rest despite dexamethasone + epinephrine, requiring multiple epinephrine doses, significant distress, unable to tolerate oral fluids, or age <6 months with moderate-severe croup. Continuous pulse oximetry, PRN nebulized epinephrine, reassess for bacterial tracheitis if not improving.[4]
ICU admission / airway management
Impending or actual respiratory failure, requiring intubation, or severe croup unresponsive to maximal therapy. ETT 0.5-1.0 size smaller than age-predicted; continue Dexamethasone q6-12h to facilitate extubation; plan extubation with a leak test after 24-72 hours. <3% of hospitalized croup patients require intubation.[4]

6 · Do NOT

Common croup pitfalls

Do NOT discharge on the basis of an epinephrine response alone. Benefit is transient (1-2 hours) — observe at least 2 hours for rebound first.[4]

Do NOT limit epinephrine to a single dose. Repeated doses may prevent intubation in severe disease.[4]

Do NOT rely on humidified air or cool mist — neither has evidence of benefit.[2]

Do NOT keep treating for croup when it is not improving with dexamethasone + epinephrine — reconsider bacterial tracheitis, epiglottitis, or foreign body.[4]

Do NOT attribute the 0.15 mg/kg vs. 0.6 mg/kg dexamethasone non-inferiority finding to the Parker prednisolone-vs-dexamethasone RCT — that trial compared different drugs and never tested a 0.15 mg/kg dexamethasone arm; the dose-ranging evidence is the pooled Aregbesola Cochrane review.[3][6]
