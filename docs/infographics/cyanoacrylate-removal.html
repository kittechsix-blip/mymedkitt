Overview

Cyanoacrylate exposure is alarming but usually benign. Two rules: NEVER use acetone near the eye, and NEVER force glued eyelids apart — both cause injury. Most exposures self-resolve in hours to days.

The two things that cause harm
Acetone near the eye, lid margins, or mucous membranes causes chemical injury. Forceful traction to free glued eyelids tears lid skin and can injure the cornea. Avoid both regardless of technique chosen.

Rule out globe injury first

Before any manipulation around the eye
Assess visual acuity in each eye as best you can with lids involved. Cyanoacrylate hardens fast and usually sits ON the surface rather than penetrating, but hardened fragments can cause corneal abrasion. If there is any suspicion of open-globe or significant ocular trauma, treat per Ocular Trauma / Globe Rupture and consult ophthalmology before manipulating. If no globe injury, proceed to the appropriate de-adhesion technique.

Eyelid de-adhesion — choose approach

For glued lids, two safe, evidence-described approaches. Do not force the lids apart with traction. Apply topical anesthetic first.

Topical anesthetic options
A topical ocular anesthetic (e.g., proparacaine or tetracaine drops) along the lid margin/fissure improves comfort and cooperation before any hook technique or lash trimming. Topical anesthetics are for procedural use only — never prescribed for home use (corneal toxicity / delayed healing). Combine with liberal ophthalmic ointment to lubricate and soften glue.

Antibiotic-ointment dwell

  1. Instill topical anesthetic for comfort.
  2. Apply bacitracin or erythromycin ophthalmic ointment generously along and into the glued lid margin/lashes.
  3. Have the patient reapply every few hours; the petrolatum base softens cyanoacrylate and the lids separate over hours to a couple of days.
  4. Do not pull. As the glue loosens, lashes release; trim any persistently glued lashes with scissors rather than yanking.
  5. Recheck the cornea once the lids open.

Gentle hook technique

  1. Topical anesthetic first.
  2. Identify any small gap in the lid fissure.
  3. Gently introduce a smooth blunt instrument (muscle/Jameson hook or moistened swab) and roll/lift to free glued lashes a few at a time. Lubricate with ophthalmic ointment to ease release.
  4. No forceful traction at any point — if it does not release easily, switch to the ointment-dwell approach and let time do the work.
  5. Trim stubborn glued lashes with scissors rather than pulling.
  6. Recheck the cornea once open.
No forceful traction
If lashes do not release easily with the hook, switch to the ointment-dwell approach rather than forcing it.

Skin-only de-adhesion

  1. Soak in warm soapy water.
  2. Apply a petroleum-based product (petrolatum, mineral oil, or a petroleum-based hand cleaner) and gently roll the skin apart — do not rip glued skin surfaces apart.
  3. Acetone may be used for skin AWAY from the eye and mucous membranes, but petroleum is gentler and preferred; never use acetone near the eye, lip margins, or mucosa.
  4. Glued lips: warm water + gentle peeling from inside the mouth; reassure that the bond releases.
  5. Most skin bonds release within minutes to hours.

Recheck cornea + reassure

  • Perform a fluorescein / corneal-abrasion check once the eye is accessible — hardened glue fragments can cause corneal abrasion.
  • Treat any abrasion per standard care (topical antibiotic, analgesia, ophthalmology follow-up).
  • Remove loose glue fragments from the conjunctival surface gently; do not chase well-adhered fragments — they slough over days.
  • Reassure: residual glue on the ocular surface typically self-detaches over hours to days; forcing it off causes more harm than leaving it.

Disposition

Discharge
Discharge most patients once lids are open / skin freed and the cornea is intact or has a small abrasion managed routinely. Return precautions: worsening pain, vision change, photophobia, discharge, or signs of infection.
Ophthalmology referral
Refer for corneal abrasion needing follow-up, retained adherent ocular-surface glue causing symptoms, or any concern for globe injury.
