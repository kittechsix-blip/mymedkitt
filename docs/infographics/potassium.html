Potassium: Read the Number, Pick the Pathway

Adults ≥18 in the ED with a serum potassium result and an ECG. Excludes pediatric dosing, pregnancy-specific management, and chronic outpatient titration — each needs a modified protocol.

⚠ First response to any hyperkalemia lab is the ECG. Features confirm the diagnosis; a severe lab with a normal ECG should prompt you to consider pseudohyperkalemia and repeat the sample.
⚠ Severe hypokalemia: KCl 5–10 mEq IV over 15–30 min with continuous cardiac monitoring — and always correct concurrent hypomagnesemia (up to 50% coexist and make K⁺ repletion refractory).
⚠ Severe hyperkalemia: start ALL three temporizing measures simultaneously — calcium, insulin/dextrose, and a beta-2 agonist. They are temporizing only; K⁺ rebounds in 2–4 h without definitive elimination.
⚠ Hypokalemic periodic paralysis is transcellular redistribution, NOT true depletion. Give oral KCl 1 mEq/kg immediate-release only; aggressive IV replacement causes dangerous rebound hyperkalemia. Avoid glucose and steroids.
⚠ IV calcium (calcium gluconate 3 g IV over 10 min peripherally, or calcium chloride 1 g centrally) stabilizes the myocardium for 30–60 min only — be ready to repeat.
⚠ Dialysis is indicated for chronic hemodialysis patients or the anuric patient failing the diuretic bomb — do not delay it hoping potassium binders will work.

2 · Hyperkalemia — stabilize, shift, eliminate

Severe = K⁺ ≥ 6.5 with ECG changes, or K⁺ ≥ 6.0 with rapid rise / ongoing release (TLS, rhabdo). Chronic hyperkalemia (e.g. dialysis) is better tolerated than acute.[1]

6 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT give temporizing measures without definitive therapy** — K⁺ rebounds in 2–4 h and hyperkalemia recurs.

**Do NOT use normal saline in hyperkalemia** — high chloride worsens K⁺; use isotonic bicarbonate as the resuscitative fluid if acidotic.

**Do NOT over-replete periodic paralysis** — it is a shift, not depletion; aggressive IV KCl causes rebound hyperkalemia.

**Do NOT rely on Kayexalate or binders** as sole treatment — weak, slow, and Kayexalate risks colonic necrosis.

**Do NOT forget magnesium** — up to 50% of hypokalemia coexists with hypomagnesemia and stays refractory until Mg is corrected.
