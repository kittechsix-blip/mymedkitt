Potassium: HyperK Emergencies, HypoK Repletion, and Periodic Paralysis

Adults in the ED/inpatient setting with lab-confirmed hyperkalemia (K+ >5.5 mEq/L) or hypokalemia (K+ <3.5 mEq/L), including hypokalemic periodic paralysis. Excludes pediatric dosing, chronic dialysis-maintenance protocols beyond the acute HD-referral decision, and pregnancy-specific dosing adjustments.

⚠ Never give normal saline in hyperkalemia — its high chloride content worsens hyperkalemia via renal chloride-bicarbonate exchange. Use LR/Plasmalyte, or isotonic bicarbonate if acidotic.
⚠ Hypokalemic periodic paralysis is transcellular shift, NOT true body K+ depletion — aggressive IV KCl replacement risks dangerous rebound hyperkalemia. Immediate-release/liquid oral KCl only.
⚠ Always check and correct magnesium in hypokalemia — up to 50% of patients have concurrent hypomagnesemia, which makes K⁺ repletion refractory.
⚠ Do NOT delay dialysis while waiting for potassium binders (SZC, patiromer) to work — they are mildly effective adjuncts only, never a substitute for definitive elimination.

Start all three temporizing measures simultaneously
These shift K+ into cells and stabilize the myocardium. They are temporizing only — K+ returns to baseline in 2-4 hours without definitive elimination therapy. Do NOT give temporizing measures without a definitive plan already running.

5 · Hypokalemic periodic paralysis

Key distinction: shift, not depletion
HypoPP is transcellular redistribution, NOT true body K+ depletion — this changes treatment fundamentally. Episodic proximal > distal flaccid paralysis; triggers: rest after strenuous exercise, carbohydrate-heavy evening meals, stress, cold, high sodium intake, corticosteroids, alcohol. Genetics: CACNA1S (calcium channel) or SCN4A (sodium channel) mutations.
Potassium Chloride Oral (immediate-release/liquid ONLY)
1 mEq/kg (~60 mEq for a 60 kg patient)
PO
Then 0.3 mEq/kg if no improvement in 30 minutes. AVOID slow-release — too slow and unpredictable. Monitor K+ q30min. Avoid glucose-containing fluids and corticosteroids.
Aggressive IV repletion causes rebound hyperkalemia
This is NOT true depletion. Aggressive IV KCl risks dangerous rebound hyperkalemia. Weakness may lag behind K+ normalization.

Trigger avoidance (low sodium/carbohydrate diet, avoid steroids/cold/stress/alcohol). Daily oral KCl 10-20 mEq TID (nocturnal dose for morning attacks). Acetazolamide is first-line prevention. If intolerant: K-sparing diuretics (triamterene, spironolactone, eplerenone). Genetic counseling: CACNA1S or SCN4A testing for family planning.

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT use normal saline in hyperkalemia — high chloride content worsens it via renal chloride-bicarbonate exchange.

Do NOT give temporizing measures (calcium, insulin, beta-2 agonist) without a definitive elimination plan already running — K+ will recur in 2-4 hours.

Do NOT use Kayexalate — antiquated, probably ineffective for acute hyperK, and carries a colonic necrosis risk.

Do NOT delay dialysis while waiting for potassium binders (SZC, patiromer) to work — they are only mildly effective adjuncts.

Do NOT give aggressive IV KCl in hypokalemic periodic paralysis — it is transcellular shift, not depletion, and risks dangerous rebound hyperkalemia.

Do NOT rule out dangerous hyperkalemia based on a normal ECG — sensitivity for peaked T-waves is only ~34%.

Do NOT repeat K+ repletion without checking magnesium — up to 50% of hypokalemic patients have concurrent hypomagnesemia that makes repletion refractory.
