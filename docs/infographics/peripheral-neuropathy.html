Peripheral Neuropathy — ED Evaluation

Adults evaluated in the ED for peripheral neuropathy — new, progressive, or previously diagnosed. Covers red-flag recognition, anatomic/fiber-type/tempo classification, standard vs emergent workup, GBS-vs-mimic differentiation, symptomatic and cause-directed treatment, and disposition.

⚠ Steroids are NOT effective in GBS — do not substitute steroids for IVIG or plasmapheresis.
⚠ EMG/NCS may be normal in the first 1-2 weeks of GBS — a normal early study does NOT exclude GBS.
⚠ LP may be normal in the first week of GBS — a normal LP does NOT exclude GBS; recheck if suspicion remains.
⚠ Small fiber neuropathy has normal strength, normal reflexes, and normal EMG/NCS — standard electrodiagnostics test large fibers only and will miss it.
⚠ 25-46% of neuropathies remain idiopathic after a full standard workup (AAFP 2020).
⚠ 20-30-40 Rule for respiratory monitoring: FVC <20 mL/kg, NIF worse than -30 cmH2O, or decline >40% — but note the source consult also separately states '>30% decline from baseline' as the concerning decline threshold in the emergent-workup and GBS-management sections; this is an internal inconsistency in the source tree, not resolved here.

ED role & red-flag screen

Steps Summary

ED Role in Peripheral Neuropathy:
1. Identify emergencies (GBS, cord compression, vasculitis)
2. Rule out dangerous mimics
3. Determine workup urgency
4. Initiate symptomatic treatment

Presentation patterns: symmetric distal (most common, stocking-glove) · asymmetric/multifocal (vasculitis, mononeuritis multiplex) · proximal weakness (GBS, CIDP, myopathy).

Classification — pattern recognition

Classify by:

1. Anatomic distribution: focal (single nerve) · multifocal, asymmetric (vasculitis, DM) · symmetric distal, stocking-glove (most common) · symmetric proximal (GBS, CIDP, myopathy)

2. Fiber type: large fiber (weakness, vibration/proprioception loss, areflexia) · small fiber (pain, temperature loss, autonomic dysfunction) · mixed (most common)

3. Tempo: acute <4 weeks (GBS, toxic, porphyria) · subacute 4-8 weeks (inflammatory, paraneoplastic) · chronic >8 weeks (DM, hereditary, idiopathic)

Mononeuropathy (single nerve): carpal tunnel (median) · ulnar neuropathy (elbow) · peroneal palsy (fibular head) · radiculopathy (dermatomal)

Mononeuritis multiplex (multiple nerves, asymmetric):
• Vasculitis — PAN, RA, granulomatosis with polyangiitis
• Diabetes — diabetic amyotrophy
• Infections — Lyme, leprosy, HIV, HCV
• Sarcoidosis
• Malignancy — direct invasion, paraneoplastic

  • ESR, CRP (inflammation)
  • ANCA, RF, ANA (vasculitis)
  • HIV, Lyme, HCV serologies
  • Glucose/HbA1c
  • Consider nerve biopsy if vasculitis suspected
Urgent neurology referral
Vasculitic neuropathy can progress rapidly and requires immunosuppression.

Small Fiber Neuropathy (SFN) affects Aδ and C fibers — pain/temperature sensation and autonomic function.

Clinical features: burning pain, allodynia, "pins and needles" · length-dependent (feet → hands) · normal strength and reflexes · normal EMG/NCS (tests large fibers only) · autonomic: sweating abnormalities, orthostatic intolerance, GI dysmotility

Common causes: diabetes/prediabetes (most common) · idiopathic (30-50%) · autoimmune (Sjögren, celiac, sarcoid) · infectious (HIV, HCV) · hereditary (Fabry disease, amyloidosis) · toxic (alcohol, chemotherapy)

  • Skin biopsy (intraepidermal nerve fiber density) — gold standard
  • Autonomic testing (QSART, tilt table)
  • Standard EMG/NCS usually normal
  • Initiate symptomatic treatment; outpatient neurology referral
  • Screen for treatable causes (glucose, B12, TSH)

Workup

Tier 1 — order on ALL patients: CBC with differential · comprehensive metabolic panel · fasting glucose or HbA1c · TSH · vitamin B12

Tier 2 — if Tier 1 unrevealing: SPEP with immunofixation · HIV · hepatitis B and C · ESR/CRP · RPR/VDRL

Tier 3 — specialist-guided: anti-ganglioside antibodies (GBS variants) · paraneoplastic panel · genetic testing (CMT, Fabry) · heavy metals (arsenic, lead, thallium)

Timing caveat
GBS: EMG/NCS may be normal in the first 1-2 weeks. Chronic neuropathy: outpatient EMG is acceptable.

GBS features: acute ascending weakness (<4 weeks to nadir) · areflexia or hyporeflexia · symmetric involvement · recent infection (Campylobacter, CMV, EBV) · autonomic instability (HR, BP fluctuations).

GBS mimic workup

Spinal cord lesion: MRI spine with contrast. Look for sensory level on exam.

Cord compression
Cord compression is a surgical emergency.

Botulism: descending paralysis (cranial → limbs) · dilated, poorly reactive pupils · dry mouth, constipation · stool for botulinum toxin · contact public health.

Tick paralysis: ascending paralysis mimics GBS. Look for tick — scalp, hairline, axilla, groin. Rapid reversal (hours) after tick removal. No sensory involvement typically.

Hypokalemia: check K+ — if <2.5, can cause weakness. Resolves with potassium repletion. ECG changes often present.

Porphyria: abdominal pain, psychiatric symptoms · urine porphyrins (spot and 24h) · autonomic instability.

Myasthenia gravis: fatigable weakness · ptosis, diplopia, bulbar symptoms · ice pack test, AChR antibodies. See MG Consult.

GBS — ED management

Suspected GBS — immediate actions
Admit to ICU. Serial respiratory monitoring (FVC/NIF q4h). Neurology consult for IVIG or plasmapheresis. Continuous cardiac monitoring for autonomic instability.

1. Airway/respiratory monitoring: serial FVC and NIF q4h minimum. Intubation thresholds: FVC <20 mL/kg or <1 L · NIF worse than -30 cmH2O · rapid decline (>30% in hours) · bulbar dysfunction with aspiration risk. Early intubation preferred — RSI okay.

2. Autonomic monitoring: continuous cardiac monitoring. Watch for HR/BP lability. Avoid medications that worsen dysautonomia.

ICU admission

ICU admission protocol
Admit to ICU with continuous monitoring. Serial respiratory assessments q4h. Neurology consult for IVIG or PLEX. DVT prophylaxis. Intubation equipment at bedside.
  • Serial FVC/NIF q4h (or more frequently if declining)
  • Continuous cardiac monitoring
  • Frequent neuro checks
  • Swallow evaluation before PO intake
  • DVT prophylaxis (mechanical + pharmacologic)
  • PPI for stress ulcer prophylaxis
  • Pain management
  • Early PT/OT consult
  • Intubation equipment ready — RSI is safe in GBS
  • Avoid succinylcholine (hyperkalemia risk); rocuronium preferred
  • Consults: Neurology (emergent), pulmonology/critical care, consider IR for PLEX access

Floor admission

Floor admission protocol
Admit to floor with fall precautions. Complete workup (labs, consider LP/EMG). Neurology consult. Initiate symptomatic treatment. Clear escalation criteria to nursing.
  • Complete Tier 1 and Tier 2 labs if not done
  • Consider LP if inflammatory cause suspected
  • EMG/NCS during admission
  • MRI if structural lesion suspected
  • Neuro checks q4-6h, fall precautions, PT/OT evaluation
  • Initiate neuropathic pain medication; treat identified deficiencies (B12, thiamine)
  • Consults: neurology (routine or urgent based on acuity), PM&R for rehabilitation planning
Escalation to ICU if
FVC declining · new respiratory symptoms · autonomic instability · progression of weakness.

Discharge — outpatient management

  • No red flags (acute, progressive, motor, autonomic)
  • Able to ambulate safely
  • Chronic/stable symptoms
  • Follow-up arranged
  • Fasting glucose or HbA1c
  • TSH, B12
  • SPEP with immunofixation
  • Consider HIV, Lyme, HCV based on risk

Follow-up: PCP within 1-2 weeks. Neurology referral (routine, 2-4 weeks). EMG/NCS outpatient if indicated. Prescribe symptomatic medication for a short course only — needs follow-up.

Return precautions
Weakness spreading or worsening · difficulty breathing or swallowing · unable to walk · loss of bladder/bowel control · any rapid change in symptoms.

Management algorithm

Classification — pattern recognition — Classify by anatomic distribution, fiber type, and tempo. What is the dominant pattern?

Emergent workup — acute neuropathy — Bedside FVC/NIF, LP (albuminocytologic dissociation), anti-ganglioside antibodies, MRI spine if cord compression suspected, emergent neurology consult, ICU if respiratory compromise.

Standard ED workup — Tier 1 on all patients (CBC, CMP, glucose/HbA1c, TSH, B12); Tier 2 if unrevealing (SPEP, HIV, hepatitis, ESR/CRP, RPR); Tier 3 specialist-guided. 25-46% remain idiopathic.

GBS vs mimics — critical differentiation — GBS: acute ascending weakness, areflexia, symmetric, recent infection, autonomic instability. Exclude transverse myelitis, cord compression, botulism, tick paralysis, hypokalemia, porphyria, MG, critical illness polyneuropathy.

EMG/NCS — when to order? — Consider urgent EMG/NCS for acute/rapidly progressive weakness, motor-predominant symptoms, suspected GBS/CIDP, asymmetric presentation, or normal initial workup with persistent symptoms. May be normal in GBS for the first 1-2 weeks.

GBS mimic workup — Targeted workup by suspected mimic: MRI spine (cord lesion, surgical emergency), stool botulinum toxin (botulism), tick search (tick paralysis), K+ (hypokalemia), urine porphyrins (porphyria), ice pack test/AChR antibodies (MG).

GBS — ED management — Serial FVC/NIF q4h, continuous cardiac monitoring, IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day x5 days OR plasmapheresis (equally effective; steroids NOT effective), DVT prophylaxis, PT/OT early. Disposition: ICU admission.

Symptomatic treatment — neuropathic pain — First-line: gabapentin, pregabalin (similar efficacy). SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine). TCAs (amitriptyline, nortriptyline). Topical (lidocaine, capsaicin). Second-line: tramadol; opioids last resort.

Treat underlying cause — Reversible causes to treat in ED if identified: B12 deficiency, thiamine deficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes/prediabetes glycemic control, toxic exposure removal, inflammatory/autoimmune neurology-directed treatment.

Disposition decision — ICU: respiratory compromise, autonomic instability, GBS, bulbar dysfunction. Floor: unable to ambulate, rapid progression, needs IV treatment, unreliable follow-up. Discharge: stable, ambulatory, follow-up arranged, no red flags.

ICU admission — Continuous monitoring, serial respiratory assessments q4h, neurology consult for IVIG/PLEX, DVT prophylaxis, intubation equipment at bedside.

Floor admission — Fall precautions, complete workup (labs, consider LP/EMG), neurology consult, initiate symptomatic treatment, clear escalation criteria to nursing.

Discharge — outpatient management — No red flags, ambulatory, chronic/stable, follow-up arranged. Start gabapentin or pregabalin. Order outpatient labs. PCP follow-up 1-2 weeks. Neurology referral. Written return precautions.
