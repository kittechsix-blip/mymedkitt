Acute Mesenteric Ischemia — Recognition to Type-Specific Treatment

Adults ≥18 in the ED/ICU with suspected or confirmed acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI). Excludes pediatrics, chronic mesenteric ischemia (chronic postprandial pain without acute presentation), and pregnancy (not separately addressed by the source consult). Weight-based heparin doses assume normal-to-moderate renal function; adjust per institutional nomogram.

⚠ Pain out of proportion to physical exam = assume AMI until proven otherwise (WSES) — do not anchor on a soft, minimally tender abdomen
⚠ Go straight to CTA — do NOT wait for labs. Normal lactate does NOT rule out AMI; lactate rises late, after irreversible ischemia has often begun
⚠ Avoid vasopressors — they worsen mesenteric ischemia. If required: norepinephrine + dobutamine combination, minimized dose/duration. Avoid vasopressin (direct splanchnic vasoconstriction)
⚠ Peritonitis, free air, or CT signs of transmural necrosis (pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas) = immediate laparotomy regardless of type — do not delay for further workup
⚠ Start anticoagulation and antibiotics immediately — do not delay for surgery. Unfractionated heparin can be reversed with protamine if the OR is needed urgently
⚠ Papaverine intra-arterial infusion is chemically incompatible with heparin — do NOT co-infuse in the same line/catheter

1 · Recognition — pain out of proportion

Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is inadequate intestinal blood flow causing bowel ischemia and, if untreated, necrosis. Mortality is 14% if treated within 12 hours vs 80-100% if delayed more than 24 hours. The classic presentation is severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical exam — the abdomen is soft with minimal tenderness despite the patient's distress.[1][2]

Pain out of proportion is the pattern to catch
WSES: severe pain out of proportion to exam findings should be assumed AMI until proven otherwise.[2]
  • AFib or recent MI
  • Age >60 with unexplained severe abdominal pain
  • Known vascular disease (PVD, CAD)
  • Hypercoagulable state
  • ICU patient on vasopressors
  • History of postprandial pain / “food fear”

5 · Type-specific treatment

AMAE — arterial embolism
No peritonitis → endovascular aspiration embolectomy first if expertise available: 88% technical success, 26% in-hospital mortality, 38% still require subsequent laparotomy. Surgical option: Fogarty catheter embolectomy via SMA arteriotomy, observe bowel viability 10-15 minutes post-revascularization. With peritonitis → immediate laparotomy with simultaneous revascularization.[2][4]
AMAT — arterial thrombosis
No peritonitis → endovascular preferred: angioplasty and stenting, 30-day mortality 17% (vs 38% with open surgery). Hybrid retrograde open mesenteric stenting (ROMS) achieves patency rates of 76-88% for complex cases.[2][4]
MVT — venous thrombosis
Primary treatment is anticoagulation — continuous unfractionated heparin infusion; early heparin improves survival. Surgery only if peritonitis develops. Minimum 6 months anticoagulation; lifelong if an underlying hypercoagulable state is identified. Thrombolytics are rarely indicated.[2][4]
NOMI — non-occlusive
Primary treatment is correcting the underlying cause (vasopressors, cardiogenic shock, sepsis) — optimize cardiac output, stop or minimize vasopressors, systemic heparin. Mortality remains 70-90% even with optimal treatment.[2][5]
Papaverine (intra-arterial)
30-60 mg/hr continuous intra-arterial infusion via SMA catheter
Intra-arterial (SMA catheter)
Typically continued ≥ 24 hours and often through resolution of vasospasm / after revascularization. Chemically incompatible with heparin — do NOT co-infuse in the same line/catheter.
Peritonitis or necrosis — OR now
Peritonitis, free air, or CT signs of transmural necrosis (pneumatosis, portal venous gas) = immediate laparotomy regardless of type — do not delay for further workup. Assess bowel viability, resect necrotic segments, revascularize if possible, plan second-look laparotomy at 24-48 hours.[2][4]

6 · Surgical considerations

  • Visual inspection: pink/healthy vs red/edematous vs black/necrotic
  • Peristalsis assessment
  • Doppler flow
  • Fluorescein angiography: 1g IV, Wood lamp (viable bowel fluoresces)

Second-look laparotomy at 24-48 hours if bowel viability was questionable at initial surgery. Additional necrotic segments are resected in 53% of second-looks — do not skip if any viability concern at initial surgery.[2][4]

7 · Disposition & long-term

All patients with confirmed AMI go to ICU. Continue heparin infusion (goal aPTT 40-60 seconds) and broad-spectrum antibiotics for a minimum of 4 days; advance enteral nutrition as tolerated.[2]

30-day mortality is 17-51% depending on treatment approach. Short bowel syndrome is a risk after extensive resection.[2][4]
