Acute Mesenteric Ischemia: Pain Out of Proportion → CTA Now

Adults ≥18 in the ED with suspected acute mesenteric ischemia — severe pain out of proportion to exam, especially with AFib, age >60, known vascular disease, hypercoagulability, or ICU/vasopressor exposure. Excludes chronic mesenteric ischemia, pediatrics, and pregnancy — each needs a modified pathway.

⚠ Pain out of proportion to exam = AMI until proven otherwise. The patient is in extreme distress with a soft, minimally tender abdomen — a normal exam does NOT reassure.
⚠ CTA immediately — do NOT wait for labs. 14% mortality if treated within 12 hours vs 80-100% if delayed beyond 24 hours. Every hour of imaging delay costs bowel.
⚠ Normal lactate does NOT rule out AMI. Lactate rises late, once necrosis has occurred — WSES and ESVS recommend against any single biomarker to include or exclude AMI.
⚠ Peritonitis, free air, pneumatosis, or instability = immediate laparotomy regardless of type. Do not delay the OR for CTA.
⚠ Avoid vasopressors — they worsen mesenteric ischemia. If unavoidable, use norepinephrine + dobutamine and minimize dose; avoid vasopressin (direct splanchnic vasoconstriction).

2 · Diagnose — CTA now, do NOT wait for labs

Biphasic CTA is the gold standard
Sensitivity 93-100%, specificity 94-100%. Do NOT wait for labs — go straight to CTA. Arterial phase shows SMA/celiac occlusion and emboli; venous phase shows MVT and bowel-wall enhancement.[2][4]
  • Filling defect in the SMA
  • Bowel-wall thickening >3 mm or absent wall enhancement
  • 'Target sign' / halo appearance
  • Mesenteric fat stranding

Order labs but do not delay imaging for them. Lactate >2 suggests irreversible ischemia (HR 4.1); lactate >3.8 mmol/L predicts mortality. Leukocytosis is >90% sensitive but nonspecific. Normal lactate does NOT rule out AMI — it rises late.[1][3]

CT signs of necrosis → OR, not more workup
Pneumatosis intestinalis (spec 97-100%) plus portal venous gas (spec 94-100%) indicate transmural necrosis in 78-81% of cases. Free intraperitoneal air = perforation. This is a surgical emergency — go directly to the OR.[3]
