Calcium Channel Blocker Overdose: Match the Shock Phenotype

Adults in the ED/ICU with known or suspected calcium channel blocker overdose. Excludes pediatrics (weight-based doses shown are adult-scaled), pregnancy, and mixed toxidromes requiring a different antidote pathway.

⚠ Insulin 1 U/kg bolus then 1-10 U/kg/hr — maintain glucose 150-250, replete K+ to 4.0-4.5
⚠ High-dose insulin euglycemia therapy (HIET): 1 unit/kg bolus, then 1-10 units/kg/hr infusion
⚠ Calcium chloride 1-2g IV q15-20min (or continuous infusion) to overcome channel blockade
⚠ Vasopressors at HIGH doses (norepinephrine 0.5-3 mcg/kg/min) for vasodilatory shock
⚠ Whole bowel irrigation (GoLYTELY 2L/hr) for ALL extended-release CCB ingestions
⚠ VA-ECMO bridges reversible CCB toxicity — contact ECMO team EARLY, full recovery possible

1 · Recognize & classify the class

The drug class predicts the shock
Calcium channel blockers are among the most lethal cardiovascular overdoses. The class tells you which shock to expect and which antidote leads.[1][3]
ClassExamplesDominant effectShock phenotypeAntidote lead
Non-dihydropyridineverapamil, diltiazemSA/AV node suppression, negative inotropyCARDIOGENIC (cool, bradycardic)HIET + inotropes
Dihydropyridineamlodipine, nifedipine, felodipinePeripheral vasodilation, reflex tachycardiaVASODILATORY (warm, tachycardic)High-dose vasopressors

Verapamil is the most cardiotoxic and causes the most deaths. Amlodipine is the most dangerous dihydropyridine — 30–50 h half-life and inherently long-acting.[3]

Extended-release deceives you
Extended-release CCBs (verapamil SR/ER, diltiazem CD/XR, nifedipine XL) can look well early, then crash 12–24 h later. Toggle Extended-release above to surface the decontamination step.
Extended-release — act now
Whole bowel irrigation (GoLYTELY 2L/hr) for ALL extended-release CCB ingestions until clear rectal effluent. Admit for a 24–48 h minimum ICU watch even if currently stable.[1]

Play: stabilize & decontaminate
No shock flagged yet. Two large-bore IVs, continuous monitor, 12-lead ECG, BMP + lactate + glucose. Give calcium as a temporizing agent and start glucagon as a bridge. Reassess the phenotype every few minutes.
Play: HIET is first-line
Cardiogenic shock → high-dose insulin euglycemia therapy leads. Insulin 1 U/kg bolus then 1-10 U/kg/hr, maintain glucose 150–250, replete K+ to 4.0–4.5. Add an inotrope (epinephrine) if CO stays poor.[2][4]
Play: high-dose vasopressors lead
Vasodilatory shock → norepinephrine 0.1–3 mcg/kg/min, titrate aggressively, add vasopressin 0.04 units/min. CCB toxicity blunts catecholamine response — expect to go far higher than in sepsis.[1][2]
Play: rescue therapies + call ECMO
Refractory to calcium + HIET + max pressors → escalate to lipid emulsion and methylene blue (for vasoplegia), and contact the VA-ECMO team now. CCB toxicity is reversible — bridge the patient through it.[1][5]

Precedence: refractory overrides everything; otherwise cardiogenic takes the HIET play and vasodilatory takes the pressor play. If both shock types are flagged, treat both components — mixed shock is common.

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT rely on atropine or pacing.** The block is at the calcium channel, not the vagus — both usually fail to restore hemodynamics.

**Do NOT underdose or stop HIET early.** Onset takes 15-60 min; titrate up to 10 units/kg/hr and supplement dextrose to avoid hypoglycemia.

**Do NOT be reassured by an extended-release patient who looks well.** Toxicity peaks 12-24 h out — admit and irrigate.

**Do NOT abandon vasopressors at conventional ceilings.** CCB toxicity needs far higher doses than sepsis — escalate and add a second agent.
