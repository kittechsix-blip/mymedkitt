Pre-Intubation RV Assessment

Emergency/critical-care clinicians performing a pre-intubation assessment for right ventricular (RV) dysfunction before intubating a patient at risk of peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse — massive/submassive PE, pulmonary hypertension, RV infarction, severe COPD/ARDS, or cardiomyopathy with RV involvement.

⚠ RV dysfunction patients are at extreme risk of peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse — if clinical signs of RV failure are present, perform POCUS before intubation
⚠ Do NOT intubate until BP is optimized if the patient is already hypotensive — start vasopressors first, then achieve targets, then intubate
⚠ AVOID phenylephrine — pure alpha agonist, increases pulmonary vascular resistance and worsens RV afterload, can precipitate RV failure
⚠ Do NOT aggressively bag post-intubation — each squeeze increases intrathoracic pressure and worsens the RV death spiral
⚠ IVC assessment is unreliable during PPV, intra-abdominal hypertension, and heart failure — use only in context

Why assess RV before intubation

Sedation causes vasodilation (↓ preload); positive pressure ventilation raises intrathoracic pressure (↓ venous return) and pulmonary vascular resistance (↑ RV afterload); hypoxia/hypercapnia during intubation causes pulmonary vasoconstriction. The thin-walled RV cannot cope — triggering an RV death spiral and cardiac arrest.

  • Massive/submassive PE
  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • RV infarction
  • Severe COPD/ARDS
  • Cardiomyopathy with RV involvement
Extreme peri-intubation risk
A significant number of peri-intubation arrests occur in unrecognized RV failure.
  1. PPV increases RV afterload — ↑ intrathoracic pressure → ↑ pulmonary vascular resistance
  2. RV dilates — thin-walled RV can't generate enough pressure, volume backs up
  3. Septal shift (D-sign) — dilated RV pushes septum into LV, LV becomes "D-shaped"
  4. LV stroke volume falls — less LV filling → less cardiac output → hypotension
  5. RV ischemia — RV is perfused when SBP > PASP; once SBP drops below PA pressure, RV becomes ischemic
  6. Cardiac arrest — often PEA; CPR is often futile because the obstructive physiology isn't fixed

POCUS assessment (60-90 seconds)

  1. Subcostal — RV:LV ratio, wall thickness, IVC
  2. Apical 4-Chamber (A4C) — RV:LV ratio, TAPSE, McConnell's sign
  3. Parasternal Short Axis (PSAX) — D-sign (septal flattening)
Red flags on POCUS
RV > LV; TAPSE <10mm; severe D-sign; McConnell's sign (acute PE).

A4C probe position: 4th-5th intercostal space, left lateral chest, left lateral decubitus, marker toward 3-5 o'clock.

RV:LV ratio (eyeball): normal RV is 2/3 or less of LV size; RV > LV = severe dilation.

TAPSE: M-mode cursor through the lateral tricuspid annulus, measure vertical excursion during systole.

McConnell's sign: RV free wall akinesis with apical sparing ("trampoline") — sensitivity 22%, specificity 97% for acute PE.

PSAX probe position: left parasternal border, 3rd-4th intercostal space, rotate 90° from PLAX (marker toward left shoulder), level of papillary muscles.

Normal: LV circular, septum curves toward RV, RV crescent-shaped and smaller than LV.

D-sign: septum flattens or bows into the LV — LV becomes "D-shaped" instead of "O-shaped".

RV:LV <0.6, TAPSE >17mm, no D-sign, normal wall thickness. When to choose awake intubation
Severe RV dysfunction (RV>LV, TAPSE <10mm), known severe pulmonary hypertension, previous peri-intubation arrest/near-arrest, or a cooperative patient with a reasonable airway.

Post-intubation management

The critical 15-30 minutes
Patients "insidiously slip into an RV death spiral" in this window. Do NOT bag aggressively — each squeeze increases intrathoracic pressure. Connect to the ventilator immediately, start the inhaled pulmonary vasodilator at maximal dose, and titrate vasopressors to targets.

  • Confirm optimization: SBP >120, MAP ~80, vasopressors running or push-dose ready, pulmonary vasodilator at bedside
  • Pre-treat with push-dose epi (10-20 mcg) + vasopressin (1-2 units)
  • Induce: ketamine or etomidate, then rocuronium
  • Maintain oxygenation via BiPAP apneic ventilation and HFNC during laryngoscopy
  • Intubate rapidly — minimize apnea time
  • Connect to ventilator immediately — do NOT aggressively bag
  • Start inhaled pulmonary vasodilator at maximal dose
  • Titrate vasopressors to maintain targets
  • Sedation: ketamine 0.5-1 mg/kg dissociative dose (maintains respiratory drive), may repeat 0.25-0.5 mg/kg PRN
  • Topical anesthesia: atomized lidocaine to oropharynx/hypopharynx; consider glossopharyngeal/SLN nerve blocks
  • Video laryngoscopy — pass the tube while the patient is still breathing, confirm placement
  • Sedate fully after tube confirmation if needed
  • Have a backup plan — if awake attempt fails, convert to RSI

Severe RV dysfunction (RV>LV, TAPSE <10mm), known severe pulmonary hypertension, previous peri-intubation arrest/near-arrest, or a cooperative patient with a reasonable airway.

Post-intubation management

Patients "insidiously slip into an RV death spiral" in this window. Do NOT bag aggressively — each squeeze increases intrathoracic pressure. Connect to the ventilator immediately, start the inhaled pulmonary vasodilator at maximal dose, and titrate vasopressors to targets.

Parameter | Target | Rationale

Vt | 6-8 mL/kg IBW | Avoid overdistension

Plateau pressure | <30 cm H₂O | Minimize PVR increase

PEEP | Adequate (avoid atelectasis) | Recruits lung

FiO₂ | Generous | Avoid hypoxia (pulmonary vasoconstrictor)

Minute ventilation | Avoid hypercapnia | CO₂ is a pulmonary vasoconstrictor

Declining BP (need more vasopressor); rising CVP (worsening RV function); declining SpO₂; falling EtCO₂ (falling cardiac output).

Parameter | Target

MAP | >65 mmHg minimum; higher if CVP elevated

SBP | >> PASP (maintain RV coronary perfusion)

CVP | 8-12 mmHg (moderate filling)

Perfusion pressure | MAP > (60 + CVP)

[Dobutamine](#/drug/dobutamine/septic shock inotrope): Start 2 mcg/kg/min, titrate to 10 mcg/kg/min max IV continuous infusion — Easy to titrate; watch for hypotension — have a vasopressor ready. Epinephrine is an alternative if inotropy + vasopressor both needed; milrinone if tolerated (avoid in severe hypotension).

Reassess RV and IVC by POCUS. Generally diurese in chronic RV failure — avoid large-volume resuscitation. Small boluses (250 mL) only if clearly hypovolemic.

  • Cardiac index <2.2 L/min/m²
  • PA pulsatility index (PAPI) <1.0
  • Cardiac power output <0.6 W
  • Refractory shock despite optimization

VA-ECMO provides circulatory support and allows RV rest as a bridge to recovery or definitive therapy (catheter-directed therapy, surgical embolectomy for PE). Don't wait until arrest — patients who arrest with RV failure have very poor outcomes with conventional CPR due to obstructive physiology; better outcomes come with planned cannulation.
