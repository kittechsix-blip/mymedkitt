Acute Diarrhea — ED Evaluation and Management

Adults and children presenting to the ED with acute diarrhea (≤14 days). Covers risk stratification, C. difficile, traveler's diarrhea, empiric antibiotics, and special populations (pregnancy, pediatric, elderly). Excludes chronic diarrhea (>30 days), which exceeds ED scope and requires GI referral.

⚠ STEC suspected (bloody, afebrile diarrhea + undercooked beef/unpasteurized product exposure) — do NOT give antibiotics (increases HUS risk) and do NOT give antimotility agents (delays pathogen clearance).
⚠ Bloody diarrhea / dysentery — mandatory workup before empiric treatment: stool culture + CBC with platelets (HUS screen) + BMP.
⚠ AVOID antimotility agents (loperamide, bismuth subsalicylate) with bloody diarrhea, high fever, suspected inflammatory cause, or immunocompromised patients — risk of toxic megacolon and delayed pathogen clearance.
⚠ C. difficile fulminant disease (hypotension, ileus, megacolon) requires vancomycin 500 mg PO/NG QID PLUS metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h AND emergent surgical consult — colectomy may be lifesaving.
⚠ Pediatric STEC/HUS — most common cause of acute kidney failure in children. Do NOT give antibiotics or antimotility agents if STEC suspected; monitor platelet count, hemolytic anemia, renal function.
⚠ Pregnancy — AVOID fluoroquinolones (cartilage toxicity) and TMP-SMX (folate antagonism, teratogenic risk especially first trimester and near term). AVOID diphenoxylate (CNS effects) and bismuth subsalicylate (contains salicylates).
⚠ Surgical abdomen can present as diarrhea — severe pain out of proportion to exam, or age >50 with vascular risk factors, warrants CT abdomen/pelvis and surgical consult BEFORE treating as gastroenteritis.

1 · Initial assessment

Diarrhea is a change in bowel movements with increased water content, volume, or frequency (>200 g/day or >3 stools/day). Most cases are self-limited, but serious disease can present with diarrhea as the chief complaint.

Definitions: Acute ≤14 days · Persistent 14-30 days · Chronic >30 days.

Classification: Secretory (does not stop with fasting, e.g. cholera) · Osmotic (stops with fasting) · Inflammatory (bloody, fever, cramps) · Non-inflammatory (watery, nausea/vomiting).

  • ABCs — secure airway if altered mental status
  • 2 large-bore IVs — NS or LR bolus 20 mL/kg
  • Cardiac monitor — assess for arrhythmia from electrolyte derangement
  • POC glucose — hypoglycemia common in pediatric patients
  • Assess for sepsis: fever + tachycardia + hypotension → septic diarrhea until proven otherwise (lactate, blood cultures)
  • Labs: CBC, BMP, lactate, blood cultures, type & screen if severe anemia suspected
—IV bolus (NS/LR, 20 mL/kg)
  • Stool character: frequency, volume, blood, mucus, melena — ask specifically, patients may not recognize black stool as blood
  • Duration: <48h likely viral; >48h raises suspicion for bacterial cause
  • Associated symptoms: nausea/vomiting (viral), fever >38.5°C (invasive bacteria), tenesmus
  • Travel: recent foreign travel, lake/stream exposure
  • Diet: raw/undercooked meat, seafood, eggs, unpasteurized milk/juice
  • Medications: antibiotics in past 3 months (C. difficile risk)
  • Contacts: ill contacts, day care, nursing home, food handlers
  • PMH: immunocompromised, IBD, diabetes
Red flags
Severe dehydration or hemodynamic instability · bloody diarrhea or dysentery · fever >38.5°C (101.3°F) with systemic illness · immunocompromised · severe abdominal pain (surgical etiology concern) · elderly with comorbidities · neurologic involvement.

5 · Diagnostics

Selective testing — routine labs are NOT indicated for most patients. Fecal leukocytes/lactoferrin: community-acquired, traveler's, nosocomial, or diarrhea >7 days. Stool culture: bloody stool, fever >38.5°C + positive fecal markers, immunocompromised, severe/persistent illness (yield 1.5-5.6%, but identifies public health threats). C. difficile toxin: antibiotic use past 3 months. Ova & parasites: rarely indicated acutely — consider if >14 days, immunocompromised, or no antibiotic response. CBC/BMP: severe illness, bloody diarrhea (HUS screen), elderly, immunocompromised.

Most common cause: enterotoxigenic E. coli. Southeast Asia: >80% fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter — use azithromycin instead of ciprofloxacin. Combination therapy (antibiotic + loperamide) is most efficacious.

9 · Special populations & disposition

Reachable from any treatment endpoint in the source app via a manual jump link (not a sequential next-step). Toggle Special population above to view pregnancy, pediatric, or elderly-specific guidance.

Diarrhea in pregnancy
Dehydration is dangerous for both mother and fetus — aggressive rehydration is top priority. Loperamide acts peripherally (FDA Category B) — safest antimotility option. AVOID diphenoxylate (CNS effects) and bismuth subsalicylate (contains salicylates). Metronidazole 500 mg PO TID × 7-10 days — Category B after first trimester, if benefits outweigh risks; coordinate with OB/GYN. AVOID fluoroquinolones (cartilage toxicity) and TMP-SMX (folate antagonism, teratogenic risk). Low threshold for admission if unable to maintain oral hydration.
Pediatric diarrhea
Rotavirus is the most common cause. ORS is preferred over IV (WHO-endorsed) — use commercial ORS (Pedialyte), NOT sports drinks or plain water. No antimotility agents. No routine antiemetics except ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg IV/ODT (max 4 mg) to facilitate oral rehydration. STEC/HUS is the most common cause of acute pediatric kidney failure — do NOT give antibiotics if suspected; monitor platelets, hemolytic anemia, renal function. Admit if: severe dehydration, altered mental status, unable to hydrate orally, suspected HUS, or very young (<3 months).
Ondansetron
0.15 mg/kg IV or ODT
Max 4 mg. Single dose often sufficient to facilitate oral rehydration.
Diarrhea in the elderly
Higher risk of serious etiologies: ischemic colitis, C. difficile (more lethal), diverticulitis, colonic malignancy, EHEC. Dehydration risk is higher (disordered thirst, diuretics, limited fluid access). IV rehydration caution with cardiac/renal disease. Fluoroquinolones increase theophylline/warfarin levels, can alter phenytoin levels. Lower threshold for CT abdomen, admission, and surgical consultation.

Vancomycin: 125 mg PO QID × 10 days — Alternative if fidaxomicin unavailable, non-severe and severe disease.

Vancomycin (fulminant): 500 mg PO/NG QID — Fulminant disease — combine with IV metronidazole. Rectal vancomycin enema if ileus present.

Metronidazole (fulminant): 500 mg IV q8h — Added to vancomycin for fulminant C. difficile. Surgical consult — colectomy may be lifesaving.

Admit severe and fulminant cases. Non-severe may be discharged with close follow-up. Metronidazole alone is NOT recommended for severe disease.

7 · Traveler's diarrhea treatment

Most common cause: enterotoxigenic E. coli. Southeast Asia: >80% fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter — use azithromycin instead of ciprofloxacin. Combination therapy (antibiotic + loperamide) is most efficacious.

Loperamide: 4 mg PO initially, then 2 mg after each loose stool — Max 16 mg/day. Faster stool frequency reduction than bismuth.

Bismuth Subsalicylate: 524 mg PO q30-60 min PRN — Max 8 doses/day. Additional antiemetic properties. Antibiotics not typically needed for mild traveler's diarrhea.

Ciprofloxacin: 500 mg PO BID × 3 days — First-line for most regions. Add loperamide as adjunct (reduces duration by 1 day). Avoid if bloody diarrhea or high fever.

Azithromycin: 1000 mg PO × 1 dose, or 500 mg PO daily × 3 days — Use for Southeast Asia travel (>80% fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter).

TMP-SMX (children): 8 mg/kg/day (TMP component), divided BID — Max 320 mg TMP/day. Indication-hint mismatch note: myMedKitt links this drug for traveler's diarrhea, but the weight-based dosing is filed under the drug-store's "Pediatric diarrhea" entry — used the correct, more specific value here.

8 · Community-acquired treatment

Rehydration is the cornerstone of management. Oral rehydration is preferred when tolerated — commercial ORS (Pedialyte) is preferred over sports drinks (inadequate sodium replacement). BRAT diet has no proven advantage over resuming a normal diet.

Loperamide: 4 mg PO initially, then 2 mg after each loose stool — Max 16 mg/day. Preferred antimotility agent — no CNS effects, not habit-forming. AVOID with bloody diarrhea, high fever, or inflammatory cause.

Bismuth Subsalicylate: 524 mg PO q30-60 min PRN — Max 8 doses/day. Preferred when nausea/vomiting is prominent.

Ondansetron: 4 mg IV or ODT — For vomiting preventing oral intake.

IV rehydration — NS or LR bolus, reassess after each liter (urine output, vitals, oral tolerance). Assess for empiric antibiotic criteria: diarrhea >48 hours, fever >38.5°C + positive fecal markers, or acute dysentery.

Ondansetron: 4 mg IV q4-6h PRN — For vomiting preventing oral intake.

Loperamide: 4 mg PO initially, then 2 mg PRN — Max 16 mg/day. AVOID if bloody diarrhea, high fever, or suspected inflammatory cause.

Ciprofloxacin: 500 mg PO BID × 3-5 days — Adults. Elderly: monitor theophylline/warfarin/phenytoin interactions. Do NOT use if STEC suspected.

TMP-SMX (children): 8 mg/kg/day (TMP component), divided BID — Max 320 mg TMP/day.

Metronidazole: 250 mg PO TID × 7-10 days — For persistent diarrhea (2-4 weeks) without systemic symptoms. Covers Giardia. Disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.

Do NOT give antibiotics if STEC (E. coli O157:H7) suspected — increases HUS risk.

9 · Special populations & disposition

Reachable from any treatment endpoint in the source app via a manual jump link (not a sequential next-step). Toggle Special population above to view pregnancy, pediatric, or elderly-specific guidance.

Dehydration is dangerous for both mother and fetus — aggressive rehydration is top priority. Loperamide acts peripherally (FDA Category B) — safest antimotility option. AVOID diphenoxylate (CNS effects) and bismuth subsalicylate (contains salicylates). Metronidazole 500 mg PO TID × 7-10 days — Category B after first trimester, if benefits outweigh risks; coordinate with OB/GYN. AVOID fluoroquinolones (cartilage toxicity) and TMP-SMX (folate antagonism, teratogenic risk). Low threshold for admission if unable to maintain oral hydration.

Rotavirus is the most common cause. ORS is preferred over IV (WHO-endorsed) — use commercial ORS (Pedialyte), NOT sports drinks or plain water. No antimotility agents. No routine antiemetics except ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg IV/ODT (max 4 mg) to facilitate oral rehydration. STEC/HUS is the most common cause of acute pediatric kidney failure — do NOT give antibiotics if suspected; monitor platelets, hemolytic anemia, renal function. Admit if: severe dehydration, altered mental status, unable to hydrate orally, suspected HUS, or very young (<3 months).

Ondansetron: 0.15 mg/kg IV or ODT — Max 4 mg. Single dose often sufficient to facilitate oral rehydration.

Higher risk of serious etiologies: ischemic colitis, C. difficile (more lethal), diverticulitis, colonic malignancy, EHEC. Dehydration risk is higher (disordered thirst, diuretics, limited fluid access). IV rehydration caution with cardiac/renal disease. Fluoroquinolones increase theophylline/warfarin levels, can alter phenytoin levels. Lower threshold for CT abdomen, admission, and surgical consultation.

Discharge if | Admit/observe if

Hemodynamically stable | Failed ED rehydration or unable to tolerate oral fluids

Benign abdominal examination | Severe or persistent dehydration

Tolerating oral fluids without vomiting | Diagnostic dilemma (atypical presentation)

Adequate urine output | Extremes of age with comorbidities

Low risk for complications | Immunocompromised

Reliable follow-up, understands return precautions | Possible surgical etiology or significant electrolyte derangement

Usually exceeds ED scope. Exclude serious illness, ensure stability, begin infectious workup, refer to gastroenterology.
