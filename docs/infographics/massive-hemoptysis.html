Massive Hemoptysis — ABCDE Approach

Adults with life-threatening hemoptysis (volume causing airway obstruction, respiratory failure, or hemodynamic instability — classic threshold >=600 mL/24h, or >=300 mL expectorated as the best mortality predictor). ED/ICU setting. Excludes minor/self-limited hemoptysis not meeting these criteria.

⚠ Death in massive hemoptysis is from asphyxiation (drowning), NOT exsanguination — the adult tracheobronchial tree holds only 150-200 mL.
⚠ Arrest / peri-arrest: intubate with the largest ETT available (>=8.5 mm ID) -> if bleeding side known, advance ETT into the contralateral mainstem bronchus -> suction aggressively -> standard ACLS. Treat the underlying cause — this is a potentially reversible etiology.
⚠ Position the bleeding side DOWN as soon as it is lateralized — gravity protects the unaffected lung from spillage. Up to 35% plasma volume can extravasate during severe hemoptysis; lay flat only after hemorrhage control or intubation.
⚠ Avoid double-lumen ETT for emergent airway control — difficult to place emergently, narrow lumens clog with blood, and a therapeutic bronchoscope cannot pass through it.
⚠ Avoid cough suppressants — retained blood in the airway causes obstruction.
⚠ Topical/bronchoscopic epinephrine carries a reported risk of fatal arrhythmia — use cautiously and only via bronchoscopic instillation.
⚠ Vitamin K MUST be given WITH PCC/FFP for warfarin reversal — PCC/FFP effect lasts ~8 hours; Vitamin K takes 6-12 hours to work, so omitting it risks INR/bleeding rebound.

Recognition

Life-threatening hemoptysis = any volume causing airway obstruction, respiratory failure, or hemodynamic instability. Classic threshold >=600 mL/24h; alternative >=200 mL/single episode OR >=100 mL/hr; best mortality predictor >=300 mL expectorated. Adult tracheobronchial space is only 150-200 mL — even "small" volumes can cause asphyxiation.

Etiology by regionUS/developed vs worldwide causes, source vessel
Cause (US/developed)Frequency
Bronchiectasis20-30%
Bronchogenic CA20-25%
Chronic bronchitis15-20%
Pneumonia10-15%
Mycetoma/aspergilloma5-10%

Worldwide, TB is the most common cause (7-85% by region). Other causes: vascular (PE, pulmonary AVM, Rasmussen aneurysm), iatrogenic (post-biopsy, PA catheter perforation), autoimmune (Goodpasture, GPA, DAH), cardiac (mitral stenosis). Source: ~90% from bronchial arteries (systemic pressure), 5% pulmonary, 5% collaterals.

Initial Assessment (all patients)

  1. Supplemental O2 (target SpO2 >92%)
  2. Large-bore IV x2
  3. Type & crossmatch
  4. STAT labs: CBC, BMP, coags (INR, PTT, fibrinogen), ABG
  5. Review medications (anticoagulants, antiplatelets)
  6. STAT portable CXR — may lateralize bleeding (~50% sensitivity)

CT chest with IV contrast (bronchial artery protocol preferred) — identifies source, etiology, and bronchial artery anatomy for IR. While awaiting CT: continuous SpO2 monitoring, airway equipment ready, position patient if side known, start Tranexamic Acid empirically. Low threshold to escalate — edema/clot can compromise the airway rapidly.

Airway (A = Airway, B = Block)

Bleeding side DOWN
Lateral decubitus with the bleeding lung DOWN uses gravity to prevent spillage into the unaffected lung. Apply as soon as bleeding is lateralized; prioritize lung protection over V/Q matching. If side unknown: upright or semi-recumbent to minimize aspiration.

  • Worsening hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% despite O2)
  • Altered mental status
  • Hemodynamic instability
  • Procedural airway control needed

Large ETT facilitates bronchoscopy, endobronchial blocker placement, and suctioning. Avoid double-lumen ETT — difficult emergent placement, narrow lumens clog with blood, cannot pass a therapeutic bronchoscope; only 7% of physicians advocate for initial DLT.

Lung isolation: mainstem intubation (advance ETT into the unaffected bronchus, bougie with 1/4 turn technique, right mainstem easier to cannulate) OR endobronchial blocker (Arndt, Cohen, EZ-Blocker, Fuji Uniblocker — can isolate a lobar/segmental bronchus, temporizing for 48-72 hours). Confirm position with fiberoptic bronchoscopy.

Arrest / peri-arrest management
1) Intubate with large ETT (>=8.5mm). 2) If side known: advance ETT into contralateral mainstem bronchus. 3) Suction aggressively. 4) Standard ACLS resuscitation. Options if available: emergent rigid bronchoscopy, VA-ECMO for refractory arrest from a reversible cause, emergent thoracotomy (massive trauma with hemothorax source). Treat the underlying cause — this is a potentially reversible etiology.

Definitive Therapy (D = Definitive)

Flexible bronchoscopy: localizes bleeding (93% success), can be done at bedside/ICU, but limited suction and blood obscures view. Rigid bronchoscopy: superior suction/visualization, therapeutic (packing, APC, laser), requires OR + general anesthesia. Flexible first for localization; rigid if therapeutic intervention needed or massive bleeding.

First-line definitive therapy for most patients. Technical success 92-97%, clinical success 70-93%, recurrence 10-58% (varies by etiology), major complication rate 0.1%. Embolization materials: PVA particles (355-500 micron, CIRSE recommended), N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA, lowest recurrence), coils, Gelfoam. Highest recurrence risk: aspergilloma/mycetoma, TB, bronchiectasis, non-bronchial collateral supply.

  • BAE technical failure
  • Recurrent hemoptysis despite multiple BAEs
  • Life-threatening bleeding precluding safe BAE
  • Localized, resectable lesion with adequate pulmonary reserve
Surgical intervention
Best candidates: previously healthy, localized disease (aspergilloma, broncholith, abscess), adequate FEV1. Poor candidates: diffuse lung disease, advanced malignancy, bilateral disease. Procedures: lobectomy (preferred) > bi-lobectomy > pneumonectomy. Emergency surgery mortality 10-40%; pneumonectomy carries 2x the mortality of lobectomy. Consult CT surgery EARLY — even if BAE is planned, surgery may be needed as backup.

Disposition

All massive/life-threatening hemoptysis requires ICU. Indications: any massive hemoptysis, ongoing bleeding despite initial treatment, respiratory failure or intubation, hemodynamic instability, post-BAE or bronchoscopy monitoring, active coagulopathy reversal. Consults: Interventional Radiology (BAE), Interventional Pulmonology, CT Surgery (backup for BAE failure).

Transfer considerations (not on the sequential path)When to transfer, transfer essentials checklist

Transfer if your facility lacks: interventional radiology with BAE capability, interventional pulmonology, cardiothoracic surgery, or ICU with ventilator capability. Stabilize and transfer EARLY — do not delay for diagnostic workup.

Transfer essentials: large-bore IV access (x2), intubated with large ETT if airway threatened, bleeding-side-down positioning, TXA started, type & screen/crossmatch completed.

Massive hemoptysis recognized — Death is from asphyxiation, not exsanguination — adult airway holds only 150-200 mL.

Etiology review (bronchiectasis, CA, TB, vascular) — ~90% of bleeding is from bronchial arteries at systemic pressure.
