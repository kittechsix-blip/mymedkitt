Angioedema — Airway Triage, Mechanism, and Etiology-Specific Treatment

Adults and children presenting to the ED with angioedema (face/lip/tongue/pharyngeal/laryngeal/abdominal swelling). Covers histamine-mediated (allergic/anaphylaxis-spectrum) and bradykinin-mediated (ACEi, hereditary, acquired, post-tPA, idiopathic) mechanisms. Excludes isolated urticaria without angioedema and airway obstruction from causes other than angioedema.

⚠ Secure the airway now for stridor, hypoxemia, secretion intolerance/drooling, rapidly progressive tongue/floor-of-mouth swelling, altered mental status, exhaustion, confirmed laryngeal edema, or an AJEM score >7 — dual setup (intubation + cricothyrotomy) with the most experienced operator; do NOT paralyze until the airway anatomy is visualized and a rescue plan is ready.
⚠ Do NOT rely on NIPPV (CPAP/BiPAP) for airway-threatened angioedema — it does not treat a supraglottic/anatomic obstruction and can delay definitive airway control.
⚠ ACE-inhibitor angioedema is NOT allergic — epinephrine, antihistamines, and corticosteroids are generally ineffective. Stop the ACE inhibitor immediately and permanently; up to 30% of ED angioedema visits are ACEi-related, and the risk is 5× higher in Black patients.
⚠ Bradykinin-mediated angioedema (ACEi, HAE, acquired, post-tPA) does not respond to epinephrine, antihistamines, or steroids — treat the mechanism, not the histamine pathway.
⚠ Post-tPA angioedema: AVOID tranexamic acid — contraindicated in acute ischemic stroke (thrombotic risk with ongoing cerebral ischemia).
⚠ Stage 4 (laryngeal) angioedema: 67% probability of airway intervention, 17% require ICU without intervention, 0% are discharged from the ED — ICU admission is required.

What to do now

Anaphylaxis features present — treat as anaphylaxis
Epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg IM q5-20min (up to 3 doses) is first-line. Add H1/H2 blockers and steroid as adjuncts only — never delay epinephrine for them. See the anaphylaxis protocol below.[3][4]
Post-tPA angioedema — AVOID tranexamic acid
This is bradykinin-mediated (kinins rise with tPA) — epinephrine/antihistamines/steroids are not the mechanism-directed therapy. Do NOT give tranexamic acid — contraindicated in acute ischemic stroke. Consider icatibant 30 mg SQ, Berinert 20 units/kg IV, or FFP 2 units if targeted therapy is unavailable; weigh stopping the tPA infusion against stroke severity.[5]
ACE inhibitor use — stop the drug, this is not allergic
Stop the ACE inhibitor immediately and permanently — this is 5-fold higher risk in Black patients (5% vs 0.5%). Epinephrine, antihistamines, and corticosteroids are generally ineffective. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV load then 1 g over 8 hours is a reasonable first targeted option; icatibant, FFP, or Berinert are alternatives. Observe minimum 4-6 hours.[5][20][21][22]

Risk bands: score <5 low risk (~5% intubation), score 5-7 moderate risk (~16% intubation), score >7 high risk (~68% intubation). Anatomic risk still matters — Ishoo stage 1 lip/face is low-risk; stage 3 tongue/floor-of-mouth needs admission-level monitoring; stage 4 laryngeal involvement is ICU/airway territory.[7][8][26]

Dual setup: intubation + cricothyrotomy ready simultaneously. Call the most experienced airway operator early. Notify anesthesia/ENT/trauma airway when available. Keep the patient upright and spontaneously breathing if oxygenation allows. Preferred approach is awake flexible endoscopic or awake video-assisted technique with topical lidocaine and surgical airway backup. Do NOT paralyze until the airway anatomy is visualized and a viable rescue plan is ready unless the patient is crashing. Nasotracheal route may bypass lingual swelling — NEAR angioedema data reported higher first-attempt success with nasotracheal than orotracheal intubation. Extubation: wait for visible improvement, extubate over an airway exchange catheter, confirm laryngeal edema resolution by video laryngoscopy/flexible scope.

Escalate for voice progression, drooling, dyspnea, stridor, hypoxemia, worsening tongue/floor-of-mouth swelling, or score rising above 7.[5][7][8][26][27]

When etiology is unclear, assume histamine-mediated (most common, 40-70%) and start empiric treatment: Epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg IM + Diphenhydramine 25-50 mg IV. Reassess in 30-60 minutes — response confirms histamine-mediated; no response suggests bradykinin-mediated.[3][5]

Bradykinin-mediated angioedema is NOT an allergic reaction — epinephrine, antihistamines, and corticosteroids are generally INEFFECTIVE. Key history: ACEi/ARB use, family history, prior similar episodes, recent tPA, age of first episode, lymphoproliferative disorder.[3][5][20]

Recurrent angioedema without urticaria, no identified cause despite thorough evaluation. Two subtypes by treatment response: histaminergic — responds to high-dose antihistamines (up to 4× standard), leukotriene antagonists, and/or steroids; non-histaminergic — refractory to antihistamines, consider Tranexamic Acid (often effective, especially as suppressive therapy) or icatibant/C1-INH (may sometimes help). Trial high-dose Cetirizine or Diphenhydramine first — response confirms histaminergic subtype.[4][25]

Pediatric HAE: 50-75% have first attack by age 12 (mean onset 5-11yr). Smaller airway → faster asphyxia; fiberoptic laryngoscopy more difficult. 5 · Abdominal angioedema

GI involvement occurs in up to 73% of HAE patients; abdominal pain is present in 95% of GI cases. Can mimic acute abdomen → unnecessary imaging/surgical exploration. CT: ascites (most common), segmental bowel wall edema, straightening of bowel segments. Pediatrics: abdominal ultrasound preferred (radiation-sparing).[5]

In patients with known HAE and a typical recurrent pattern, it is reasonable to defer imaging if the clinical picture is consistent with prior episodes AND the patient improves with targeted therapy. Avoid unnecessary surgical exploration — angioedema-related bowel edema resolves with treatment. If diagnosis is uncertain, standard acute-abdomen workup remains appropriate; send C4 if HAE suspected.[4][5]

AVOID tranexamic acid — contraindicated in acute ischemic stroke (thrombotic risk with ongoing cerebral ischemia). Continue standard stroke management.[5]

Recurrent angioedema without urticaria, no identified cause despite thorough evaluation. Two subtypes by treatment response: histaminergic — responds to high-dose antihistamines (up to 4× standard), leukotriene antagonists, and/or steroids; non-histaminergic — refractory to antihistamines, consider Tranexamic Acid (often effective, especially as suppressive therapy) or icatibant/C1-INH (may sometimes help). Trial high-dose Cetirizine or Diphenhydramine first — response confirms histaminergic subtype.[4][25]

Pediatric HAE: 50-75% have first attack by age 12 (mean onset 5-11yr). Smaller airway → faster asphyxia; fiberoptic laryngoscopy more difficult. Abdominal presentations common (43.7% of pediatric HAE ED visits present with respiratory distress) — US preferred over CT (radiation-sparing, identifies bowel wall edema/intussusception).[5][24]

Berinert (pediatric): 20 units/kg IV IV — FDA-approved for pediatric HAE, preferred agent.

Tranexamic Acid (pediatric alternative): 25 mg/kg IV q8h, max 1.5 g IV — If C1-INH unavailable. Icatibant FDA-approved ≥18 only; limited ecallantide data <12yr.

Pregnancy/lactation: physiologic changes can mitigate, aggravate, or not affect HAE — variable. Berinert 20 units/kg IV preferred (plasma-derived, considered safest). If unavailable: Ruconest (recombinant, no rabbit allergy) or icatibant (life-threatening attacks only, small studies suggest safety). Estrogens can exacerbate bradykinin signaling — this alternative regimen is genuinely different from the pediatric alternative above (TXA), not the same fallback.[5]

5 · Abdominal angioedema

GI involvement occurs in up to 73% of HAE patients; abdominal pain is present in 95% of GI cases. Can mimic acute abdomen → unnecessary imaging/surgical exploration. CT: ascites (most common), segmental bowel wall edema, straightening of bowel segments. Pediatrics: abdominal ultrasound preferred (radiation-sparing).[5]

Berinert: 20 units/kg IV IV — Symptom improvement confirms the diagnosis. Alternative: icatibant 30 mg SQ or TXA 1 g IV.

In patients with known HAE and a typical recurrent pattern, it is reasonable to defer imaging if the clinical picture is consistent with prior episodes AND the patient improves with targeted therapy. Avoid unnecessary surgical exploration — angioedema-related bowel edema resolves with treatment. If diagnosis is uncertain, standard acute-abdomen workup remains appropriate; send C4 if HAE suspected.[4][5]

6 · Disposition — Ishoo staging

Disposition combines anatomic location (Modified Ishoo Staging), trajectory during observation, mechanism, and the objective airway-risk score. All patients need strict return precautions and PCP follow-up.[4][7][8][26]

Stage | Findings

Stage 1 | Facial rash, facial edema, lip edema

Stage 2 | Soft palate edema

Stage 3 | Tongue edema, floor of mouth edema

Stage 4 | Laryngeal edema (voice change, stridor)

Stage | Outcome data | Disposition

Stage 1-2: face/lip/soft palate | Lip-only edema has no increased intubation risk; Stage 1: 85% treated and discharged from ED, 0.1% require airway intervention | ED observe 4-6h minimum, then discharge with return precautions if criteria met

Stage 3: tongue/floor of mouth | Significantly higher risk of ICU admission and airway intervention than Stage 1-2 | Admit for close airway monitoring, serial assessments incl. flexible laryngoscopy, dual setup at bedside, low threshold for ICU

Stage 4: laryngeal involvement | 67% probability of airway intervention, 17% require ICU without intervention, 0% discharged from ED | ICU admission required, airway secured or continuous monitoring with dual setup, serial laryngoscopy

  • No progression of symptoms during observation
  • No signs of airway compromise
  • Able to tolerate oral intake
  • Reliable patient with understanding of return precautions
  • If allergic component: prescribe epinephrine autoinjector + short antihistamine course
  • If ACEi-induced: stop ACEi permanently, PCP follow-up for alternative antihypertensive
  • If HAE suspected: send C4 level, refer to allergist/immunologist

Stage 4 extubation planning: wait for visible improvement, extubate over an airway exchange catheter, confirm resolution by video laryngoscopy. HAE patients: discharge with targeted therapy (home C1-INH or icatibant) to reduce recurrence risk; refer to allergist/immunologist for long-term prophylaxis.[7][8][9]

7 · One-glance algorithm

Immediate airway threat, run objective risk, or clearly low-risk? — Stridor, hypoxemia, secretion failure, or rapidly progressive voice/laryngeal symptoms = immediate threat. Otherwise run the AJEM/Ishoo risk tool unless lip/facial edema only.

AJEM score / hard trigger / Ishoo stage — which band? — Score <5 low (~5% intubation), 5-7 moderate (~16%), >7 high (~68%). Hard triggers bypass the score.

Secure the airway — dual setup, awake technique, no paralysis until visualized — Dual setup (intubation + cric). Most experienced operator. Awake flexible/video technique preferred. Do NOT paralyze until anatomy visualized.

Airway monitoring — q15min reassessment, no NIPPV — Supplemental O2, continuous monitoring, q15min reassessment. Do NOT rely on NIPPV — doesn't treat anatomic obstruction.

Urticaria/pruritus, no urticaria, or unclear? — Histamine (responds to epi) vs bradykinin (does NOT respond to epi/antihistamines/steroids) is the single highest-yield decision.

Empiric epi+antihistamine — response or no response? — Assume histamine-mediated (40-70% of cases) when unclear. Response confirms histamine; no response suggests bradykinin.

Anaphylaxis features or isolated angioedema? — Multisystem involvement (hypotension, wheeze, GI/CV) = anaphylaxis protocol. Localized swelling only = mild pathway.

Anaphylaxis protocol — epinephrine first-line — Epi 0.3-0.5mg IM q5-20min x3. Refractory: IV drip. H1/H2/steroid adjuncts. Observe 4-6h, biphasic risk.

Isolated histamine-mediated angioedema — Diphenhydramine/cetirizine +/- famotidine +/- methylprednisolone. Observe 4-6h.

ACEi, HAE, post-tPA, acquired, idiopathic, or abdominal? — Not allergic — epi/antihistamines/steroids generally ineffective. Key history: ACEi/ARB, family history, recent tPA, age >40, abdominal pain.

ACEi-induced — stop drug, TXA/icatibant/FFP — STOP ACEi permanently. Not allergic. 30% of ED visits, 5x risk in Black patients. TXA 1g load+1g/8h first targeted option; icatibant, FFP, or Berinert (off-label) alternatives.

Known HAE, unknown/first presentation, pediatric, or pregnant? — Type 1 (85%, low C1-INH level), Type 2 (15%, low function), Type 3 (rare, normal C1-INH/factor XII).

HAE acute treatment — C1-INH replacement first-line — Berinert 20u/kg IV (preferred, 99% resolution) or Ruconest. Alt icatibant/ecallantide. Adjunct TXA/aminocaproic/FFP. First presentation: send C4, don't delay treatment for confirmation.

Special populations — pediatric or pregnant/lactating HAE — Peds: smaller airway, US preferred, Berinert first-line, TXA 25mg/kg alt. Pregnancy: Berinert preferred (safest), Ruconest/icatibant if unavailable — different alternates, not interchangeable.

Acquired angioedema (AAE) — same agents, screen for malignancy — ~1.5/million, onset >40yr, low C1q distinguishes from HAE. Same first-line drugs as HAE but may need higher/repeat dosing (autoantibody consumption). Screen for lymphoproliferative disorder.

Post-tPA angioedema — AVOID TXA — 0.9-7.9% incidence. Icatibant or Berinert (case reports). AVOID TXA — contraindicated in acute stroke.

Idiopathic — trial high-dose antihistamine first — Histaminergic subtype responds to high-dose antihistamine; non-histaminergic refractory subtype: TXA or icatibant/C1-INH.

Abdominal angioedema — treat underlying cause — GI involvement in 73% of HAE. Mimics acute abdomen. Berinert 20u/kg — symptom improvement confirms diagnosis. Avoid unnecessary surgery.

Ishoo Stage 1-2, Stage 3, or Stage 4? — Stage 1-2 face/lip/soft palate; Stage 3 tongue/floor of mouth; Stage 4 laryngeal (voice change/stridor).

Disposition by stage — Stage 1-2: observe 4-6h then discharge. Stage 3: admit for close monitoring. Stage 4: ICU — 67% need airway intervention.
