Pelvic Pain, Female: Sick Check First, Then Time-Critical Exclusions

Female patients of reproductive age and older presenting to the ED with pelvic pain, including postpartum and post-gynecologic-procedure presentations. Pregnancy testing is obtained for anyone with reproductive potential regardless of stated contraception or sterilization history. Excludes third-trimester obstetric labor complications and non-gynecologic chronic pelvic pain workups, which need separate protocols.

⚠ 5 DO NOT MISS: ruptured ectopic pregnancy (positive β-hCG + pain/shock — pregnancy test on everyone), ovarian torsion (sudden severe unilateral pain, nausea, adnexal mass), tubo-ovarian abscess / severe PID (fever, cervical motion tenderness, sepsis), hemorrhagic ovarian cyst with hemoperitoneum (sudden pain, free fluid, falling H/H), and non-gynecologic surgical abdomen — appendicitis, obstructing stone, pyelonephritis (do not anchor on "gyn").[1][2][3][4]
⚠ Choose the most dangerous lane first. PID/TOA, ectopic pregnancy, torsion, appendicitis, and ruptured cyst can overlap clinically — do not anchor on one lane too early.[1][6]
⚠ Pain plus a positive pregnancy test is ectopic until safely explained. An indeterminate ultrasound does not exclude ectopic pregnancy. Do not use a β-hCG below a discriminatory zone to reassure yourself when symptoms are concerning.[4][5]
⚠ High-suspicion torsion gets OB/GYN consultation even with Doppler flow present. Normal arterial/venous flow does not rule out torsion; ACOG emphasizes Doppler flow alone should not guide decision-making. If CT already suggests torsion, do not delay surgical consultation waiting for ultrasound.[1][3]
⚠ Do not wait for NAAT results to treat clinically suspected PID when the minimum clinical threshold is met — pelvic/lower abdominal pain with no better cause plus cervical motion, uterine, or adnexal tenderness. CDC recommends anaerobic coverage when TOA is present.[2]
⚠ Rule out ectopic and trend physiology before calling this benign cyst pain. Sudden unilateral pain after exertion/intercourse, peritoneal irritation, free pelvic fluid, anticoagulation, or a falling hemoglobin can mean hemorrhagic cyst rupture or another bleeding source.[1][4][5]
⚠ A pelvic exam diagnosis should not override peritoneal signs or surgical-abdomen physiology. Appendicitis, diverticulitis, bowel obstruction, perforation, and inflammatory bowel complications can present as pelvic pain.[1][6]
⚠ Postpartum or procedural pelvic pain deserves a lower threshold for OB/GYN involvement and imaging. Danger lanes: hemorrhage, retained products, endometritis, septic pelvic thrombophlebitis, uterine perforation, bowel/bladder injury, ovarian vein thrombosis, wound/abscess, and PE/vascular disease.[1][6]
⚠ Do not discharge unresolved high-risk pelvic pain just because initial imaging is equivocal. Admit, transfer, or consult urgently for suspected ectopic, torsion, TOA/severe PID, sepsis, surgical abdomen, large hemoperitoneum, unstable vitals, pregnancy complication, immunocompromise with infection, failed outpatient therapy, inability to tolerate PO, uncontrolled pain, or persistent diagnostic uncertainty with a concerning exam.[1][2][3][4]

Ovarian / adnexal torsion
Classic pattern: sudden unilateral pelvic pain, nausea/vomiting, adnexal tenderness or mass, intermittent episodes, pregnancy, cyst, fertility treatment, or adolescent patient. Fever and leukocytosis may be absent. Imaging: transvaginal/transabdominal pelvic ultrasound with Doppler is first line, but normal arterial/venous flow does not rule out torsion. If CT already suggests torsion, do not delay surgical consultation waiting for ultrasound. Action: NPO, analgesia/antiemetic, pregnancy test, OB/GYN early — diagnosis and treatment are surgical.
PID / tubo-ovarian abscess
Open Pelvic Inflammatory Disease. PID minimum threshold: pelvic/lower abdominal pain with no better cause plus cervical motion, uterine, or adnexal tenderness — treat empirically when the clinical threshold is met, do not wait for NAAT results. TOA / severe-PID flags: fever, toxicity, vomiting, pregnancy, tubo-ovarian abscess on imaging, severe illness, failed outpatient therapy, inability to tolerate PO, or possible surgical emergency. CDC recommends anaerobic coverage when TOA is present.
Ruptured hemorrhagic cyst / hemoperitoneum
Sudden unilateral pain, pain after exertion/intercourse, peritoneal irritation, free pelvic fluid, anticoagulation, or falling hemoglobin suggests hemorrhagic cyst rupture or another bleeding source. Actions: pregnancy test to exclude ectopic, IV access, CBC trend, type and screen if significant pain/free fluid/instability, analgesia, pelvic ultrasound, OB/GYN if unstable, persistent peritoneal signs, large hemoperitoneum, anticoagulated, or falling Hgb.
Appendicitis / surgical abdomen / GI mimics
Appendicitis, diverticulitis, bowel obstruction, perforation, and inflammatory bowel complications can present as pelvic pain. Migration, anorexia, peritoneal signs, persistent vomiting, focal RLQ/LLQ tenderness, or systemic illness should pull the workup beyond gynecology. Imaging: choose US first when pregnancy/gynecologic source is likely; CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast is appropriate when non-gynecologic surgical disease is likely and pregnancy is excluded or risk/benefit supports imaging.
Urinary / renal lane
Open UTI-Adult when infection fits. Includes: pyelonephritis, infected stone, renal colic, urinary retention, urethritis/cervicitis overlap. Fever with obstruction, solitary kidney, pregnancy, AKI, uncontrolled pain/vomiting, or sepsis physiology needs escalation. UA can be contaminated by vaginal bleeding/discharge — interpret with symptoms, microscopy, culture, and imaging when stone/obstruction is plausible.
Postpartum / post-procedure pain
Recent delivery, miscarriage management, abortion, IUD procedure, hysteroscopy, laparoscopy, or pelvic surgery changes the differential. Danger lanes: hemorrhage, retained products, endometritis, septic pelvic thrombophlebitis, uterine perforation, bowel/bladder injury, ovarian vein thrombosis, wound/abscess, and PE/vascular disease. Use vitals, bleeding amount, fever, peritoneal signs, lactate if ill, CBC, pregnancy status as relevant, pelvic ultrasound, and CT when surgical complication is plausible. Involve OB/GYN early for unstable or septic patients.
Rescue / initial bundle + reassess
Default ED bundle: pregnancy test, vitals trend, IV access if moderate/severe pain or any bleeding/instability. Analgesia and antiemetic early — pain response does not rule out torsion, ectopic, or surgical disease. CBC, CMP as indicated; UA/culture by urinary symptoms; GC/CT testing and wet prep when PID/cervicitis possible. Pelvic exam when it changes care and patient consents. Bedside US for free fluid/IUP when skilled; formal pelvic US for suspected gynecologic etiology.

3 · Reassess, imaging, and disposition

Reassess after initial bundle
Repeat vitals and abdominal/pelvic exam after analgesia, fluids, antiemetics, and first imaging/labs. Unstable, worse pain, peritoneal signs, persistent vomiting, or concerning US/CT/labs → return to time-critical exclusions. Stable but diagnosis still uncertain or imaging needed → use imaging strategy. Clear low-risk diagnosis, improving, tolerating PO, follow-up available → disposition checklist. Persistent or worsening pain after initial care should widen the differential, not narrow it.
Imaging strategy
Pregnancy positive or pregnancy possible: pelvic US first for IUP/ectopic/free fluid; OB involvement for indeterminate but concerning cases. Gynecologic source likely, beta-hCG negative: transabdominal plus transvaginal pelvic US with Doppler is usually first-line — do not use normal Doppler flow to exclude torsion when suspicion is high. Non-gynecologic source likely: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast is often appropriate when appendicitis, diverticulitis, bowel pathology, abscess, or urinary obstruction is the active question. MRI: useful when ultrasound is nondiagnostic and radiation avoidance matters, especially pregnancy or selected young patients.

Do not discharge unresolved high-risk pelvic pain just because initial imaging is equivocal.
Observation / serial exam
Observe when pain is improving but diagnosis remains uncertain, ultrasound is pending/equivocal, hemoglobin or vitals require trend, pregnancy of unknown location needs a plan, or serial abdominal exams will determine CT/surgical consultation. Observation is appropriate when the initial snapshot does not safely explain the pain.
Discharge checklist
Discharge only when vitals are stable, pregnancy/ectopic risk has an explicit plan, pain is controlled, PO tolerated, abdominal/pelvic exam is non-peritoneal, dangerous causes have been addressed, and follow-up is realistic. Return now for: worsening pain, syncope, shoulder pain, heavy bleeding, fever, vomiting, new discharge, positive pregnancy test without follow-up, dizziness, or inability to tolerate PO.

5 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT skip pregnancy testing. Ectopic pregnancy can present with pain, bleeding, syncope, shoulder pain, or minimal symptoms — history and contraception do not safely exclude pregnancy.

Do NOT rule out torsion with normal Doppler flow. Intermittent torsion and dual ovarian blood supply can preserve flow. High-suspicion torsion still needs OB/GYN consultation.

Do NOT wait for STI/NAAT results to treat PID. CDC minimum clinical criteria support empiric treatment when PID is plausible and no better cause explains the pain.

Do NOT anchor on gynecology when the abdomen is surgical. Appendicitis, diverticulitis, bowel pathology, urinary obstruction, and vascular disease can present as pelvic pain.

Do NOT discharge unresolved high-risk pelvic pain. Persistent severe pain, peritoneal signs, unstable vitals, pregnancy risk, vomiting, fever, or diagnostic uncertainty needs observation, imaging, or consultation.
