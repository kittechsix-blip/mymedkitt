ECMO: VV vs VA Selection, Management, and Disposition

Adults with refractory respiratory and/or cardiac failure being considered for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, in the ED/ICU. Excludes neonatal/pediatric ECMO protocols and detailed perfusionist circuit-management decisions — this supports the initial selection, timing, and referral decision, not bedside circuit troubleshooting.

⚠ VA-ECMO timing is time-critical. Each 12-hour delay from admission to VA-ECMO initiation increases adjusted in-hospital mortality by 6%; initiation after 24 hours is associated with worse outcomes. Call the ECMO team at SCAI Shock Stage D (escalating/multiple pressors, lactate >4 and rising) — do NOT wait for Stage E (near-arrest or arrest).
⚠ Harlequin (North-South) syndrome. In peripheral VA-ECMO, a recovering native heart can eject still-deoxygenated blood (lungs still failing) to the upper body while retrograde ECMO flow oxygenates the lower body. Recognize by right hand SpO₂ <90% with normal lower-body saturation and low cerebral NIRS — risk of coronary and cerebral hypoxia. Use a right radial arterial line for early detection.
⚠ LV distension. VA-ECMO's retrograde flow raises afterload; a failing LV may not eject, distending and risking pulmonary edema and LV thrombus. Watch for worsening pulmonary edema and an aortic valve that fails to open on echo. If the aortic valve is not opening after 24-48h, risk of LV thrombus is high — options are low-dose inotropy, reducing ECMO flow, or LV venting (Impella, IABP, surgical vent, atrial septostomy).
⚠ Contraindications. Absolute: irreversible underlying disease with no transplant candidacy, advanced malignancy, severe irreversible neurologic injury, unwitnessed arrest with prolonged downtime and no signs of life, uncontrolled bleeding or an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation. VA-ECMO-specific: severe aortic regurgitation (worsens with retrograde flow), severe peripheral vascular disease, and Type A aortic dissection with branch involvement.
⚠ Distal perfusion cannula is essential for peripheral VA-ECMO. The femoral arterial return cannula can obstruct antegrade flow to the leg; a 6-8 Fr distal perfusion cannula spliced into the arterial line prevents limb ischemia, compartment syndrome, and amputation. Check distal pulses and NIRS hourly.

1 · VV vs VA — the fundamental split

ECMO is prolonged extracorporeal cardiopulmonary bypass for refractory respiratory and/or cardiac failure. The key question at the bedside: what is failing — the lungs, the heart, or both?[1][2]

VV-ECMO — lungs only
Provides oxygenation and CO₂ removal ONLY — no hemodynamic support. The patient must have adequate cardiac function. Bridge to lung recovery or transplant.
VA-ECMO — lungs and heart
Provides oxygenation AND circulatory support — bypasses both heart and lungs. For cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest (ECPR). Bridge to recovery, LVAD, or transplant.
When unsure, go VA
VA-ECMO can support both heart and lungs; VV-ECMO cannot support a failing heart. When in doubt, go VA.

2 · VV-ECMO — indications, timing, management

VV-ECMO is indicated for severe, potentially reversible respiratory failure. EOLIA criteria: P/F ratio <50 for >3 hours, OR P/F ratio <80 for >6 hours, despite optimal ventilation, prone positioning, and FiO₂ >80%. Hypercapnic failure: pH <7.20-7.25 with PaCO₂ >60 mmHg despite optimal ventilation and rescue therapies. The lungs must be recoverable or the patient must be a transplant candidate.

  • ARDS (viral, bacterial pneumonia)
  • COVID-19 ARDS
  • Aspiration pneumonitis
  • Trauma/contusion
  • Status asthmaticus (refractory)
  • Drowning/near-drowning
  • Massive air leak syndrome
  • Bridge to lung transplant
Call the ECMO center EARLY
Mortality with mechanical ventilation before ECMO: 77% mortality vs 38% if <7 days. Don't delay the call waiting for everything to fail — optimize prone positioning, neuromuscular blockade, conservative fluids, and lung-protective ventilation first, but call while doing so.

Femorojugular (most common): drainage cannula in the femoral vein (tip at IVC/RA junction), return cannula in the right IJ (tip at SVC/RA junction), separated by 8-15 cm to minimize recirculation. Dual-lumen (Avalon): single IJ cannula, drains SVC+IVC, returns toward the tricuspid valve — technically challenging, needs fluoroscopy/TEE. Initial flow target: BSA × 1.8 L/min/m².

Recirculation troubleshootingsigns, formula, fixes

Recirculation = oxygenated blood returns to the circuit before reaching systemic circulation. Signs: poor systemic oxygenation despite high flow/FiO₂, pre-membrane SpreO₂ >75-80% with normal post-membrane SpostO₂ (~100%) and low patient SpO₂. Formula: Recirculation = (SpreO₂ − SvO₂) / (SpostO₂ − SvO₂) × 100%. Causes: cannulas <8 cm apart, excessive ECMO flow vs cardiac output, cannula malposition. Fixes: reposition cannulas, decrease ECMO flow, increase native cardiac output, or convert to dual-lumen. Acceptable recirculation: <10-15%.

3 · VA-ECMO — indications, timing, management

VA-ECMO provides both oxygenation and circulatory support for: refractory cardiogenic shock (acute MI, decompensated HF, fulminant myocarditis, post-cardiotomy, sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy), massive/unstable PE, refractory arrhythmia storm, cardiotoxic drug overdose (beta-blocker, CCB, TCA) failing HIET and pressors, and ECPR. Composite hemodynamic indication: lactate >4 and rising + CI <2.0 + MAP <65 despite high-dose pressors.

Call at SCAI Stage D, not Stage E
Each 12-hour delay from admission to VA-ECMO initiation increases adjusted in-hospital mortality by 6%; initiation after 24 hours is associated with worse outcomes. SCAI Stage C (classic shock, may still respond) → Stage D (failing/escalating pressors, lactate >4 and rising — this is the time to call) → Stage E (near-arrest, may be too late).

Peripheral cannulation (femoral-femoral): drainage from the femoral vein (IVC/RA junction), return to the femoral artery (common iliac/distal aorta) — ECMO flow is retrograde. Central (surgical) cannulation: direct aortic and RA cannulation, essentially cardiopulmonary bypass, nearly all get an LV vent. Initial flow target: BSA × 2.4 L/min/m², may need 80-100% of cardiac output initially.

Right radial arterial line — mandatory
Monitors for differential hypoxemia (Harlequin syndrome). Also: pulse oximeter on the right hand/ear, hourly distal-limb perfusion checks (NIRS if available), and daily echo for aortic valve opening, MR, and LV thrombus. Anticoagulation target same as VV: PTT 50-70 sec OR anti-Xa 0.3-0.5 IU/mL.
Harlequin (North-South) syndrome
Right hand SpO₂ <90% (upper body) with normal lower-body SpO₂ and low cerebral NIRS — upper body cyanotic, lower body pink. Danger: coronary and cerebral hypoxia. Management: optimize native lung function, increase ECMO flow, convert to VAV-ECMO, central cannulation, or dual VA+VV circuit. Prevention: early recognition, right radial arterial line.
LV distension
VA-ECMO's retrograde flow raises afterload; a failing LV can't eject, distends, and risks pulmonary edema and thrombus. Signs: worsening pulmonary edema, aortic valve not opening on echo, severe MR, rising PA pressures, blood-tinged ETT secretions. Management: low-dose dobutamine/milrinone, reduce ECMO flow (if tolerated), or LV venting (Impella, IABP, surgical vent, atrial septostomy). If AV not opening >24-48h → high risk of LV thrombus.

4 · ECPR — extracorporeal CPR

ECPR = VA-ECMO during cardiac arrest. Conventional CPR provides only 20-30% of normal cardiac output; ECMO provides full circulatory support, allowing time to treat a reversible cause. PRAGUE OHCA (Belohlavek RCT): 31.5% survived to 180 days with good neurologic outcomes (ECPR) vs 22% (conventional CPR).

  • Witnessed arrest with initial shockable rhythm (VF/pVT)
  • Age <65-70 years
  • Short low-flow time (<60 min ideal, <90 min max)
  • High-quality CPR ongoing, presumed reversible cause
  • No severe comorbidities; signs of life during CPR (ETCO₂ >10, pupil reactivity)
<60 min ideal, <90 min maxIdeal low-flow (CPR) time window

Poor candidates: unwitnessed arrest, asystole as initial rhythm, prolonged downtime (>60-90 min), non-reversible cause, severe comorbidities, known poor baseline function.

Cannulate fast; don't prognosticate early
Target: cannulation within 60 minutes of arrest. Continue mechanical CPR (e.g. LUCAS) during cannulation. Do NOT prognosticate in the first 72 hours — allow rewarming and sedation washout, then serial neuro exams/EEG/imaging.

5 · Contraindications

Absolute contraindications
Irreversible underlying disease with no transplant candidacy, advanced malignancy with poor prognosis, severe irreversible neurologic injury (massive stroke, anoxic brain injury), unwitnessed arrest with prolonged downtime and no signs of life, uncontrolled bleeding or an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation.

VV-ECMO relative: mechanical ventilation >7-10 days with Pplat >30 + FiO₂ >90%, multi-organ failure (high SOFA), severe immunosuppression, pulmonary hypertension (mPAP >50 — consider VA instead).

VA-ECMO relative: severe aortic regurgitation (LV distension risk), severe peripheral vascular disease (cannulation difficulty), Type A aortic dissection with branch involvement.

General relative: age >70-75 (institution-dependent), frailty (Clinical Frailty Scale ≥4), pre-existing organ dysfunction (cirrhosis, ESRD without a transplant plan), prolonged CPR without ROSC, morbid obesity (technical difficulty).

6 · Cannulation strategies

Femorojugular (most common): drainage 21-25 Fr femoral vein (tip IVC/RA junction), return 17-21 Fr right IJ (tip SVC/RA junction), 8-15 cm apart. Dual-lumen (Avalon/Crescent): single 27-31 Fr IJ cannula — easier mobilization, technically challenging insertion. Femoral-femoral VV: both via femoral veins, higher recirculation risk, reserved for when IJ isn't accessible.

VA cannulation — distal perfusion cannula essential
Peripheral femoral-femoral (most common): drainage 21-25 Fr femoral vein (tip IVC/RA junction), return 15-19 Fr femoral artery (tip common iliac or distal aorta). A 6-8 Fr distal perfusion cannula spliced into the arterial return line is ESSENTIAL to prevent limb ischemia (compartment syndrome, amputation) — check distal pulses and NIRS. Central (surgical): direct RA + aortic, sternotomy/thoracotomy, post-cardiotomy, LV vent typically placed simultaneously. Subclavian/axillary return: antegrade flow to brain/coronaries, avoids limb ischemia and differential hypoxemia, technically more challenging with higher stroke risk.

9 · Disposition

Initiate ECMO
Proceed with ECMO cannulation per institutional protocol; ICU admission with ECMO team management. Pre-cannulation: consent, blood products (PRBCs/FFP/platelets/cryo), type and screen, access assessment, anticoagulation plan, goals-of-care discussion. Remember: ECMO is a bridge, not a destination — have an exit strategy.
Not an ECMO candidate
Document specific contraindications and any ECMO-team discussion. Continue aggressive conventional care (lung-protective ventilation, prone positioning, maximal medical hemodynamic support, other rescue therapies as appropriate) or transition to comfort care per goals of care.
Transfer for ECMO evaluation
Contact the ECMO center early — don't wait until the patient is crashing. Some centers can send a mobile ECMO retrieval team. Optimize for transport (airway, access, blood products, pressors) before transfer; if in arrest or near-arrest, ECMO must be initiated locally or the patient is unlikely to survive transfer.

10 · Critical actions (Do NOT)

VV-ECMO provides oxygenation ONLY — do not select VV if the patient does not have adequate cardiac function; it cannot support a failing heart.
When in doubt between VV and VA, go VA — VA can support both heart and lungs, VV cannot.
ECPR candidacy: best outcomes with witnessed arrest, bystander CPR, shockable rhythm, and CPR duration <60 min.
Severe aortic regurgitation is an absolute contraindication for VA-ECMO — retrograde flow worsens AR and precipitates LV distension.
Do not miss Harlequin syndrome — upper-body cyanosis with lower-body pink skin signals coronary and cerebral hypoxia risk on VA-ECMO.

Watch for Harlequin syndrome (differential hypoxemia) — Right hand SpO₂ <90% with normal lower-body SpO₂ = coronary/cerebral hypoxia risk.

Watch for LV distension — Aortic valve not opening on echo; not opening >24-48h = high risk of LV thrombus.

ECPR: candidacy + logistics — Best candidate: witnessed arrest, shockable rhythm, <60min low-flow, reversible cause. Target cannulation within 60 min.

Contraindications check — Absolute: irreversible disease, severe neuro injury, uncontrolled bleeding. VA-specific: severe AR.

Which cannulation configuration?

VV cannulation: femorojugular / dual-lumen / fem-fem — Drainage 21-25 Fr femoral vein; return 17-21 Fr right IJ; 8-15 cm separation.

VA cannulation: fem-fem + distal perfusion cannula — Distal perfusion cannula (6-8 Fr) ESSENTIAL to prevent limb ischemia.

Complications, survival scores, ECMO-call checklist — Bleeding 30-50%, thrombosis 10-20%, limb ischemia (VA) 10-20%. RESP (VV) / SAVE (VA) scores aid triage.

ECMO candidate — where does the patient go?

Initiate ECMO — ICU admission with ECMO team

Not a candidate — conventional care or comfort care

9 · Disposition

Proceed with ECMO cannulation per institutional protocol; ICU admission with ECMO team management. Pre-cannulation: consent, blood products (PRBCs/FFP/platelets/cryo), type and screen, access assessment, anticoagulation plan, goals-of-care discussion. Remember: ECMO is a bridge, not a destination — have an exit strategy.

Document specific contraindications and any ECMO-team discussion. Continue aggressive conventional care (lung-protective ventilation, prone positioning, maximal medical hemodynamic support, other rescue therapies as appropriate) or transition to comfort care per goals of care.

Contact the ECMO center early — don't wait until the patient is crashing. Some centers can send a mobile ECMO retrieval team. Optimize for transport (airway, access, blood products, pressors) before transfer; if in arrest or near-arrest, ECMO must be initiated locally or the patient is unlikely to survive transfer.

10 · Critical actions (Do NOT)

VV-ECMO provides oxygenation ONLY — do not select VV if the patient does not have adequate cardiac function; it cannot support a failing heart.

When in doubt between VV and VA, go VA — VA can support both heart and lungs, VV cannot.

ECPR candidacy: best outcomes with witnessed arrest, bystander CPR, shockable rhythm, and CPR duration <60 min.

Severe aortic regurgitation is an absolute contraindication for VA-ECMO — retrograde flow worsens AR and precipitates LV distension.

Do not miss Harlequin syndrome — upper-body cyanosis with lower-body pink skin signals coronary and cerebral hypoxia risk on VA-ECMO.
