Myasthenia Gravis — Crisis Recognition & Management

Adults with known or suspected myasthenia gravis in the ED/ICU, including myasthenic and cholinergic crisis. Excludes pediatric MG, congenital myasthenic syndromes, and Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome — each has a distinct pathway.

⚠ Myasthenic (DRY, mydriasis) vs cholinergic (WET, SLUDGE/BBB, miosis) crisis need OPPOSITE treatment — if uncertain, STOP all anticholinesterase and support the airway rather than guessing.
⚠ Intubate at FVC <15-20 mL/kg or NIF weaker than -20 to -25 cmH2O — the threshold is LOW; do not wait for overt respiratory arrest.
⚠ Cholinergic crisis: STOP all anticholinesterase (pyridostigmine/neostigmine) + atropine 0.5-1 mg IV for muscarinic effects only — atropine does NOT reverse the underlying weakness.
⚠ AVOID in MG: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, beta-blockers (including ophthalmic timolol), IV magnesium, full-dose neuromuscular blockers — all can precipitate crisis.
⚠ Corticosteroids can cause INITIAL WORSENING in up to 50% of patients in the first 1-2 weeks — start in hospital with IVIG/PLEX "cover" for moderate-severe disease.
⚠ Rocuronium: MG patients are VERY SENSITIVE — use 1/3 to 1/2 the normal dose (0.3-0.5 mg/kg); have sugammadex available and avoid succinylcholine if possible.
⚠ IVIG is contraindicated in IgA deficiency (anaphylaxis risk) — check IgA level before the first dose if not already known.

1 · Recognize & classify

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction — antibodies target acetylcholine receptors (AChR) or related proteins. Hallmark: fatigable weakness — worse with use, better with rest, with diurnal variation (worse through the day). Ocular symptoms (ptosis, diplopia) are present in 85% at onset; bulbar involvement (dysarthria, dysphagia, facial weakness) and limb weakness (proximal > distal) follow. Respiratory muscle involvement is the emergency.[1]

Do not confuse with GBS
MG weakness fluctuates and improves with rest. GBS weakness is progressive ascending weakness that does NOT fluctuate.

▶ What to do now

Stable — proceed to routine workup

Myasthenic crisis confirmed — proceed to immunotherapy
DRY, mydriasis, no fasciculations, no SLUDGE. Start IVIG or plasmapheresis immediately; do NOT delay. Continue pyridostigmine unless cholinergic concern.
Cholinergic crisis — STOP anticholinesterase + atropine
WET, SLUDGE/BBB, miosis, fasciculations. STOP all anticholinesterase medications now and give atropine for muscarinic effects (does NOT reverse weakness).
Uncertain crisis type — safe universal approach
STOP all anticholinesterase (safe for both types), support the airway, atropine PRN for secretions, and reassess in 30-60 minutes before restarting pyridostigmine.

2 · Myasthenic crisis & the critical distinction

15-20% of MG patients will experience crisis; modern mortality is 4-8% (down from 40% historically). Mean ICU stay ~2 weeks. Infection is the most common trigger (40%), followed by surgery (especially thymectomy), medication changes, non-compliance with pyridostigmine or immunosuppressants, and pregnancy/postpartum.

Intubation threshold
FVC <15-20 mL/kg or NIF weaker than -20 to -25 cmH2O, a declining trend despite treatment, severe bulbar dysfunction (aspiration risk), or respiratory distress with accessory-muscle use.

3 · Cholinergic crisis management

  1. STOP all anticholinesterase medications (pyridostigmine, neostigmine).
  2. Suction secretions; intubate early if significant bronchorrhea/bronchospasm.
  3. Give atropine for muscarinic effects (secretions, bradycardia, bronchospasm) — does NOT reverse nicotinic weakness.
  4. IV fluids and cardiac monitoring — bradycardia can be severe; monitor for aspiration.
  5. Effects resolve as pyridostigmine metabolizes (half-life 1-2 hours); restart at a lower dose only after resolution.

4 · Uncertain crisis type — safe approach

  1. STOP all anticholinesterase medications — safe for both crisis types.
  2. Support the airway; low threshold for intubation; suction secretions.
  3. Atropine 0.5-1 mg IV if significant secretions.
  4. Reassess in 30-60 minutes: cholinergic signs resolve if cholinergic; weakness persists or worsens without cholinergic signs if myasthenic.
  5. Do NOT give more pyridostigmine until confident this is myasthenic crisis.

5 · Diagnostic workup

Ice pack test — ice on closed eyelid for 2 minutes; >2mm improvement in ptosis is positive, 80% sensitive for ocular MG. AChR antibodies are 85% sensitive in generalized MG (50% in ocular MG); anti-MuSK is found in 5-10% of "seronegative" MG; anti-LRP4 is rare. Repetitive nerve stimulation with >10% decrement is positive; single-fiber EMG is the most sensitive test (92-99%). CT chest: thymoma in 10-15%, thymic hyperplasia in 65% — all newly diagnosed MG patients need chest imaging. Check TFTs (associated autoimmune thyroid disease) and screen for other autoimmune disorders.

Plasmapheresis: faster onset, requires central venous access and specialized equipment; complications include hypotension, citrate toxicity, line infection.

Do NOT start high-dose steroids without IVIG/PLEX cover in moderate-severe disease — up to 50% transiently worsen.

Do NOT use a standard/full-dose neuromuscular blocker for intubation — MG patients are very sensitive; use rocuronium 0.3-0.5 mg/kg and have sugammadex available.

Do NOT operate (thymectomy) during crisis — elective only, after stabilization.
