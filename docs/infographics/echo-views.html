Basic Emergency Echocardiography Views (FoCUS)

Point-of-care focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) for the emergency/critical-care setting — rapid assessment of global cardiac function, pericardial effusion/tamponade, RV size and strain (PE evaluation), and volume status (IVC) using the 5 essential emergency echo views: PLAX, PSAX, A4C, subcostal 4-chamber, and IVC. Requires a phased-array cardiac transducer (2–5 MHz). For quantitative LV function via EPSS, see the companion Echo-EPSS consult.

⚠ This is a focused (not comprehensive) exam — formal echocardiography is recommended for complex cases or when findings would significantly change management.
⚠ IVC assessment is less reliable in mechanically ventilated patients.
⚠ Pericardial fat pad can mimic effusion — fat is slightly echogenic; true effusion is anechoic.
⚠ Poor parasternal/apical acoustic windows (COPD, obesity, mechanical ventilation) — use the subcostal window instead.

FoCUS overview

Focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) gives a rapid bedside read on global cardiac function (LV squeeze), pericardial effusion/tamponade, RV size and strain (PE evaluation), and volume status (IVC). Probe: phased-array cardiac transducer (2–5 MHz). Patient position: supine or left lateral decubitus.

  1. Parasternal Long Axis (PLAX)
  2. Parasternal Short Axis (PSAX)
  3. Apical 4-Chamber (A4C)
  4. Subcostal 4-Chamber
  5. IVC (from subcostal)

PLAX — acquisition

Position: left sternal border, 3rd–4th intercostal space. Indicator: toward right shoulder (or left hip in some conventions). Visualizes the left ventricle, left atrium, mitral valve, aortic valve/root, RVOT, and pericardium.

The 3 L's rule
Parasternal Long axis should have the Left ventricle on the Left side of the screen.

PLAX — assessment

Pericardial effusion: anechoic stripe between epicardium and pericardium, most commonly posterior; size small (<10mm), moderate (10–20mm), large (>20mm). LV function: visual estimation of global squeeze; normal EF = walls move in >50% during systole; see EPSS measurement for a quantitative read. RV size: RVOT should be <2/3 of LV diameter — an enlarged RV suggests right heart strain. Aortic root: normally <4 cm; look for a dissection flap if clinically indicated. Wall motion: regional hypokinesis suggests ischemia.

PSAX — acquisition

From PLAX: rotate the probe 90° clockwise. Indicator: toward left shoulder. Fan through 4 levels: base (aortic valve — "Mercedes-Benz sign"), mitral valve ("fish mouth"), papillary muscle (best level for LV function; LV should look circular, an "O" shape), and apex (smallest cavity — look for apical thrombus).

PSAX — assessment

Wall motion: all walls should contract symmetrically; regional hypokinesis/akinesis suggests coronary-territory infarction. LV function: watch the squeeze at the papillary muscle level — walls should thicken and move inward. RV size: compare RV to LV (RV wraps around LV) — an enlarged RV suggests right heart strain.

D-sign (septal flattening)
Normal LV is circular ("O" shape). Pressure overload pushes the septum flat into a "D" shape — seen in massive PE and pulmonary hypertension. Flattening during systole = pressure overload; flattening during diastole = volume overload.

A4C — acquisition

Position: cardiac apex, mid-clavicular line, 5th–6th intercostal space. Indicator: toward the patient's left flank (3 o'clock). Technique: probe nearly horizontal, pointing toward the right shoulder. Visualizes all 4 chambers — LV apex at the top of screen, RV to the left of LV — plus mitral and tricuspid valves and both septa.

A4C — assessment

RV:LV ratio: normal RV <0.6× LV size; RV = LV suggests moderate RV dilation; RV > LV suggests severe RV dilation (massive PE). TAPSE (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion): M-mode through the lateral tricuspid annulus; normal ≥17 mm; <17 mm suggests RV dysfunction. Global LV function: visual estimation of ejection fraction, compare to A2C view. Pericardial effusion: may see circumferential effusion from this view; RA/RV diastolic collapse suggests tamponade physiology.

McConnell sign
RV free wall akinesis with apical sparing — the apex "winks" while the rest of the RV doesn't move. Highly specific for acute PE.
RV:LV ratio — massive PE
RV = LV suggests moderate dilation. RV > LV suggests severe dilation (massive PE).

Subcostal — acquisition

Position: just below the xiphoid process. Technique: probe nearly flat against the abdomen, angled toward the left shoulder. Indicator: toward the patient's left. When to use: poor parasternal/apical windows (COPD, obesity, ventilated patients); best view for pericardial effusion (liver provides the acoustic window); required for IVC assessment. Visualizes all 4 chambers (RV closest to probe), the liver, and the pericardium.

Subcostal — assessment

Often the best view for posterior pericardial effusion — look for an anechoic stripe between the heart and liver, between the epicardium and the bright pericardial line. Global cardiac function is often easier to see here in patients with poor windows.

Tamponade physiology
RA systolic collapse (very sensitive). RV diastolic collapse (very specific). Swinging heart in a large effusion.
Pitfall
Pericardial fat pad can mimic effusion — fat is slightly echogenic, effusion is anechoic.

IVC assessment

From the subcostal view: rotate the probe to visualize the IVC entering the RA (longitudinal orientation). Measure IVC diameter 2–3 cm from the RA junction, and respiratory variation (sniff test or quiet breathing).

IVC DiameterCollapseEstimated CVP
<2.1 cm>50%0–5 mmHg (low)
<2.1 cm<50%5–10 mmHg
>2.1 cm>50%5–10 mmHg
>2.1 cm<50%10–20 mmHg (elevated)

Clinical applications: a plethoric IVC (>2.1 cm, <50% collapse) suggests elevated RA pressure, volume overload, RV failure, or tamponade. A collapsing IVC suggests the patient is likely volume responsive.

Pitfall
IVC assessment is less reliable in mechanically ventilated patients.

Summary & disposition

  • Pericardial effusion — PLAX, subcostal (best)
  • LV function — PLAX, PSAX, A4C (visual + EPSS)
  • RV size/strain — A4C (RV:LV ratio, McConnell sign)
  • Volume status — IVC (diameter + collapse)
Red flags — notify immediately
Large pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. Severely dilated RV with hypotension (massive PE). Severely reduced LV function with cardiogenic shock. Plethoric IVC with signs of volume overload.
Recommendation
Complete 5-view focused cardiac ultrasound and integrate findings with the clinical presentation. Formal echocardiography is recommended for complex cases or when findings would significantly change management. For quantitative LV assessment, see EPSS Measurement or the Echo-EPSS Consult.
