STEMI: ECG Diagnosis to Reperfusion

Adults ≥18 in the ED or prehospital setting with suspected acute coronary syndrome. STEMI is an ECG diagnosis — do NOT wait for troponin. Excludes stable chronic coronary disease, pediatrics, and pregnancy-specific management.

⚠ STEMI is an ECG diagnosis — do NOT wait for troponin. A negative troponin does not rule it out; 8.4% of ACS presents WITHOUT chest pain.
⚠ Do NOT give nitrates or diuretics in suspected RV infarction (inferior STEMI + hypotension, STE ≥1 mm in V4R) — these patients are preload-dependent; nitrates can cause hemodynamic collapse.
⚠ Do NOT give morphine unless pain is refractory to nitroglycerin — it delays P2Y12 inhibitor absorption and was associated with higher mortality in ACS (CRUSADE).
⚠ AIVR after reperfusion is benign — a sign of successful reperfusion. Do NOT treat with antidysrhythmics; suppressing it can cause hemodynamic collapse.
⚠ In cardiogenic shock, do NOT withhold vasopressors for fear of worsening ischemia — PCI regardless of time from onset, and coronary perfusion is the goal until revascularization.

1 · Recognize & confirm

12-lead ECG within 10 minutes
Obtain a 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of first medical contact. STEMI is an ECG diagnosis — do not delay for troponin, and a negative troponin does not rule it out.[4]
  • Chest pain + diaphoresis carries the highest likelihood ratio for STEMI.[1]
  • Radiation to both arms — 96% specific but only 11% sensitive.[2]
  • 8.4% of ACS presents WITHOUT chest pain — dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, or syncope may be the only clue.[3]
STEMI ECG criteria
New J-point elevation in ≥2 contiguous leads: ≥1 mm in all leads except V2–V3. In V2–V3: ≥2 mm (men ≥40y), ≥2.5 mm (men <40y), ≥1.5 mm (women). Always hunt for reciprocal changes — they confirm true occlusion.[7]
TerritoryLeadsVessel
AnteriorV1–V4LAD
InferiorII, III, aVFRCA (85%) or LCx (15%)
LateralI, aVL, V5, V6LCx
PosteriorV7–V9 (± V1–V3 depression)LCx

ST depression V1–V3 with upright T waves and a prominent R wave suggests posterior STEMI. Obtain posterior leads V7–V9. STE ≥0.5 mm in any posterior lead confirms it (≥1 mm in men <40y is more specific) — manage as STEMI. Posterior STEMI is frequently missed.[6]

Sgarbossa criterionPoints
Concordant STE ≥1 mm (positive-QRS leads)5
Concordant ST depression ≥1 mm, V1–V3 (or modified Smith ST/S ratio < −0.25)3
Discordant STE ≥5 mm (negative-QRS leads)2
Score ≥3 = 98% specificity for STEMI. LBBB + ongoing ischemic symptoms → manage as STEMI regardless of whether the LBBB is old or new.[8][9][7]

STE in aVR with ≥1 mm ST depression in multiple leads raises concern for left main or triple-vessel disease. Only 10% of this pattern represents acute thrombotic coronary occlusion — hemodynamic instability favors emergent catheterization.[10]

Serial ECGs at 15–30 minute intervals for the first 1–2 hours. 8% of all STEMIs are identified only on repeat ECG. Bedside echo (RWMA) is sensitive but not specific for AMI.[11][12]

STEMI criteria not met but ACS remains on the differential: serial troponins q3–6h, continuous monitoring, Aspirin if not already given, cardiology consult for risk stratification.

Common STEMI mimics: pericarditis (diffuse concave STE, PR depression, no reciprocal changes), hyperkalemia, LVH strain pattern, benign early repolarization, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, Brugada syndrome.[13]

4 · Complications & special populations

Screen for cardiogenic shock, RV involvement (inferior STEMI + hypotension), reperfusion arrhythmias, AV block, and mechanical complications (VSD, papillary muscle rupture, free-wall rupture).

RV infarctionDetail
Incidence33–50% of inferior STEMIs
DiagnosisSTE ≥1 mm in V4R (right-sided leads)
ManagementAVOID nitrates and diuretics; volume resuscitation (NS 250–500 mL boluses); dobutamine/milrinone if refractory

Most common reperfusion arrhythmias: PVCs, VT, AIVR, AF, VF. AIVR is usually a sign of successful reperfusion — well-tolerated, self-limited. Do NOT treat with antidysrhythmics; may cause hemodynamic collapse. VT/VF → defibrillate per ACLS. AV block occurs in 7% of thrombolytic-treated patients — manage bradycardia with atropine or transcutaneous pacing.[23][24]

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT wait for troponin to diagnose STEMI — it is an ECG diagnosis. A negative troponin does not rule it out.[4]

Do NOT give nitrates or diuretics in RV infarct (inferior STEMI with STE ≥1 mm in V4R) — preload drop can cause collapse.[26]

Do NOT give morphine routinely — reserve for refractory pain; it delays P2Y12 absorption and raises mortality.[15][16]

Do NOT treat AIVR after reperfusion — it is a benign sign of successful reperfusion; suppression can cause collapse.[23]

Do NOT miss posterior STEMI — V1–V3 depression with upright T and a tall R wave warrants posterior leads V7–V9.[6]

Do NOT withhold vasopressors in cardiogenic shock for fear of worsening ischemia — the goal is coronary perfusion until revascularization.[25]
