Le Fort Fracture: Airway First, Then Hemorrhage, CSF, and Disposition

Adults presenting to the ED with suspected Le Fort (midface) fracture from blunt facial trauma, confirmed by mobility exam and CT. All Le Fort fractures require pterygoid plate disruption by definition — if the pterygoid plates are intact on imaging, this is NOT a Le Fort fracture and this pathway does not apply.

⚠ All Le Fort fractures involve disruption of the pterygoid plates — airway and hemorrhage are the immediate threats; secure the airway first.
⚠ NEVER nasally intubate or place an NG tube in suspected Le Fort II/III — risk of intracranial passage through the fractured cribriform plate.
⚠ Double setup: have the cricothyrotomy kit OPEN when intubating — CICO risk is high. Limit to 1-2 laryngoscopy attempts before converting to a surgical airway.
⚠ Airway obstruction can be delayed and rapidly worsen — 80% are secured by oro/nasotracheal intubation, 8% require urgent cricothyrotomy, 6% require tracheostomy (43.5% in Le Fort III).
⚠ Massive midface hemorrhage (internal maxillary / sphenopalatine artery) is common, can be life-threatening, and can obscure the airway.
⚠ If nasal packing fails, call interventional radiology early for maxillary artery embolization — do not wait.
⚠ Do not pack the nose if CSF rhinorrhea is present or suspected. Halo sign is nonspecific — confirm with beta-2 transferrin.
⚠ Le Fort III has the highest CSF leak rate and 43.5% eventually require tracheostomy.
⚠ Raccoon eyes (Le Fort II) may be delayed 1-3 days — their absence does not rule out injury. Le Fort II is associated with increased mortality compared to isolated facial fractures.
⚠ C-spine injury until proven otherwise — maintain inline stabilization until cleared.
⚠ Orbital compartment syndrome is an emergency (tense globe with proptosis, decreased visual acuity, afferent pupillary defect, or IOP >40 mmHg) — do not delay lateral canthotomy for imaging.

1 · Classification — pterygoid rule & mobility exam

Le Fort fractures are midface fractures involving separation of the maxilla from the skull base.[1][2] All Le Fort fractures disrupt the pterygoid plates — if the pterygoid plates are intact, it is NOT a Le Fort fracture. Mechanism: motor vehicle collisions (most common), assault/interpersonal violence, falls from height, sports injuries. Mixed patterns — different Le Fort levels on each side — occur in 56.5% of cases.

Mobility exam: place fingers on the nasal bridge while mobilizing the maxillary central incisors with the other hand.[1][2]

FindingFracture Type
Only maxilla movesLe Fort I
Maxilla + nose move togetherLe Fort II
Entire midface + zygoma moveLe Fort III
Le Fort I — floating palate
Also called Guerin fracture. Transverse fracture through the maxilla above the tooth roots, crossing the lateral nasal walls, inferior maxillary sinus walls, and pterygoid plates. Unique identifier: fracture of the anterolateral margin of the nasal fossa. Signs: swollen upper lip, anterior open bite malocclusion, ecchymosis of the maxillary buccal vestibule and palate, mobile dental arch, epistaxis.
Le Fort II — pyramidal fracture
Pyramid-shaped fracture with base at the teeth, apex at the nasofrontal suture — nasal bones, medial orbital walls, orbital floor, infraorbital rims, lateral maxillary walls, pterygoid plates. Unique identifier: fracture of the inferior orbital rim. Signs: bilateral periorbital edema and ecchymosis ("raccoon eyes"), palpable step-off at the infraorbital rim, infraorbital nerve paresthesia, possible CSF rhinorrhea. Le Fort II is associated with increased mortality compared to isolated facial fractures. Raccoon eyes may be delayed 1-3 days — their absence does not rule out injury.
Le Fort III — craniofacial disjunction
Also called floating face. Transverse fracture through the nasofrontal suture, medial and lateral orbital walls, zygomaticofrontal suture, zygomatic arches, pterygoid plates. Unique identifier: zygomatic arch fracture. Signs: "dish-face deformity," traumatic telecanthus, mastoid ecchymosis (Battle sign) if basilar skull involved, CSF rhinorrhea/otorrhea (highest leak rate), hemotympanum, anosmia. 43.5% eventually require tracheostomy.
Mixed pattern — treat to the highest level
Mixed patterns occur in 56.5% of cases — different Le Fort levels on each side. Manage to the HIGHEST level present (e.g., Le Fort II on one side + Le Fort III on the other → manage as Le Fort III). CT with 3D reconstruction is essential for classification and surgical planning.

Significant hemorrhage → packing sequence
Direct pressure and hemostatic sutures first, then anterior nasal packing (epinephrine-soaked gauze or a commercial device), then posterior packing/Foley balloon tamponade (10-15 mL inflated in the nasopharynx with anterior traction) if anterior packing fails. Posterior packing requires admission, telemetry, and sometimes intubation.
Minimal bleeding → proceed to CSF and associated-injury workup

Endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation is an alternative (>85% success, lower complication risk, requires OR/GA). TXA 1 g IV is a reasonable adjunct for significant hemorrhage in trauma.

3 · Airway management

Mechanisms of airway compromise: posterior-inferior displacement of the fractured midface into the oropharynx, hemorrhage flooding the upper airway, soft tissue edema (may be delayed), foreign bodies (teeth, bone fragments, blood, vomitus). 80% are secured by oro/nasotracheal intubation, 8% require urgent cricothyrotomy, 6% require tracheostomy; Le Fort III: 43.5% eventually require tracheostomy.

CRITICAL CONTRAINDICATION: NEVER nasally intubate or place an NG tube in suspected Le Fort II/III — risk of intracranial passage through the fractured cribriform plate. Preferred approaches: video laryngoscopy with copious suction (first-line), awake intubation with ketamine, retromolar intubation, surgical airway (cricothyrotomy) for a CICO scenario. Double setup: prepare for both orotracheal intubation AND cricothyrotomy simultaneously. Manual reduction of the displaced maxilla forward can temporize obstruction and reduce bleeding.

Positioning: maintain C-spine precautions (inline stabilization), slight reverse Trendelenburg if possible, suction immediately available. Technique: preoxygenate, video laryngoscopy with large-bore suction, manual midface reduction by an assistant if needed, limit to 1-2 attempts before surgical airway. Double setup: cricothyrotomy kit OPEN, scalpel in assistant's hand, bougie ready, 6.0 cuffed ETT ready. Avoid succinylcholine if there is concern for hyperkalemia (crush injury).

4 · Hemorrhage control

Le Fort II and III have higher hemorrhage risk than other facial injuries. Common sources: internal maxillary artery, sphenopalatine artery branches. Can be life-threatening and obscure the airway.

StepTechnique
1. Direct pressure & hemostatic suturesControl lacerations; pack oral cavity if needed
2. Anterior nasal packingGauze soaked in 1:10,000 epinephrine; commercial devices (Rapid Rhino, Merocel); can leave up to 48 hours
3. Posterior packing / balloon tamponadeFoley catheter inflated 10-15 mL in the nasopharynx with anterior traction; double-balloon catheters 70% success; overall success 48-83%; caution: intracranial malposition risk if skull base fractured; requires admission, telemetry (nasal-cardiac reflex), sometimes intubation

If packing fails: interventional radiology superselective embolization of maxillary artery branches (~90% success; complications: monocular blindness, stroke, rare but serious, ~2%). Endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation (>85% success, lower complication risk than embolization, requires OR/general anesthesia). Manual reduction: grasp the maxilla through the mouth and pull anteriorly.

5 · CSF leak, c-spine, imaging, ocular

CSF rhinorrhea/otorrhea indicates a skull base/cribriform plate fracture. Halo test: clear fluid diffusing around a blood drop on filter paper — nonspecific (mucus and tears can also create halos). Confirmatory: beta-2 transferrin or beta-trace protein. 40.7% have a concurrent skull fracture; highest leak rate in Le Fort III. Management: neurosurgery consult, head-of-bed elevation, avoid nose blowing and straining; prophylactic antibiotics controversial — discuss with neurosurgery.

C-spine injury in 1.4-5.4% of midface fractures (C5-C7 commonly involved); maintain inline stabilization until cleared. Le Fort III has the highest association with cervical spine, intracranial, and neck vascular injuries.

CT face (gold standard): fine-cut (2-3mm), axial/coronal/sagittal planes, bone and soft tissue windows. Key findings: pterygoid plate fractures (mandatory for diagnosis), anterolateral nasal fossa (Le Fort I), inferior orbital rim (Le Fort II), zygomatic arch (Le Fort III). 3D reconstruction is the best method for classification and is essential for surgical planning. Additional required imaging: CT head (required — 40.7% have skull fractures, 5.4% TBI), CT C-spine (required, maintain immobilization until cleared), CT angiography if penetrating trauma or vascular injury suspected.

Ocular injury present in 24-28% of facial fractures, higher risk with Le Fort II and III. Examine for pupil irregularity, hyphema, restricted extraocular movements, proptosis vs enophthalmos, visual acuity changes, afferent pupillary defect. Orbital compartment syndrome is an EMERGENCY requiring emergent lateral canthotomy if tense globe with proptosis, decreased visual acuity, afferent pupillary defect, or IOP >40 mmHg — do not delay decompression for imaging. Ophthalmology consult for all Le Fort II/III fractures.

Critical pitfalls7 items
Do NOT assume a Le Fort fracture if pterygoid plates are intact — all Le Fort fractures involve pterygoid plate disruption; if intact, this is NOT a Le Fort fracture.
Do NOT nasally intubate or place an NG tube in suspected Le Fort II/III — risk of intracranial passage through the fractured cribriform plate.
Do NOT intubate without a double setup — have the cricothyrotomy kit OPEN before attempting laryngoscopy; CICO risk is high.
Do NOT clear C-spine by assumption — maintain inline stabilization until formally cleared; treat as injured until proven otherwise.
Do NOT rule out Le Fort II because raccoon eyes are absent — periorbital ecchymosis may be delayed 1-3 days.
Do NOT trust a positive halo sign alone — it is nonspecific (mucus and tears also halo); confirm CSF with beta-2 transferrin, and do not pack the nose if a CSF leak is present.
Do NOT underestimate Le Fort II — it carries increased mortality compared to isolated facial fractures despite being a lower classification number than Le Fort III.

OMFS + specialty consults per injury pattern — OMFS all; neurosurgery for CSF leak/skull base; ophthalmology for orbital involvement; trauma surgery, ENT, IR as indicated. Rare discharge: stable Le Fort I, reliable follow-up.

Disposition: admit; rare stable Le Fort I discharge — Admit for observation, OMFS consult, CT face 3D recon, CT head/C-spine, ophthalmology if orbital involvement.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT assume a Le Fort fracture if pterygoid plates are intact — all Le Fort fractures involve pterygoid plate disruption; if intact, this is NOT a Le Fort fracture.

Do NOT nasally intubate or place an NG tube in suspected Le Fort II/III — risk of intracranial passage through the fractured cribriform plate.

Do NOT intubate without a double setup — have the cricothyrotomy kit OPEN before attempting laryngoscopy; CICO risk is high.

Do NOT clear C-spine by assumption — maintain inline stabilization until formally cleared; treat as injured until proven otherwise.

Do NOT rule out Le Fort II because raccoon eyes are absent — periorbital ecchymosis may be delayed 1-3 days.

Do NOT trust a positive halo sign alone — it is nonspecific (mucus and tears also halo); confirm CSF with beta-2 transferrin, and do not pack the nose if a CSF leak is present.

Do NOT underestimate Le Fort II — it carries increased mortality compared to isolated facial fractures despite being a lower classification number than Le Fort III.
