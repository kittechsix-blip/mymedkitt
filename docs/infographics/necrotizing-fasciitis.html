Adults ≥18 with suspected or confirmed necrotizing soft tissue infection (NSTI) in the ED or ICU. Excludes pediatric-specific dosing and simple cellulitis without red flags — see the cellulitis branch for that lower-acuity pathway.

1 · Recognize NSTI

What NSTI is
Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infection (NSTI) is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening infection of the fascia and subcutaneous tissue. Mortality 25–35% even with treatment.[1][2] This is a clinical and surgical diagnosis — labs and imaging may support suspicion but CANNOT rule it out. When in doubt, get surgery to the bedside.[2][3]
Hard signs — surgical emergency
Hemorrhagic bullae (~96% specific), crepitus/gas on imaging, skin necrosis or violaceous discoloration, and "dishwater" gray drainage are high-specificity findings. Call surgery NOW — do not wait for imaging, labs, or LRINEC.[2][3]
Soft signs — maintain high suspicion
Pain out of proportion is the most sensitive finding (~90%) but can be difficult to assess. Also: rapidly spreading erythema/edema, edema extending beyond erythema, systemic toxicity with focal pain. Risk factors: diabetes (most common), immunocompromise, IV drug use, recent surgery/trauma, obesity, PVD, cirrhosis.[1][2][4]

CT with IV contrast is preferred: ~94% sensitive, 77% specific — look for gas tracking along fascial planes, fascial thickening, non-enhancing fascia. Normal CT does NOT rule out early NSTI — never delay surgery for imaging when the clinical picture is clear.[2][6]

LRINEC ≥6 raises suspicion (~92% PPV in the original study), but scores <6 do NOT rule out NSTI — Canadian guidelines recommend against using LRINEC for decision-making. Use as an adjunct only; clinical suspicion always trumps the score.[2][5]

2 · Resuscitation & surgical urgency

  1. IV access — 2 large-bore IVs or central line.
  2. 30 mL/kg LR bolus within 3 hours.[2][7]
  3. Broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour (regimen in section 3).
  4. Labs: CBC, BMP, lactate, CRP, coags, blood cultures ×2, wound cultures.
  5. Mark wound margins with time — reassess for progression.

Vasopressors if needed: Norepinephrine 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min first-line; add Vasopressin 0.03–0.04 units/min early. Target MAP ≥65 mmHg.[2][7]

Do NOT delay surgery for
Complete resuscitation, hemodynamic stability, culture results, or additional imaging. Surgical debridement is the single most important intervention.[2][3]
Surgical consultation — STAT
Call surgery IMMEDIATELY. Bedside finger test (2 cm incision to fascia, probe for lack of tissue resistance / dishwater drainage) can be performed by the ED physician if surgery is delayed — false-negative rate ~20%, so a negative test does not rule out NSTI. Expect wide debridement, extension until healthy bleeding tissue, and typically multiple return trips to OR every 24–48 h. Each hour of surgical delay increases mortality.[2][3]

3 · Empiric antimicrobial therapy

Triple-drug regimen, covering gram-positives (incl. MRSA), gram-negatives, and anaerobes:[2][3][10]

1. Backbone: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h (preferred) OR Meropenem 1 g IV q8h (resistant organisms suspected)
2. MRSA coverage: Linezolid 600 mg IV q12h — PREFERRED, superior toxin suppression, avoids nephrotoxicity, no levels to monitor — OR Vancomycin 25–30 mg/kg IV load, then 15–20 mg/kg IV q8–12h
3. Toxin suppression (add to ALL regimens): Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h

—Vancomycin loading dose (25 mg/kg) — if used instead of linezolid
—Vancomycin loading dose (30 mg/kg) — if used instead of linezolid
—Vancomycin maintenance (15 mg/kg/dose, q8–12h)
Discontinue vancomycin if no MRSA is cultured within 48 hours. Duration overall: 7–14 days total, continuing 48–72 hours after final debridement with clinical improvement.[10]

Flip Special exposure above if the wound was exposed to saltwater/raw oysters or freshwater/leeches — these require ADDING agents to the empiric regimen above, not replacing it.

Vibrio vulnificus — saltwater exposure
>50% mortality. Risk factors: cirrhosis (100× risk), hemochromatosis, immunocompromise, raw oyster ingestion. Extremely rapid progression (hours), hemorrhagic bullae characteristic, septic shock common at presentation. Add: Doxycycline 100 mg IV q12h PLUS Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h OR Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h.[4][11]
Aeromonas hydrophila — freshwater exposure
Risk factors: freshwater injury, immunocompromise, liver disease, medicinal leech therapy. Often resistant to ampicillin and first-generation cephalosporins. Add: Doxycycline 100 mg IV q12h PLUS Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h OR Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h.[4]

No special exposure — proceed with the standard empiric regimen above; cultures will guide de-escalation (section 5).

4 · Adjunctive therapies

Clindamycin — toxin suppression is critical
Inhibits bacterial protein synthesis at the 50S ribosome, suppressing exotoxin production; effect persists even as bacteria die. Beta-lactams may paradoxically INCREASE toxin release (Eagle effect). Some experts use 1200 mg IV q6h for severe TSS. Continue throughout the antibiotic course in toxin-producing infections (GAS, TSS, Clostridial).[2][3]

Flip Streptococcal TSS / refractory shock above for critically ill patients with suspected streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.

Dual toxin suppression
For critically ill patients with streptococcal TSS, consider Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h PLUS Linezolid 600 mg IV q12h — different ribosomal binding sites, potentially synergistic.[2]
IVIG — streptococcal TSS
1 g/kg IV on Day 1, then 0.5 g/kg IV on Days 2–3 (or 2 g/kg total divided over 1–5 days). Neutralizes circulating superantigens. Evidence is mixed — a Canadian observational study showed OR 8.1 for survival, but a larger US study showed no mortality benefit. WSES guidelines recommend IVIG for streptococcal/staphylococcal NSTI with TSS or refractory shock.[2][12]

Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO) is controversial — no RCTs, only observational data with selection bias (only stable patients can be transferred). NEVER delay surgical debridement for HBO. Consider only as an adjunct after initial debridement in a stable patient.[13]

5 · Monitoring, de-escalation & disposition

  1. Serial wound exams every 4–6 hours initially — mark borders with time at each exam.
  2. Trend lactate, WBC, CRP (expect improvement by 48–72 h). Watch renal function and coagulation (DIC common).
  3. Return to OR typically every 24–48 hours for re-look until no further necrotic tissue is found — may require 3–5+ debridements.
  4. Persistent/worsening shock, rising lactate, or expanding wound margins signal inadequate source control — re-evaluate debridement adequacy and antibiotic coverage.

De-escalate by culture results (typically 48–72 h): GAS → Penicillin G 4 million units IV q4h PLUS clindamycin (continue for toxin suppression). MRSA → vancomycin or linezolid PLUS clindamycin if toxin-producing. Clostridial → Penicillin G 4 million units IV q4h PLUS clindamycin. Total duration 7–14 days, continuing IV therapy 48–72 hours after final debridement.[10]

Disposition — ICU for ALL
ALL patients with confirmed or suspected NSTI require ICU admission — hemodynamic instability, post-operative monitoring, serial wound assessments, and potential return to OR. Multidisciplinary team: surgery, ID, critical care, wound care, PT/OT, social work, psychology. Notify family early.[2][3]
If NSTI is confidently excluded — simple cellulitis
Mark wound borders, treat with Cephalexin 500 mg PO q6h × 5–7 days (or TMP-SMX if MRSA risk), and give mandatory return precautions: erythema spreading beyond marks, increasing pain, fever, bullae, or any systemic symptoms. Follow-up within 24–48 hours is mandatory.[2]

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT delay surgical debridement for imaging, labs, or LRINEC scoring — each hour of delay increases mortality.[3]

Do NOT trust a normal LRINEC (<6) to rule out NSTI — poor sensitivity; clinical suspicion always trumps the score.[2][5]

Do NOT trust a normal CT to rule out early NSTI — early disease may have minimal CT findings.[2][6]

Do NOT omit clindamycin from any NSTI regimen — beta-lactams alone may paradoxically increase toxin release (Eagle effect).[2][3]

Do NOT delay repeat debridement for hyperbaric oxygen — HBO has no RCT evidence and is never a substitute for surgery.[13]
