Necrotizing Fasciitis: A Surgical Diagnosis

Adults in the ED with suspected necrotizing soft tissue infection (NSTI). A clinical/surgical decision aid — not a rule-out tool. Excludes isolated pediatric dosing beyond the weight-based references shown, and does not replace bedside surgical evaluation.

⚠ Hard signs = surgical emergency — call surgery NOW. Do not delay for imaging or labs. Hemorrhagic bullae are ~96% specific for NSTI.
⚠ These patients are septic. 30 mL/kg LR, broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour, norepinephrine if MAP <65 — but do NOT delay surgery for resuscitation.
⚠ Each hour of surgical delay increases mortality. Call surgery STAT; if surgery is delayed, a bedside finger test can help; expect multiple OR trips.
⚠ Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h is critical for toxin suppression — add it to every NSTI regimen alongside pip-tazo + linezolid (or vancomycin).
⚠ A normal LRINEC score AND a normal CT do NOT rule out NSTI. Clinical suspicion trumps labs and imaging — do not delay surgery for either.
⚠ Fournier gangrene (perineal NSTI) carries ~40% mortality — always include broad gram-negative + anaerobic + MRSA coverage; surgical debridement is mandatory.
⚠ Head/neck NSTI threatens the airway and can descend to the mediastinum — early intubation, consult ENT and thoracic surgery; often odontogenic.
⚠ Saltwater or oyster exposure + hemorrhagic bullae = Vibrio vulnificus (>50% mortality) — add doxycycline + a 3rd-generation cephalosporin.

1 · Recognize — a clinical & surgical diagnosis

What NSTI is
Necrotizing soft tissue infection (NSTI) is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening infection of fascia and subcutaneous tissue with mortality of 25-35% even with treatment.[1][2] It is a CLINICAL and SURGICAL diagnosis — labs and imaging may support suspicion but cannot rule it out. When in doubt, get surgery to the bedside.[2][3]

Pain out of proportion is the most sensitive finding (~90%). Hemorrhagic bullae are among the most specific, at ~96%.[2] Risk factors: diabetes (most common), immunocompromise, IV drug use, recent surgery or trauma, obesity.[1][4]

3 · Resuscitate & get surgery

Treat as septic shock
30 mL/kg LR within 3 hours, broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour, blood cultures ×2 and wound cultures, lactate, CBC/BMP/CRP/coags. Do NOT delay surgery for resuscitation or imaging.[2][7]
Norepinephrine (first-line pressor if MAP <65)
0.05-0.5 mcg/kg/min
IV titrated
Add vasopressin 0.03-0.04 units/min early for refractory shock. Target MAP ≥65 mmHg.
Surgery is the treatment
Call surgery STAT — each hour of surgical delay increases mortality. Debridement, not antibiotics, controls the source. If surgery is delayed, a bedside finger test (2 cm incision to fascia; lack of resistance + dishwater drainage = positive) can support the diagnosis, but its ~20% false-negative rate means a negative test does not rule out NSTI.[2][3]
Imaging — CT with contrast (adjunct only)94% sens · 77% spec

CT with IV contrast is the imaging of choice: sensitivity ~94%, specificity ~77%.[2][6] Look for gas tracking along fascial planes (most specific), fascial thickening, non-enhancing fascia, and vessel thrombosis.

A normal CT does NOT rule out early NSTI. Do not delay surgery for imaging when the clinical diagnosis is clear.[2]

4 · Antibiotics — triple therapy + toxin suppression

Empiric triple-drug therapy
Cover gram-positives (incl. MRSA), gram-negatives, and anaerobes, plus a toxin-suppressing agent:[2][3][10]

1. Backbone: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV q6h (or Meropenem 1 g IV q8h).
2. MRSA: Linezolid 600 mg IV q12h (preferred — superior toxin suppression) or Vancomycin.
3. Toxin suppression: Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h — add to every regimen.
Why clindamycin is non-negotiable
Clindamycin suppresses exotoxin production and its effect persists as bacteria die. Beta-lactams alone may paradoxically increase toxin release (Eagle effect). Give 900 mg IV q8h to all suspected/confirmed NSTI; some experts use 1200 mg IV q6h in severe toxic shock.[2][3]

Drug-store weight-based reference of 15 mg/kg (÷ 4/day). For NSTI, MRSA coverage typically uses a 25-30 mg/kg load then 15-20 mg/kg maintenance dosed to trough — linezolid is preferred for toxin suppression.
ADD for saltwater / Vibrio vulnificus
Vibrio vulnificus (cirrhosis = 100× risk, >50% mortality). ADD to empiric therapy: Doxycycline 100 mg IV q12h plus Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h (or Ceftazidime).[4][11]
ADD for freshwater / Aeromonas
Aeromonas hydrophila (freshwater, leech therapy; resistant to ampicillin). ADD to empiric therapy: Doxycycline 100 mg IV q12h plus Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h (or Levofloxacin 750 mg IV q24h).[4]

5 · Special situations

Fournier gangrene — perineal NSTI (~40% mortality)polymicrobial

NSTI of the perineum, scrotum, and genital region; mortality ~40%.[4][8] Polymicrobial — always include broad gram-negative AND anaerobic coverage. May require fecal diversion (colostomy); testes are usually spared (separate blood supply). Surgical debridement is mandatory.

Cervical / head-neck NSTI — airway firstmediastinitis risk
Often odontogenic. Airway compromise is the primary threat and it can descend to the mediastinum. CT neck AND chest with contrast; early intubation (awake fiberoptic if concern); consult ENT and thoracic surgery.[9]
