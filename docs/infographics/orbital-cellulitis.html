Orbital vs Preseptal Cellulitis: Any Orbital Sign Changes Everything

Adults and children in the ED with periorbital or orbital swelling and suspected preseptal or orbital cellulitis. Excludes isolated hordeolum/chalazion without cellulitis, allergic/angioedema periorbital swelling, and post-traumatic orbital compartment syndrome (separate emergent pathway).

⚠ ANY orbital sign — proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, vision change, pain with eye movement, chemosis, or an afferent pupillary defect — means treat as orbital cellulitis until proven otherwise. Do NOT delay imaging or IV antibiotics.
⚠ Bilateral signs, rapidly progressive proptosis/chemosis, CN VI palsy (earliest and most common), complete ophthalmoplegia, V1/V2 sensory loss, or severe headache with fever suggest cavernous sinus thrombosis (Chandler V) — mortality 20-30% even with treatment. A unilateral presentation can become bilateral within 24-48 hours. MRI with MR venography is the gold standard.
⚠ Orbital (intraconal) abscess (Chandler IV) is a surgical emergency — emergent ophthalmology and ENT consultation with surgical drainage indicated; consider neurosurgery if intracranial extension.
⚠ ICU admission is required for cavernous sinus thrombosis, sepsis, altered mental status, intracranial extension, or orbital compartment syndrome — with neurosurgery, ophthalmology, ENT, and infectious disease consultation.
⚠ Cavernous sinus thrombosis treatment uses a distinct HIGH-DOSE regimen — Ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h (CNS-penetration dosing), plus high-dose vancomycin and metronidazole. This is NOT the standard orbital-cellulitis adult ceftriaxone dose of 2g IV q24h — using the standard dose in CST under-doses a life-threatening infection. 4-6+ weeks IV, guided by infectious disease.

3 · Preseptal pathway

Preseptal cellulitis does NOT progress to orbital cellulitis due to the protective orbital septum. Causes: local skin trauma, upper respiratory infection, chalazion/hordeolum, impetigo, dacryocystitis. Microbiology: *S. aureus* (incl. MRSA), *Streptococcus*, *H. influenzae* (declining with vaccination). If uncertain, image to rule out orbital involvement.[1][3]

4 · Orbital imaging & staging

CT orbits with IV contrast, thin-cut (2-3mm) axial and coronal, including sinuses. Indications: proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, diplopia, vision loss, pain with eye movement, ANC >10,000/µL, age <1 year with any periorbital infection, no improvement 24-48h on antibiotics, CNS signs, or unable to adequately examine the eye.[1][2][4]

Chandler IV: orbital (intraconal) abscess — surgical emergency
Ring-enhancing intraconal collection, marked proptosis, severe mass effect. Emergent ophthalmology + ENT consultation, IV antibiotics, surgical drainage indicated. Consider neurosurgery if intracranial extension. Complications: vision loss, cavernous sinus thrombosis, intracranial extension, sepsis.[1][2][4]
  • Unilateral signs → bilateral within 24-48 hours (pathognomonic).
  • CN VI palsy — earliest and most common — limited abduction.
  • Complete ophthalmoplegia (CN III, IV, VI).
  • V1/V2 sensory loss (periorbital numbness), decreased corneal reflex.
  • Severe retro-orbital/frontal headache, high fever, sepsis.
  • Altered mental status, meningismus, papilledema.

CST treatment — distinct high-dose regimen (do NOT use standard orbital dosing)

Consider anticoagulation (controversial) and dexamethasone for cerebral edema (controversial). Drain primary sinus source. Duration: 4-6+ weeks IV, guided by infectious disease.[1][2][5]

Adjunctive management5 items
  • Nasal decongestant (oxymetazoline 0.05% spray BID × 3 days) to promote sinus drainage.
  • Nasal saline irrigation to help clear sinuses.
  • Head elevation 30-45 degrees to reduce orbital edema.
  • Pain control — acetaminophen/ibuprofen; consider opioids for severe pain.
  • ENT consultation — sinus source in >90% of cases; may need endoscopic sinus surgery, especially if frontal sinusitis. Avoid warm compresses if abscess suspected (may promote spread).

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT** attribute lid swelling to preseptal cellulitis without checking vision, EOM, and pupils — any orbital sign changes the entire pathway.

**Do NOT** delay CT orbits with IV contrast or IV antibiotics while awaiting ophthalmology/ENT consultation — start both first.

**Do NOT** use warm compresses on a suspected abscess — may promote spread.

**Do NOT** discharge a preseptal cellulitis patient without confirmed 24-48 hour follow-up — this is mandatory, not optional.

**Do NOT** miss cavernous sinus thrombosis — a unilateral presentation can become bilateral within 24-48 hours; CN VI palsy (limited abduction) is the earliest sign, often before complete ophthalmoplegia.

**Do NOT** wait for imaging alone to reassess — vision loss, new APD, increasing proptosis, new ophthalmoplegia, or bilateral signs demand immediate re-evaluation regardless of imaging timing.

**Do NOT** stop antibiotics at symptom improvement alone — total duration is 3-4 weeks minimum (longer for ethmoid sinusitis with bone destruction).
