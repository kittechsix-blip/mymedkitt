Sports Injuries — ED Triage & Management

Adults and pediatric athletes presenting to the ED with an acute sports-related injury, spanning triage red flags, concussion/c-spine, shoulder, knee, ankle/foot, and pediatric physeal-injury pathways. Excludes chest pain or syncope during exertion, which requires cardiac workup rather than routine sports-injury management.

⚠ Loss of consciousness, persistent confusion, seizure, or high-energy mechanism bypasses routine triage — go directly to the head/c-spine pathway.
⚠ Open fracture or neurovascular compromise distal to the injury needs orthopedics now, not routine workup.
⚠ Acute joint dislocation that has NOT spontaneously reduced needs urgent reduction, not conservative management.
⚠ Chest pain or syncope during exertion is a cardiac workup, NOT a routine sports complaint.
⚠ Concussion: no same-day return to play — stepwise progression over a minimum of 6 days, no shortcuts.
⚠ Pediatric tenderness near a physis is a Salter-Harris fracture until proven otherwise — ligaments are stronger than physes.

Overview — first triage

First decision on any sports injury: are there red flags that bypass routine workup? Screen for head injury/concussion, cervical spine injury, open fracture/neurovascular compromise, and unreduced joint dislocation before moving to a routine extremity workup.

Critical bypass triggers
Loss of consciousness, persistent confusion, or seizure → head/concussion pathway. Suspected cervical spine injury (axial neck pain, neurologic deficit, high-energy mechanism) → immobilize and image. Open fracture or neurovascular compromise distal to injury → orthopedics now. Unreduced joint dislocation → urgent reduction. Chest pain or syncope during exertion → cardiac workup, NOT a routine sports complaint.

Open fracture / vascular compromise

  1. Stop active bleeding (direct pressure, tourniquet for life-threatening hemorrhage)
  2. Reduce gross deformity if pulse absent and orthopedics delayed (gentle longitudinal traction)
  3. Cover open wound with sterile saline-soaked gauze
  4. IV access, tetanus, first-dose antibiotic (see dose below); add aminoglycoside for grossly contaminated wounds
  5. NPO, type & screen, plain films, advanced imaging if pulse not restored
  6. Orthopedic surgery consult NOW; vascular surgery if pulse asymmetry persists

Acute joint dislocation

Joint-specific dislocation pathways live in the joint consult trees: Shoulder dislocation for anterior/posterior reduction technique and sling care, and Wrist injuries for the perilunate/lunate dislocation pathway. Patellar dislocation reduces with knee extension + medial pressure on the patella. Hip dislocation needs urgent reduction — avascular necrosis risk if delayed >6 hours.

General principles
Procedural sedation as needed, post-reduction films, orthopedics follow-up, sling/splint per joint.

Head injury / concussion / c-spine

Concerning features mandating CT
LOC >1 minute, persistent confusion or amnesia >30 min, severe/worsening headache, repeated vomiting, focal neurologic deficit, seizure, high-energy mechanism, anticoagulation/coagulopathy, or suspected skull fracture (basilar signs, palpable depression).

For adults, use the Canadian CT Head Rule or New Orleans Criteria. For children, use PECARN age-stratified criteria. While awaiting imaging: c-spine precautions if mechanism warrants, anticoagulation reversal if applicable (e.g. warfarin → 4F-PCC + vitamin K), and repeat neuro exam every 15–30 minutes.

CT-guided disposition
CT positive → neurosurgery consult, admit. CT negative + persistent symptoms → admit for observation if anticoagulated; otherwise discharge with strict head-injury precautions and concussion follow-up.

Use the concussion symptom checklist at presentation and again before discharge. Discharge criteria (no CT indicated): GCS 15, normal neuro exam, symptoms improving, a reliable observer at home, and strict instructions to return for any neurologic decline.

  1. Day 1: symptom-limited activity
  2. Day 2: light aerobic activity
  3. Day 3: sport-specific drills
  4. Day 4: non-contact training
  5. Day 5: full-contact practice (medical clearance required)
  6. Day 6: return to play
Stepwise return-to-play — minimum 6 days
No symptoms at any step before advancing. Any symptoms reappearing → drop back to the previous step for 24 hours. No same-day return to play.

Use NEXUS or the Canadian C-Spine Rule to determine need for imaging. NEXUS: no midline tenderness, no focal neuro deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication, no distracting injury → no imaging. Canadian C-Spine: age >65, dangerous mechanism, or paresthesias → image; otherwise low-risk factors permit clinical clearance. If any criterion fails → maintain immobilization, CT cervical spine; MRI if persistent pain with normal CT or any neurologic finding.

Helmet and shoulder pad removal
In athletes wearing helmets/shoulder pads, follow sport-specific equipment-removal protocols. Football helmet + shoulder pads must be removed together to maintain spinal alignment — never pull the helmet off alone.

Shoulder injuries

Inspection: asymmetry, deformity, swelling. Palpation: AC joint, sternoclavicular joint, biceps groove, greater tuberosity. ROM: forward flexion, abduction, internal/external rotation — pain arc 60–120° suggests impingement, pain at end-range suggests AC joint. Provocative tests: Neer, Hawkins-Kennedy (impingement), empty can (supraspinatus), apprehension/relocation (anterior instability), cross-body adduction (AC joint). Neurovascular: axillary nerve (lateral deltoid sensation, deltoid contraction), radial pulse. Non-localizing exam or severe trauma → AP + axillary or scapular Y plain films.

A direct blow or fall on the adducted shoulder produces an AC joint injury graded I–VI by Rockwood. Plain films (AP, Zanca view) confirm the grade.

Tendinopathy (overuse): pain with overhead activity, painful arc, positive Neer/Hawkins → relative rest, NSAIDs, structured PT (4–6 wk). Acute tear (often after a fall or sudden lift): weakness with empty can / external rotation, drop-arm sign — MRI for diagnosis if a conservative trial fails or weakness is severe. Disposition: sling for comfort, NSAIDs, sports medicine or orthopedics follow-up within 1–2 weeks. Complete inability to elevate the arm → urgent orthopedic referral (massive tear).

Most fractures (middle third, <100% displaced, <2 cm shortening): sling, ice, NSAIDs, early ROM at 2 wk, ortho follow-up. Surgical indications: open fracture, skin tenting/threat, neurovascular compromise, distal third with coracoclavicular ligament disruption, or significant displacement (>2 cm shortening) in an athlete or laborer. Obtain AP and 30° cephalad films; examine for pneumothorax and brachial plexus injury.

Plain films (AP + axillary or scapular Y): trauma with deformity, suspected fracture, suspected dislocation, or persistent symptoms after 2–3 weeks of conservative care. MRI: suspected rotator cuff tear with weakness, suspected labral injury (recurrent dislocations, mechanical symptoms), or persistent pain after normal X-ray and conservative trial. ED MRI is rarely indicated unless septic arthritis, abscess, or massive cuff tear with functional loss is a concern.

Knee injuries

Ottawa Knee Rule — image the knee if any of: age ≥55, isolated patellar tenderness, tenderness at the fibular head, inability to flex to 90°, or inability to bear weight (4 steps) immediately AND in the ED. Use the calculator below to score this patient.

—Ottawa Knee criteria met

AP, lateral, and skyline patellar views. Add tibial plateau views if posterolateral pain or a high-energy mechanism (Lipscomb sign). If fracture present: tibial plateau/patella displaced → orthopedics; avulsion fragment with instability → consider a Segond fracture (pathognomonic for ACL tear); open fracture → the vascular pathway. If no fracture but clinically unstable (positive Lachman or pivot shift) → outpatient MRI within 1 week.

Mechanism narrows the differential: pop + immediate effusion + giving way → ACL. Twisting + locking + joint-line tenderness → meniscus. Direct lateral blow + medial pain → MCL. Hyperextension + posterior pain → PCL. Patellar dislocation history → patellofemoral instability. Key tests: Lachman (most sensitive for ACL), McMurray (meniscus), valgus/varus stress, posterior drawer, patellar apprehension.

Ottawa-rules-positive or normal X-ray: rest, ice, NSAIDs, knee immobilizer, crutches, weight-bear as tolerated. Outpatient MRI within 1 week and orthopedics referral. Do not promise a return-to-play timeline in the ED — surgical management (BTB or hamstring graft) typically requires 6–9 months of rehab; some non-pivoting athletes manage non-operatively.

Segond fracture pearl
A Segond fracture on plain film is highly specific for an ACL tear.

Findings: joint-line tenderness, positive McMurray, locked knee (mechanical block to extension). ED workup: plain films to rule out fracture; if locked, prompt orthopedic consult — may need urgent surgical reduction. Disposition (non-locked): rest, ice, NSAIDs, range-of-motion exercises, outpatient MRI and ortho referral within 1–2 weeks.

Graded I (sprain) to III (complete tear). Grade I/II: hinged knee brace, weight-bear as tolerated, NSAIDs, PT, return to sport in 2–6 weeks. Grade III: brace at 30° flexion, ortho referral; surgical repair occasionally needed (especially LCL with posterolateral corner injury). Isolated LCL injuries are rare — always evaluate the posterolateral corner and PCL when the LCL is torn.

Reduction: extend the knee + medial pressure on the patella; procedural sedation rarely needed. Post-reduction films (AP, lateral, skyline) evaluate for osteochondral fracture; MRI if locked, hemarthrosis, or recurrent. Disposition: knee immobilizer in extension, weight-bear as tolerated, outpatient orthopedics within 1 week, PT for VMO strengthening.

Ankle / foot injuries

Ottawa Ankle Rule — image if any of: bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus, bone tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal or navicular (foot rule), or inability to bear weight 4 steps immediately AND in the ED. Sensitivity ~98% for clinically significant fractures.

—Ottawa Ankle criteria met

Ankle: AP, lateral, mortise. Foot: AP, lateral, oblique. Unstable/displaced → orthopedics, splint, non-weight-bearing.

Most are lateral ankle sprains (ATFL > CFL > PTFL). Treatment: PRICE (protection, relative rest, ice, compression, elevation), functional bracing or lace-up brace (better than rigid immobilization for return-to-sport), weight-bear as tolerated, early ROM + proprioception PT, short-course NSAIDs. High ankle sprain (syndesmosis): positive squeeze test, external rotation pain — longer recovery, may need a boot, ortho follow-up. Persistent pain >6 weeks → MRI for occult fracture, osteochondral lesion, or syndesmotic injury.

Pediatric considerations

Open growth plates change everything: ligaments are stronger than physes, so what looks like a "sprain" in an adult may be a Salter-Harris fracture in a child. Apophyseal injuries (Osgood-Schlatter, Sever, Little League shoulder/elbow) are overuse — relative rest, gradual return.

Physes fail before ligaments
Tenderness at the physis on plain film — or even with a negative X-ray — should be treated as Salter-Harris I and immobilized.

Always document neurovascular status
Especially for distal radius and supracondylar elbow injuries.
  • Osgood-Schlatter (tibial tubercle apophysitis): pain at the tibial tubercle in an adolescent — relative rest, ice, NSAIDs, quad/hamstring stretching; resolves with skeletal maturity
  • Sever disease (calcaneal apophysitis): heel pain in an active 8–14 year-old — relative rest, heel cup, calf stretching
  • Little League elbow (medial epicondyle apophysitis): pain in the pitching arm — STOP THROWING, strict pitch counts on return
  • Little League shoulder (proximal humeral physeal injury): X-ray may show widening of the proximal humeral physis — stop throwing for at least 3 months
Education, not casting
Overuse injuries respond to rest and gradual progression, not prolonged casting.

Sports-driven stress fractures (tibia, metatarsals, navicular, femoral neck, pars interarticularis) appear in adolescent runners and gymnasts. Plain films are often negative for 2–4 weeks — MRI is the gold standard for early diagnosis. High-risk locations needing urgent ortho: femoral neck (tension side, superior cortex), anterior tibial cortex ("dreaded black line"), navicular, talus, 5th MT base.

Screen for the Female Athlete Triad / RED-S
Female athletes with amenorrhea + low energy availability + low bone mineral density = the Female Athlete Triad. Screen for an eating disorder and refer to sports medicine.

Disposition and return-to-play

ED pearls: splint or sling for comfort, NSAIDs, ice, elevation; crutches if non-weight-bearing; ortho or sports medicine follow-up (concussions ideally to a concussion clinic); strict return precautions (worsening pain, neurovascular changes, fever, increased swelling); discuss timeline expectations — sports injuries rarely resolve in days.

Concussion discharge instructions
Cognitive rest 24–48h, no driving until cleared, no return to sport without medical clearance, contact sports off-limits until the full stepwise progression is complete.
  • Full pain-free range of motion
  • Strength symmetric to the uninjured side (≥90%)
  • No swelling at rest or after activity
  • Sport-specific functional testing (cutting, jumping) without symptoms
  • Cleared by the treating clinician — concussion requires clearance by a physician familiar with concussion management
Criteria are joint- and sport-specific
Refer to sports medicine for definitive clearance. Avoid blanket "return when pain-free" advice — this is too lenient for ACL, concussion, stress fractures, and Salter-Harris fractures.
  1. Missing the proximal fibula in ankle injuries — Maisonneuve fracture goes home as an "ankle sprain"
  2. Calling pediatric extremity tenderness a "sprain" — physes fail before ligaments
  3. Discharging concussion without an explicit return-to-play plan — second-impact syndrome can be fatal
  4. Returning an ACL-injured athlete to play the same day — knee instability + cutting = catastrophic re-injury
  5. Ignoring the posterolateral corner with an LCL injury — combined injury misses the surgical window
  6. Not screening female adolescent athletes for the Triad — a stress fracture without metabolic workup misses an underlying eating disorder
  7. Removing helmet/pads separately in suspected c-spine injury — remove together, do not pull the helmet off alone

If any criterion fails → maintain immobilization, CT cervical spine; MRI if persistent pain with normal CT or any neurologic finding.

In athletes wearing helmets/shoulder pads, follow sport-specific equipment-removal protocols. Football helmet + shoulder pads must be removed together to maintain spinal alignment — never pull the helmet off alone.

Shoulder injuries

Inspection: asymmetry, deformity, swelling. Palpation: AC joint, sternoclavicular joint, biceps groove, greater tuberosity. ROM: forward flexion, abduction, internal/external rotation — pain arc 60–120° suggests impingement, pain at end-range suggests AC joint. Provocative tests: Neer, Hawkins-Kennedy (impingement), empty can (supraspinatus), apprehension/relocation (anterior instability), cross-body adduction (AC joint). Neurovascular: axillary nerve (lateral deltoid sensation, deltoid contraction), radial pulse. Non-localizing exam or severe trauma → AP + axillary or scapular Y plain films.

A direct blow or fall on the adducted shoulder produces an AC joint injury graded I–VI by Rockwood. Plain films (AP, Zanca view) confirm the grade.

Grade | Findings | Management

I | Sprain, no deformity | Sling 1–2 wk, NSAIDs, early ROM

II | AC ligament torn, coracoclavicular ligament intact, mild step-off | Sling 2–3 wk

III | Both ligaments torn, prominent distal clavicle | Sling, conservative trial; surgery if persistent symptoms or laborer/athlete

IV–VI | Posterior, severe inferior, or panclavicular displacement | Orthopedic surgery

Tendinopathy (overuse): pain with overhead activity, painful arc, positive Neer/Hawkins → relative rest, NSAIDs, structured PT (4–6 wk). Acute tear (often after a fall or sudden lift): weakness with empty can / external rotation, drop-arm sign — MRI for diagnosis if a conservative trial fails or weakness is severe. Disposition: sling for comfort, NSAIDs, sports medicine or orthopedics follow-up within 1–2 weeks. Complete inability to elevate the arm → urgent orthopedic referral (massive tear).

Most fractures (middle third, <100% displaced, <2 cm shortening): sling, ice, NSAIDs, early ROM at 2 wk, ortho follow-up. Surgical indications: open fracture, skin tenting/threat, neurovascular compromise, distal third with coracoclavicular ligament disruption, or significant displacement (>2 cm shortening) in an athlete or laborer. Obtain AP and 30° cephalad films; examine for pneumothorax and brachial plexus injury.

Plain films (AP + axillary or scapular Y): trauma with deformity, suspected fracture, suspected dislocation, or persistent symptoms after 2–3 weeks of conservative care. MRI: suspected rotator cuff tear with weakness, suspected labral injury (recurrent dislocations, mechanical symptoms), or persistent pain after normal X-ray and conservative trial. ED MRI is rarely indicated unless septic arthritis, abscess, or massive cuff tear with functional loss is a concern.

Knee injuries

Ottawa Knee Rule — image the knee if any of: age ≥55, isolated patellar tenderness, tenderness at the fibular head, inability to flex to 90°, or inability to bear weight (4 steps) immediately AND in the ED. Use the calculator below to score this patient.

Ottawa Knee criteria met: (computed)

criteria: No X-ray needed 0-0; X-ray indicated 1-5

AP, lateral, and skyline patellar views. Add tibial plateau views if posterolateral pain or a high-energy mechanism (Lipscomb sign). If fracture present: tibial plateau/patella displaced → orthopedics; avulsion fragment with instability → consider a Segond fracture (pathognomonic for ACL tear); open fracture → the vascular pathway. If no fracture but clinically unstable (positive Lachman or pivot shift) → outpatient MRI within 1 week.

Mechanism narrows the differential: pop + immediate effusion + giving way → ACL. Twisting + locking + joint-line tenderness → meniscus. Direct lateral blow + medial pain → MCL. Hyperextension + posterior pain → PCL. Patellar dislocation history → patellofemoral instability. Key tests: Lachman (most sensitive for ACL), McMurray (meniscus), valgus/varus stress, posterior drawer, patellar apprehension.

Ottawa-rules-positive or normal X-ray: rest, ice, NSAIDs, knee immobilizer, crutches, weight-bear as tolerated. Outpatient MRI within 1 week and orthopedics referral. Do not promise a return-to-play timeline in the ED — surgical management (BTB or hamstring graft) typically requires 6–9 months of rehab; some non-pivoting athletes manage non-operatively.

A Segond fracture on plain film is highly specific for an ACL tear.

Findings: joint-line tenderness, positive McMurray, locked knee (mechanical block to extension). ED workup: plain films to rule out fracture; if locked, prompt orthopedic consult — may need urgent surgical reduction. Disposition (non-locked): rest, ice, NSAIDs, range-of-motion exercises, outpatient MRI and ortho referral within 1–2 weeks.

Graded I (sprain) to III (complete tear). Grade I/II: hinged knee brace, weight-bear as tolerated, NSAIDs, PT, return to sport in 2–6 weeks. Grade III: brace at 30° flexion, ortho referral; surgical repair occasionally needed (especially LCL with posterolateral corner injury). Isolated LCL injuries are rare — always evaluate the posterolateral corner and PCL when the LCL is torn.

Reduction: extend the knee + medial pressure on the patella; procedural sedation rarely needed. Post-reduction films (AP, lateral, skyline) evaluate for osteochondral fracture; MRI if locked, hemarthrosis, or recurrent. Disposition: knee immobilizer in extension, weight-bear as tolerated, outpatient orthopedics within 1 week, PT for VMO strengthening.

Ankle / foot injuries

Ottawa Ankle Rule — image if any of: bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus, bone tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal or navicular (foot rule), or inability to bear weight 4 steps immediately AND in the ED. Sensitivity ~98% for clinically significant fractures.

Ottawa Ankle criteria met: (computed)

criteria: No X-ray needed 0-0; X-ray indicated 1-5

Ankle: AP, lateral, mortise. Foot: AP, lateral, oblique. Unstable/displaced → orthopedics, splint, non-weight-bearing.

Fracture | Key finding

Maisonneuve | Proximal fibula + medial malleolus/deltoid disruption — palpate the proximal fibula on every ankle injury

Lisfranc | Tarsometatarsal disruption — tenderness at base of 1st/2nd MT; weight-bearing films, MRI/CT if subtle

Jones | 5th MT base, proximal diaphysis — high non-union risk

Pilon | Distal tibia plafond — high-energy, requires CT

Most are lateral ankle sprains (ATFL > CFL > PTFL). Treatment: PRICE (protection, relative rest, ice, compression, elevation), functional bracing or lace-up brace (better than rigid immobilization for return-to-sport), weight-bear as tolerated, early ROM + proprioception PT, short-course NSAIDs. High ankle sprain (syndesmosis): positive squeeze test, external rotation pain — longer recovery, may need a boot, ortho follow-up. Persistent pain >6 weeks → MRI for occult fracture, osteochondral lesion, or syndesmotic injury.

Pediatric considerations

Open growth plates change everything: ligaments are stronger than physes, so what looks like a "sprain" in an adult may be a Salter-Harris fracture in a child. Apophyseal injuries (Osgood-Schlatter, Sever, Little League shoulder/elbow) are overuse — relative rest, gradual return.

Tenderness at the physis on plain film — or even with a negative X-ray — should be treated as Salter-Harris I and immobilized.

Type (SALTR) | Description | Disposition

I — Slipped | Through physis only (often radiographically occult) | Closed reduction + immobilization, outpatient ortho

II — Above | Through physis + metaphysis (most common) | Closed reduction + immobilization, outpatient ortho

III — Lower | Through physis + epiphysis (intra-articular) | Orthopedics consult (often OR for anatomic reduction)

IV — Through | Through physis, metaphysis, epiphysis (intra-articular) | Orthopedics consult (often OR for anatomic reduction)

V — Ruined | Crush injury — worst prognosis for growth | Orthopedics consult (often OR for anatomic reduction)

Especially for distal radius and supracondylar elbow injuries.

  • Osgood-Schlatter (tibial tubercle apophysitis): pain at the tibial tubercle in an adolescent — relative rest, ice, NSAIDs, quad/hamstring stretching; resolves with skeletal maturity
  • Sever disease (calcaneal apophysitis): heel pain in an active 8–14 year-old — relative rest, heel cup, calf stretching
  • Little League elbow (medial epicondyle apophysitis): pain in the pitching arm — STOP THROWING, strict pitch counts on return
  • Little League shoulder (proximal humeral physeal injury): X-ray may show widening of the proximal humeral physis — stop throwing for at least 3 months

Overuse injuries respond to rest and gradual progression, not prolonged casting.

Sports-driven stress fractures (tibia, metatarsals, navicular, femoral neck, pars interarticularis) appear in adolescent runners and gymnasts. Plain films are often negative for 2–4 weeks — MRI is the gold standard for early diagnosis. High-risk locations needing urgent ortho: femoral neck (tension side, superior cortex), anterior tibial cortex ("dreaded black line"), navicular, talus, 5th MT base.

Female athletes with amenorrhea + low energy availability + low bone mineral density = the Female Athlete Triad. Screen for an eating disorder and refer to sports medicine.

Disposition and return-to-play

ED pearls: splint or sling for comfort, NSAIDs, ice, elevation; crutches if non-weight-bearing; ortho or sports medicine follow-up (concussions ideally to a concussion clinic); strict return precautions (worsening pain, neurovascular changes, fever, increased swelling); discuss timeline expectations — sports injuries rarely resolve in days.

Cognitive rest 24–48h, no driving until cleared, no return to sport without medical clearance, contact sports off-limits until the full stepwise progression is complete.

  • Full pain-free range of motion
  • Strength symmetric to the uninjured side (≥90%)
  • No swelling at rest or after activity
  • Sport-specific functional testing (cutting, jumping) without symptoms
  • Cleared by the treating clinician — concussion requires clearance by a physician familiar with concussion management

Refer to sports medicine for definitive clearance. Avoid blanket "return when pain-free" advice — this is too lenient for ACL, concussion, stress fractures, and Salter-Harris fractures.

  • Missing the proximal fibula in ankle injuries — Maisonneuve fracture goes home as an "ankle sprain"
  • Calling pediatric extremity tenderness a "sprain" — physes fail before ligaments
  • Discharging concussion without an explicit return-to-play plan — second-impact syndrome can be fatal
  • Returning an ACL-injured athlete to play the same day — knee instability + cutting = catastrophic re-injury
  • Ignoring the posterolateral corner with an LCL injury — combined injury misses the surgical window
  • Not screening female adolescent athletes for the Triad — a stress fracture without metabolic workup misses an underlying eating disorder
  • Removing helmet/pads separately in suspected c-spine injury — remove together, do not pull the helmet off alone
  • Triage red flags first: head, c-spine, vascular, dislocation
  • Decision rules save imaging: Ottawa Knee, Ottawa Ankle, NEXUS, Canadian C-Spine
  • Concussion → SCAT-style assessment, stepwise RTP, no shortcuts
  • Knee + ACL → Lachman is most sensitive; Segond = ACL
  • Maisonneuve → palpate the proximal fibula on every ankle injury
  • Pediatric: physes fail before ligaments → Salter-Harris until proven otherwise
  • Female adolescent athlete + stress fracture → screen for the Triad
  • Disposition: splint/sling, NSAIDs, ice, elevation, ortho/sports med follow-up, strict return precautions
