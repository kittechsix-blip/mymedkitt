Adults with suspected or confirmed Guillain-Barré syndrome in the ED/inpatient setting. Excludes pediatric-specific dosing and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP, defined by progression >8 weeks) — a different treatment pathway.

⚠ Bulbar or respiratory symptoms (dysphagia, weak cough, dyspnea) at presentation = immediate respiratory assessment — do not wait for weakness to progress.
⚠ The 20/30/40 rule: FVC <20 mL/kg (or <1L absolute), NIF weaker than -30 cmH2O, or FVC decline >30% from baseline = prepare for intubation.
⚠ Early elective intubation is safer than emergent intubation — patients may appear comfortable but have critically low respiratory reserve. Bulbar weakness increases aspiration risk independent of FVC.
⚠ Start IVIG or plasmapheresis within 2 weeks of symptom onset — do NOT combine both (no additional benefit; PLEX may remove IVIG) and do NOT use corticosteroids (ineffective, may delay recovery).
⚠ AVOID succinylcholine for intubation — denervated muscle releases excessive potassium, risking life-threatening hyperkalemia. Use rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg instead.

1 · Recognize GBS

The lesion
Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy: rapidly progressive, symmetric ascending weakness with hyporeflexia or areflexia. Sensory symptoms (paresthesias, numbness) often precede weakness. Progression occurs over days to 4 weeks (nadir typically by 4 weeks). Antecedent infection in 2/3 of cases — Campylobacter jejuni (most common, axonal variants), CMV, EBV, Zika, rare post-vaccination. 3-5% mortality from respiratory failure, autonomic dysfunction, or PE.[1][2][3]
Red flags requiring immediate attention
Bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysarthria, drooling); respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, weak cough, orthopnea); autonomic dysfunction (BP lability, arrhythmias, urinary retention); rapid progression (<24-48 hours to severe weakness). If progression continues past 8 weeks, consider CIDP (chronic form) instead of GBS.[1][2]

NIF interpretation: closer to 0 = worse (e.g., -15 is worse than -40). Monitor Q4-6h in all admitted GBS patients, Q2h if declining or concerning trend, with continuous pulse oximetry. Stable — continue monitoring, proceed to workup
FVC >20 mL/kg, NIF stronger than -30 cmH2O, no rapid decline, no bulbar involvement. Continue serial FVC/NIF Q4-6h and proceed to diagnostic workup.[1][2]
Concerning trend — escalate monitoring, prepare to intubate
Declining FVC/NIF approaching thresholds. Escalate to Q2h monitoring with continuous pulse oximetry. Early elective intubation is safer than emergent — do not wait for the patient to appear distressed.[1][2]
Meets intubation criteria or bulbar compromise — intubate now
FVC <20 mL/kg, NIF weaker than -30 cmH2O, or airway compromise from bulbar weakness — proceed to elective intubation now. Patients may appear comfortable but have critically low respiratory reserve; bulbar weakness alone can mandate earlier intubation independent of FVC.[1][2]

3 · Diagnostic workup

LP: albuminocytologic dissociation
Elevated protein + normal WBC count confirms GBS. Protein may be normal in the first week — repeat if high suspicion. WBC typically <10 cells/µL. WBC >50 cells/µL — consider HIV, Lyme, CMV, sarcoidosis instead.[1][2][4]

EMG/nerve conduction studies may be normal in the first 1-2 weeks. Demyelinating pattern (AIDP): prolonged distal latencies, conduction block, prolonged F-waves. Axonal pattern (AMAN/AMSAN): reduced amplitudes, normal latencies. Anti-ganglioside antibodies (GM1 → AMAN, GQ1b → Miller Fisher >90%, GD1a → AMAN/pharyngeal-cervical-brachial) take days to result — do NOT delay treatment waiting for them. Labs: CBC, BMP, LFTs, ESR/CRP, HIV, Campylobacter serology; consider Lyme, CMV, EBV.[1][2][4]

Imaging & mimics to excludeMRI spine to rule out compressive lesion — botulism, tick paralysis, MG are key mimics

MRI spine with contrast: primary role is to rule out a compressive lesion (cord compression, epidural abscess). GBS shows nerve-root enhancement (especially cauda equina) but enhancement may be absent early — not required for diagnosis, helps exclude mimics.

4 · Treatment: IVIG vs plasmapheresis

Start treatment early — do not wait for confirmatory testing
Most effective within 2 weeks of symptom onset; benefit diminishes after 4 weeks. Indicated for: unable to walk independently, rapidly progressive weakness, respiratory compromise, significant bulbar dysfunction. IVIG and plasmapheresis (PLEX) are equally effective — choice depends on availability, patient factors, contraindications. Do NOT combine IVIG + PLEX (no additional benefit; PLEX may remove IVIG, reducing efficacy). Corticosteroids are NOT effective for GBS — multiple RCTs show no benefit and may delay recovery — do NOT use.[1][2][5]

IVIG — contraindications, side effects, monitoring

Contraindications: IgA deficiency (anaphylaxis risk — check IgA level if possible); recent MI or stroke (thrombosis risk); severe renal impairment (sucrose-containing formulations). Common side effects: headache (most common), fever, chills, myalgias, nausea. Serious side effects: aseptic meningitis, acute renal failure, thrombosis (MI, stroke, DVT, PE), hemolytic anemia, TRALI. Monitoring: infusion reactions, headache, renal function; watch for thrombosis in at-risk patients.[2][5]

Plasmapheresis — requirements, complications

Complications: hypotension during exchanges; hypocalcemia (citrate anticoagulant); coagulopathy (removal of clotting factors); line-related complications (infection, thrombosis); autonomic instability may be exacerbated. Monitoring: hemodynamics during exchanges, calcium, coagulation studies, line complications.[2][5]

Treatment response & relapse10% treatment-related fluctuation — never use steroids

Clinical improvement typically begins within 1-2 weeks of completing treatment; maximum improvement may take 6-12 months. 80% walk independently at 6 months. Treatment-related fluctuation (TRF): worsening after initial improvement within 8 weeks, occurs in ~10% of patients — may repeat the same treatment. No response to initial treatment: consider switching modality (IVIG ↔ PLEX), re-evaluate the diagnosis, consider CIDP if progression >8 weeks. Relapse after 8 weeks → consider CIDP (needs maintenance immunotherapy). Do NOT give steroids — ineffective, may delay recovery.[1][2][5]

5 · Supportive care

DVT prophylaxis — high priority
GBS patients are immobile and at major VTE risk. Pharmacologic prophylaxis (enoxaparin or heparin) plus mechanical prophylaxis (SCDs, graduated compression stockings) until ambulatory.[1][2]

Neuropathic pain is common and often severe: Gabapentin 300-900 mg TID or Pregabalin 75-150 mg BID. NSAIDs and acetaminophen for musculoskeletal pain. Opioids may be needed but use cautiously (respiratory depression).[1][2]

Psychological support — critical
GBS causes sudden, profound paralysis in previously healthy individuals. Autonomic dysfunction — occurs in 70% of GBS patients

Early elective intubation is safer than emergent — do not wait until respiratory failure. Criteria: FVC <20 mL/kg, NIF weaker than -30 cmH2O, or bulbar compromise. Anticipate autonomic instability during laryngoscopy (bradycardia, hypotension, or hypertension) — have atropine and vasopressors ready.[1][2]

Ventilator management & weaningGBS is ventilatory failure (muscle weakness), not oxygenation failure

Lung-protective ventilation: tidal volume 6-8 mL/kg IBW, PEEP as needed for oxygenation. Weaning may require weeks to months; daily SBT when clinically improving; weaning success correlates with neurological recovery. Consider tracheostomy early if no improvement expected within 2 weeks — typically performed at 2-3 weeks if still ventilator-dependent. Minimize sedation — patients are often cognitively intact and want to communicate.

8 · Disposition & prognosis

ALL suspected GBS patients require admission
ICU admission criteria (most GBS patients): any respiratory compromise or declining FVC/NIF, bulbar symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, rapidly progressive weakness, or EGRIS score >=3. Step-down/floor admission only if stable with normal respiratory parameters after 24-48h observation and no bulbar/autonomic involvement — still requires Q4-6h FVC/NIF monitoring. Discharge from the ED is rarely appropriate.[1][2]

Anticipate autonomic instability during laryngoscopy (bradycardia, hypotension, or hypertension) — have atropine and vasopressors ready.{{ref1}}{{ref2}}

Ventilator management & weaning

Lung-protective ventilation: tidal volume 6-8 mL/kg IBW, PEEP as needed for oxygenation. Weaning may require weeks to months; daily SBT when clinically improving; weaning success correlates with neurological recovery. Consider tracheostomy early if no improvement expected within 2 weeks — typically performed at 2-3 weeks if still ventilator-dependent. Minimize sedation — patients are often cognitively intact and want to communicate.

8 · Disposition & prognosis

ICU admission criteria (most GBS patients): any respiratory compromise or declining FVC/NIF, bulbar symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, rapidly progressive weakness, or EGRIS score >=3. Step-down/floor admission only if stable with normal respiratory parameters after 24-48h observation and no bulbar/autonomic involvement — still requires Q4-6h FVC/NIF monitoring. Discharge from the ED is rarely appropriate.{{ref1}}{{ref2}}

Outcome | Rate

Walk independently at 6 months | 80%

Full recovery | 60-70%

Persistent significant disability | 5-10%

Mortality | 3-5%

Poor prognostic factors: age >60 years, rapid progression to nadir (<7 days), need for mechanical ventilation, axonal variants (AMAN, AMSAN), preceding Campylobacter infection, low MRC sum score at nadir (<40), delayed treatment initiation. Recovery typically peaks by 12-18 months; fatigue and mild weakness may persist.{{ref1}}{{ref2}}{{ref3}}
