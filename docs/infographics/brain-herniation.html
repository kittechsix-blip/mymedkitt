Brain Herniation: Recognize, Treat Now, Classify, Decide Surgery

Adults with suspected or radiographically confirmed brain herniation in the ED/ICU. Excludes pediatric-specific ICP thresholds, isolated hydrocephalus without herniation signs, and post-operative/post-craniotomy monitoring protocols not addressed by this pathway.

⚠ Anisocoria + altered consciousness = assume herniation. Treat immediately — do not wait for CT.
⚠ Emergency actions are parallel, not sequential: airway (intubate if GCS ≤8, SaO2 >95%, avoid hypotension during RSI), hyperosmolar therapy, head-of-bed positioning, hyperventilation as a bridge, and calling neurosurgery STAT all happen together.
⚠ Cushing triad (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respirations) = imminent/active herniation — tonsillar (foramen magnum) herniation is the most rapidly fatal type; act immediately.
⚠ Midline shift >5 mm on CT = emergent neurosurgery. About 1 in 1500 neurologically normal patients still show imaging signs of herniation — always correlate with the clinical exam, never rely on imaging alone.
⚠ Hyperventilation (PaCO2 32-35 mmHg) is temporizing only — maximum 2 hours. Prolonged hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction and ischemia; it is a bridge, never a sustained or first-24-hour prophylactic therapy.
⚠ Avoid hypotension aggressively — a single SBP <90 doubles mortality. Target SBP >100-110 mmHg in the ED and CPP 60-70 mmHg in the ICU.
⚠ Mannitol and hypertonic saline are both first-line — the choice is patient-specific. Prefer hypertonic saline if hypotensive (mannitol's diuretic effect can worsen perfusion); hold repeat mannitol dosing if serum osmolality is >320 mOsm/L.

Euvolemic / normotensive — start with mannitol
Mannitol 20% 1 g/kg IV over 15-20 min. Hold repeat dosing at serum osmolality >320 mOsm/L or if hypotensive.[3][4]
Hypotensive — prefer hypertonic saline
Hypertonic saline 3% 2-5 mL/kg IV bolus (often 250-500 mL) — no diuretic effect, better for a hypotensive patient. Target Na 145-155 mEq/L for ICP control. 23.4% NaCl 30 mL via central line is an alternate, more concentrated protocol option.[3][4]

Head of bed 30°, head midline, loosen a cervical collar if present. Maintain SaO2 >95%, normocapnia (PaCO2 35-45) once stable, and normothermia (36-37°C) — simple measures that reduce ICP with no dose to calculate.[3][7]

Hyperventilation — bridge only
Target PaCO2 32-35 mmHg, never below 25 mmHg — maximum 2 hours. Prolonged hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction and ischemia; wean once definitive therapy starts.[3][7][8]
Blood pressure / CPP targets
Avoid hypotension aggressively — a single episode of SBP <90 doubles mortality. ED target SBP >100-110 mmHg (age-based); ICU target CPP 60-70 mmHg (CPP = MAP − ICP). Norepinephrine is the preferred pressor if one is needed; treat the herniation, not just the number — HTN may be a protective Cushing response.[7][9]

About 1 in 1500 neurologically normal patients have imaging signs of herniation — always correlate with the clinical exam, never rely on imaging alone.[5]

Emergent surgical indications: midline shift >5 mm, epidural hematoma with any mass effect, subdural hematoma >10 mm or MLS >5 mm, cerebellar hemorrhage >3 cm, obstructive hydrocephalus, or a depressed skull fracture.[5][6]

Neurosurgical intervention
Call neurosurgery STAT for anisocoria + altered consciousness, Cushing triad, new/progressive posturing, rapid neurological decline, or GCS ≤8 with a surgical lesion. Options: craniotomy (hematoma evacuation), EVD (hydrocephalus/ICP monitoring), or decompressive craniectomy — reserved for refractory ICP >40 mmHg despite maximal medical therapy, ideally within 24-48 h of injury, with better outcomes under age 60.[6][10]

4 · ICP monitoring & reassessment

Place an ICP monitor/EVD for GCS ≤8 with abnormal CT, or GCS ≤8 with normal CT plus ≥2 of: age >40, motor posturing, SBP <90. Treat ICP 20-22 mmHg (tier 1 therapy), escalate at 25-30 mmHg, and consider decompressive craniectomy at 40 mmHg. CPP goal >60 mmHg (60-70 optimal).[7][9]

  • After each intervention, reassess in 15-30 min.
  • Pupillary exam — size, reactivity, anisocoria improving or worsening.
  • Motor exam — GCS motor component, posturing type/laterality.
  • Vital signs — BP trend (Cushing response?), heart rate, respiratory pattern.
  • ICP if monitored — absolute value, trend, waveform (A-waves = bad), CPP.
  • Repeat imaging for clinical deterioration, no improvement, or new neurological findings.

6 · Prognosis & Do NOT

Poor prognostic signs: bilateral fixed and dilated pupils, GCS 3 with loss of brainstem reflexes, Duret hemorrhage on CT, age >65 with severe TBI, refractory ICP despite maximal therapy. Discuss goals of care early; involve palliative care when prognosis is poor.[1][10]

Critical pitfalls6 items
Do NOT wait for CT if anisocoria, posturing, or Cushing triad are present — treat before imaging.
Do NOT use hyperventilation as primary or sustained therapy, or beyond 2 hours — it causes cerebral ischemia.
Do NOT give mannitol as the default choice in a hypotensive/hypovolemic patient — its diuresis can worsen perfusion; use hypertonic saline instead.
Do NOT redose mannitol without checking serum osmolality — hold if >320 mOsm/L.
Do NOT tolerate hypotension — a single SBP <90 doubles mortality.
Do NOT delay neurosurgery consultation for midline shift >5 mm, EDH with mass effect, SDH >10 mm, or cerebellar hemorrhage >3 cm.

5 · One-glance algorithm

Clinical signs, CT mass effect, or reviewing types? — Anisocoria, posturing, declining GCS, or Cushing triad = assume herniation — treat before CT.

Treat now: airway, hyperosmolar therapy, position, hyperventilate, call neurosurgery STAT — Intubate if GCS ≤8, SaO2 >95%, avoid hypotension during RSI. All actions run in parallel, not sequentially.

Which herniation syndrome? — Five types: uncal, central, subfalcine, tonsillar, upward. Uncal most common in trauma; tonsillar most rapidly fatal.

Surgical lesion on CT? — Surgical: MLS >5mm, EDH with mass effect, SDH >10mm, cerebellar hemorrhage >3cm, obstructive hydrocephalus.

Uncal — ipsilateral dilated pupil → bilateral (Kernohan notch) — Temporal lobe uncus compresses CN III/midbrain. Stage 1 ipsilateral pupil dilation → Stage 3 bilateral fixed pupils, decerebrate posturing.

ICP monitor indications: GCS ≤8 + abnormal CT (or ≥2 risk factors) — GCS ≤8 + normal CT needs ≥2 of: age >40, motor posturing, SBP <90.

Central — bilateral small pupils → midposition fixed (Duret hemorrhage) — Diffuse bilateral edema; diencephalon/midbrain descends centrally. Duret hemorrhage (pontine artery rupture) usually fatal.

Reassess pupils, motor, vitals, ICP every 15-30 min — Document time, pupil size/reactivity, GCS breakdown, and interventions given.

Tonsillar — Cushing triad, most rapidly fatal — Cerebellar tonsils compress medulla through foramen magnum. Cushing triad = imminent medullary compression.

Prognosis: bilateral fixed pupils + GCS 3 + lost brainstem reflexes = poor — Discuss goals of care early; involve palliative care if prognosis poor. Age and initial GCS are strong predictors.

Hyperosmolar choice: mannitol or hypertonic saline? — Both first-line. Prefer HTS if hypotensive — mannitol's diuresis can worsen perfusion.

Mannitol 1 g/kg IV — computed at current weight — Over 15-20 min. Hold redose if osm >320 mOsm/L or hypotensive.

Hypertonic saline 3% 2-5 mL/kg — computed at current weight — Bolus over 10-20 min. Target Na 145-155 mEq/L.

HOB 30°, head midline, SaO2 >95%, normothermia — Loosen cervical collar if present. Simple measures reduce ICP.

Hyperventilation bridge — PaCO2 32-35 mmHg, max 2 hours — Temporizing only — causes ischemia if prolonged. Never below 25 mmHg.

BP/CPP targets — avoid SBP <90-110 (age-based), CPP 60-70 mmHg — Single SBP <90 doubles mortality. Norepinephrine preferred pressor.

Emergent neurosurgery: evacuate, EVD, or decompressive craniectomy — Decompressive craniectomy for refractory ICP >40 mmHg; best within 24-48h, age <60 favors outcome.

6 · Prognosis & Do NOT

Poor prognostic signs: bilateral fixed and dilated pupils, **GCS 3** with loss of brainstem reflexes, Duret hemorrhage on CT, age >65 with severe TBI, refractory ICP despite maximal therapy. Discuss goals of care early; involve palliative care when prognosis is poor.{{ref1}}{{ref10}}

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT wait for CT** if anisocoria, posturing, or Cushing triad are present — treat before imaging.

**Do NOT use hyperventilation as primary or sustained therapy**, or beyond 2 hours — it causes cerebral ischemia.

**Do NOT give mannitol as the default choice in a hypotensive/hypovolemic patient** — its diuresis can worsen perfusion; use hypertonic saline instead.

**Do NOT redose mannitol without checking serum osmolality** — hold if >320 mOsm/L.

**Do NOT tolerate hypotension** — a single SBP <90 doubles mortality.

**Do NOT delay neurosurgery consultation** for midline shift >5 mm, EDH with mass effect, SDH >10 mm, or cerebellar hemorrhage >3 cm.
