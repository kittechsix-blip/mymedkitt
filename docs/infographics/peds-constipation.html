Pediatric Constipation & Fecal Impaction: Assess Red Flags, Confirm Impaction, Disimpact, Maintain

Children presenting to the ED with constipation or suspected fecal impaction. Excludes neonates <1 month old with delayed meconium passage (goes straight to organic-cause workup, not the functional pathway), known Hirschsprung disease, spinal cord pathology, or prior colorectal surgery — these need individualized specialist-directed management rather than this general functional-constipation protocol.

⚠ Exclude red flags for organic disease before treating as functional constipation — Hirschsprung disease, bowel obstruction, spinal cord pathology, and other organic causes must be screened first (delayed meconium >48h, bilious vomiting, severe distention, abnormal neuro exam, sacral dimple, significant weight loss).
⚠ Do NOT give enemas in suspected Hirschsprung disease — risk of enterocolitis.
⚠ Confirm fecal impaction (palpable abdominal mass or rectal exam with hard stool) before starting disimpaction.
⚠ Never combine oral and rectal disimpaction simultaneously.
⚠ Avoid sodium phosphate (Fleet's) enemas in children younger than 2 years (some guidance extends this caution to under 4 years) — risk of hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, and hypokalemia; if used in older children, single dose only.
⚠ Milk & molasses enema: rare but real hemodynamic complications have been reported (one death) — use with monitoring in dehydrated or unstable patients, and avoid if suspected bowel perforation or obstruction.
⚠ Mineral oil must not be given orally to young children or patients with swallowing difficulty — aspiration risk (lipoid pneumonia).
⚠ Start maintenance laxative therapy only AFTER disimpaction is complete — starting maintenance before disimpaction is finished can mask incomplete clearance.
⚠ Urgent admission triggers: signs of bowel obstruction or perforation, suspected Hirschsprung enterocolitis (fever + distention), severe dehydration, or electrolyte disturbance from enema complications.

1 · Assessment — Rome IV criteria & red flags

Functional constipation affects 3-5% of all pediatric visits and up to 30% of children at some point.[1][2] Most cases (95%) are functional — related to withholding behavior, dietary factors, or toilet training. Only ~5% have an organic cause.[1]

Rome IV criteria for functional constipation (≥1 month for infants; ≥2 months for children):

• ≤2 defecations/week
• ≥1 episode of fecal incontinence/week (after toilet trained)
• History of retentive posturing or excessive volitional stool retention
• History of painful or hard bowel movements
• Large fecal mass in rectum
• History of large-diameter stools that may obstruct the toilet

Key history elementsstool pattern, behavior, diet, onset, past history

Stool pattern: frequency (varies by age), consistency, pain/straining, blood on stool (fissure?).

Behavioral clues: withholding postures, fecal soiling/encopresis (overflow), toilet avoidance.

Diet: fluid/fiber intake, recent dietary changes, cow's milk protein allergy.

Onset & triggers: onset at weaning/toilet-training/school entry = functional; onset in first weeks of life → think Hirschsprung disease.

Past history: prior constipation/disimpaction, laxative use, coexisting urinary symptoms.

Red-flag categoryFindings
Hirschsprung diseaseDelayed meconium passage (>48h after birth); onset in first weeks of life; failure to thrive; explosive stool after rectal exam; empty rectum on exam despite constipation
Bowel obstructionBilious vomiting; severe abdominal distention; absent bowel sounds
Other organic causesFever (enterocolitis risk in Hirschsprung); bloody stool (beyond fissure bleeding); abnormal neurologic exam (spinal cord lesion); sacral dimple, tuft of hair, or asymmetric gluteal cleft; abnormal lower-extremity reflexes; significant weight loss; thyroid abnormalities

3 · Confirm fecal impaction

Fecal impaction = hard stool in the rectum that cannot be evacuated voluntarily.[1]

Abdominal exam: palpable fecal mass in the left lower quadrant (often midline, rubbery).

Rectal exam (if tolerated): hard stool in the rectal vault — routine rectal exam is NOT recommended if the diagnosis is already clear from history plus abdominal exam.[1]

Abdominal X-ray: not routinely recommended; consider only if diagnosis is uncertain or exam is limited.[1]

4 · Disimpaction — oral PEG vs rectal enema

ESPGHAN/NASPGHAN recommends oral PEG as first-line for fecal disimpaction — equal efficacy to rectal enemas, less invasive, no risk of electrolyte disturbance.[1][3]

Choose oral (PEG) if the child can tolerate oral intake and outpatient management is feasible.

Consider rectal (enema) if PEG failed/not tolerated, rapid disimpaction is needed, or there is significant distention/overflow incontinence.

Never combine oral and rectal disimpaction simultaneously.

6 · Maintenance therapy

PEG 3350 (MiraLAX)
0.4-0.8 g/kg/day (start 0.4, titrate up), max 17 g/day
PO
Once daily, mixed in any liquid. Continue a minimum of 2-3 months, often 6-12 months. Titrate weekly: too loose → decrease; still hard or <1/day → increase. Taper by 25% every 2-4 weeks after a minimum of 2 symptom-free months — relapse is common if stopped too soon.
—Maintenance dose — start (0.4 g/kg/day)
—Maintenance dose — top of titration (0.8 g/kg/day)

Alternative — lactulose: 1-3 mL/kg/day divided BID if PEG unavailable; more gas/bloating than PEG.

Goal: 1-2 soft stools per day, no withholding, no soiling.

7 · Behavioral & dietary counseling

Toilet training: scheduled toilet sitting 5-10 minutes after meals (gastrocolic reflex); proper positioning (feet flat, knees above hips); positive reinforcement for sitting, not for producing stool — never punish accidents.

Diet: fiber intake goal = age + 5 g/day; adequate fluids; limit cow's milk to <24 oz/day; limit constipating foods (bananas, rice, applesauce, toast).

Withholding: explain that holding stool makes it bigger/harder; reassure the stool will be soft with laxatives; never force toilet sitting.

Activity: regular exercise reduces stool transit time.

—Daily fiber goal (age + 5 g/day)

8 · Disposition home

Discharge criteria: successful disimpaction (or an ongoing outpatient PEG plan); tolerating oral intake; family understands the maintenance plan; follow-up arranged.

Parent education: this is a chronic condition — expect months of treatment; do NOT stop laxatives when stools normalize, taper slowly; soiling may persist initially and is overflow, not defiance; call if no stool ×3 days, severe abdominal pain, vomiting, or fever.

Follow-up: PCP within 2-4 weeks; Pediatric GI referral if refractory despite compliance, concern for organic cause, or >6 months without improvement.

Return precautions: bilious vomiting, severe distention, bloody stool, fever, inability to tolerate oral intake, worsening despite treatment.
