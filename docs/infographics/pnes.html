Functional Seizures (PNES) — ED Recognition & Management

ED patients with a witnessed paroxysmal event concerning for seizure, where psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (functional/dissociative seizures) are being considered as an alternative to epileptic seizure. Assumes dangerous mimics (status epilepticus, syncope/cardiac, hypoglycemia, toxic/metabolic causes, CNS pathology) are actively screened for first — if any doubt remains, the source consult directs clinicians to treat as epileptic seizure.

⚠ Do NOT give benzodiazepines for suspected PNES — 26% adverse effect rate including respiratory depression
⚠ Do NOT intubate for suspected PNES — associated with worse short-term and long-term outcomes, including death
⚠ 10-30% of PNES patients also have epilepsy — do not assume all seizures in a given patient are functional
⚠ If uncertain whether an event is epileptic or functional, it is safer to initially treat as epileptic seizure

Recognition & initial approach

Psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (PNES), now preferably called functional seizures or dissociative seizures, are paroxysmal episodes that resemble epileptic seizures but are NOT caused by abnormal electrical brain activity. These are real seizures — patients are not faking. PNES is classified as a functional neurological disorder (FND) / conversion disorder under DSM-5: the brain's "software" is malfunctioning, not its "hardware."

7.2 yearsMean diagnostic delay
  • 5-10% of epilepsy outpatients, 20-40% of epilepsy inpatients
  • 11-27% of ED "seizure" presentations
  • 70% female, peak age 20-40 years

Before considering PNES, exclude life-threatening conditions. Clinical pearl: if uncertain between PNES and epileptic seizure, it is SAFER to initially treat as epileptic seizure. However, if high suspicion for PNES based on semiologic features, withhold benzodiazepines.

CategoryRed flag features
True status epilepticusContinuous >5 min or recurrent without recovery; lateral tongue bite; incontinence + prolonged postictal confusion
Syncope / cardiacConvulsive syncope can mimic seizure — check ECG if concern for arrhythmia
HypoglycemiaAlways check fingerstick glucose
Toxic / metabolicDrug intoxication, withdrawal, hyponatremia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy
CNS pathologyConsider if focal deficits, fever, headache
Red flags present — treat as epileptic seizure
See Status Epilepticus for management — benzodiazepines are first-line for status epilepticus, with airway protection if prolonged. After stabilization, if seizure features were atypical, reconsider PNES; video-EEG monitoring may be needed to differentiate. Remember: 10-30% of PNES patients ALSO have epilepsy — a single patient can have both.

Avoiding iatrogenic harm

No benzodiazepines. No intubation. No ASM escalation.
Misdiagnosis of PNES as status epilepticus causes significant harm. Benzodiazepines given for suspected PNES carry a 26% adverse effect rate (respiratory depression requiring intervention; 1 in 5 admitted to ICU). Intubation for PNES is associated with prolonged hospitalization and deaths reported. Anti-seizure medication escalation is ineffective for PNES and only exposes patients to side effects and delays appropriate psychiatric treatment.
  1. Side rails up, remove nearby hazards — do NOT restrain (may worsen event)
  2. Protect head from injury (pillow/cushion); do NOT put anything in the mouth
  3. Speak calmly: "You are safe. I am Dr. [name]. You are in the emergency room." Do NOT suggest the patient is faking
  4. Document semiology: eye position, movement pattern, duration, responsiveness, recovery pattern
  5. Video record the event if possible (with family consent) — invaluable for neurology review

Most PNES episodes resolve spontaneously within 2-15 minutes. Time the event — PNES often lasts longer than epileptic seizures (>2 min).

Ictal eye closure is the MOST predictive sign of PNES — positive likelihood ratio 40.5. Eyes are forcibly closed during the event, with resistance to passive eye opening and possible flutter/rapid blinking. Epileptic seizures typically cause eye opening at onset (tonic/clonic: eyes deviated, open; temporal lobe: staring with open eyes). Not 100% specific — some frontal lobe seizures can have eye closure. Use in combination with other semiologic features.

Diagnosis communication

Gold standard: video-EEG capturing a typical event with no epileptiform activity and history/semiology consistent with PNES. In the ED, achievable levels are possible PNES (suggestive history/semiology, no video-EEG), probable PNES (witnessed event with multiple positive signs, no epileptic features), or established PNES (prior video-EEG documented diagnosis). 31% of ED PNES visits are patients with a known diagnosis.

  • Review EMR for prior video-EEG report; confirm current event matches typical semiology
  • Ask: "Does this feel like your usual seizures?"
  • If different from usual, consider epileptic seizure and lower the threshold for workup
  • Follow institutional EMR best practice alerts if available — studies show they reduce iatrogenic interventions by 50%+
  • Focus the visit on why the patient came in today, adherence to therapy, and new stressors or safety concerns
  1. "Your seizures are REAL" — you are not making this up or faking
  2. "This is a recognized medical condition" — functional seizures / PNES; brain's software, not hardware
  3. "This is TREATABLE" — CBT helps most people, many have significant improvement or complete resolution
  4. "Epilepsy medications won't help" — your seizures aren't caused by abnormal electrical activity, so ASMs won't help and can cause side effects
Avoid minimizing language
Avoid: "It's all in your head", "There's nothing wrong with you", "It's just stress", "You're faking."

Sample explanation: your brain has "hardware" (physical structure — no tumor, no infection, no abnormal electrical activity like epilepsy) and "software" (how it processes stress/emotions). In PNES, the hardware is fine, but the software has a glitch causing these seizure-like episodes. This is treatable — CBT helps most people, some have complete resolution. Epilepsy medications won't help because they target electrical activity, which isn't the problem here. See the PNES Patient Handout for printable take-home information.

Safety, injury & workup

  • Screen for depression, anxiety, PTSD (do NOT probe for trauma details in the ED — 30-40% have trauma history, but direct questioning can be retraumatizing)
  • Ask directly: "Are you having any thoughts of hurting yourself?" / "Have you ever tried to hurt yourself?" / "Do you feel safe at home?"
  • If suicidal ideation present: psychiatric evaluation before discharge, safety planning, consider admission for psychiatric stabilization

Common PNES-related injuries: head trauma from falls, soft tissue injuries, burns near a heat source, MVA if driving. Less common: fractures, posterior shoulder dislocation (consider epileptic seizure), vertebral compression fractures. Exam: head, oral (lateral tongue bite more suggestive of epileptic seizure), extremities, baseline neuro exam. Consider imaging for significant head trauma, LOC with fall, focal findings, or concerning mechanism. Injury does NOT confirm epileptic seizure, but severe injuries are more common with epileptic GTC.

Routinely obtain fingerstick glucose and basic vitals (including SpO2). Consider BMP, serum prolactin (elevated 10-20 min post-event suggests epileptic seizure — limited utility), drug screen, or ECG based on presentation. CT head, routine labs, and ED EEG are usually NOT needed for a typical presentation. Goal: minimize unnecessary testing while ensuring safety.

Disposition & follow-up

Discharge home if: event resolved, returned to baseline, no injuries requiring admission, no active suicidal ideation, safe environment with reliable support, follow-up can be arranged.

Observation / Admit if: diagnostic uncertainty, recurrent events not resolving, significant injuries, suicidal ideation or psychiatric crisis, unable to arrange safe follow-up, or first presentation needing workup.

Psychiatric admission if: active suicidal ideation with plan/intent, unsafe to discharge, or acute psychiatric decompensation.

  1. Neurology within 2-4 weeks — video-EEG if not yet done, confirm diagnosis, manage comorbid epilepsy, coordinate care
  2. Mental health within 1-2 weeks — CBT is first-line, reduces seizure frequency by 50-60%; psychiatry for medication management if needed
  3. PCP within 1-2 weeks — care coordination, depression/anxiety treatment, ongoing support
  4. If patient already has neurology/psychiatry: contact their providers, send discharge summary, ensure the follow-up appointment is scheduled

  • PCP within 1-2 weeks — care coordination, depression/anxiety treatment, ongoing support
  • If patient already has neurology/psychiatry: contact their providers, send discharge summary, ensure the follow-up appointment is scheduled

Reduction in seizure frequency with CBT: 50-60%

Reduction in ED visits after recognition of PNES diagnosis: 39-75%

  • You had a functional seizure (also called PNES) — a real medical condition, not "faking." CBT is effective; epilepsy medications will not help
  • Return to ED if: seizure lasting >5 minutes without recovery, injury during seizure, seizure looks different from usual events, thoughts of hurting yourself, high fever with seizure, unable to return to baseline
  • Avoid driving until evaluated by neurology; avoid heights and water activities until seizures are controlled; have someone with you if seizures are frequent
  • Continue current medications unless told otherwise by your neurologist — do NOT start new seizure medications without neurology input
  • Indications: diagnostic uncertainty needing inpatient video-EEG, recurrent events not resolving, significant injuries requiring monitoring, first presentation needing workup, concern for comorbid epilepsy with breakthrough seizures
  • Psychiatric indications: active suicidal ideation, psychiatric crisis requiring stabilization, medication adjustment under supervision, safety concerns
  • Orders: seizure precautions (fall risk, safety), neurology consult, psychiatry consult if psychiatric concerns, video-EEG if available
  • Communicate to inpatient team: suspected/confirmed PNES diagnosis, document semiologic features observed, avoid escalation of ASMs
  • Avoid: ICU admission for PNES (unless other indication), intubation, aggressive medication escalation, routine benzodiazepines

Hydroxyzine (non-benzodiazepine): 25-50 mg PRN — PRN option for anxiety during inpatient stay — not a substitute for the avoid-benzodiazepines rule for suspected PNES.
