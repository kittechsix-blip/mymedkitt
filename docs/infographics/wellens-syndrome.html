Wellens Syndrome: Recognize the Pattern, Do Not Stress Test

Adults ≥18 in the ED with anterior T-wave changes (biphasic or deep symmetric inversion, V2-V3) after chest pain has improved. Excludes active STEMI/OMI and ongoing active ischemia — treat those via the standard ACS/OMI pathway, not a Wellens work-up. Not for pediatrics.

⚠ Wellens = a pre-infarction LAD warning pattern. Pain may be gone and troponin may be low or normal, but the anterior T-wave pattern can signal critical LAD stenosis. Treat as high-risk NSTE-ACS: do NOT send to stress testing; get cardiology consultation for early invasive evaluation; admit to a monitored setting.
⚠ Do NOT anchor on Wellens if the patient is actively ischemic — persistent pain, shock, VT/VF, acute HF, or dynamic ST change. Treat as active high-risk ACS/OMI and do not delay for Wellens classification.
⚠ Do NOT stress test suspected or confirmed Wellens syndrome. Exercise or pharmacologic stress testing can precipitate MI, malignant arrhythmia, or death. Remove chest-pain-observation stress-test orders.
⚠ High-suspicion Wellens is not a low-risk or outpatient diagnosis. Do NOT discharge because pain resolved and do NOT place in a low-risk chest pain pathway — a normal or mildly elevated troponin does NOT clear a classic Wellens ECG.
⚠ Recurrent/refractory ischemic pain, instability, VT/VF, acute heart failure/pulmonary edema, or dynamic ST change require an immediate cardiology/cath-lab discussion — manage as active high-risk ACS.
⚠ Use explicit handoff language on admission (e.g. "Wellens pattern. No stress test. Needs cardiology and invasive evaluation.") to prevent accidental stress testing or premature discharge.

1 · Recognize & triage presentation

The lesion
Wellens syndrome is a pre-infarction LAD warning pattern: recent anginal chest pain, now pain-free or improved, little or no ST elevation, normal-to-mildly-elevated troponin, and characteristic anterior T-wave changes in V2-V3 often extending V1-V6. The ECG can look "not that bad" while the LAD is critically narrowed and can close again.[1]

First question: how is the patient presenting right now? Active ischemic pain, shock, VT/VF, acute HF, or dynamic ST change is treated as active ACS/OMI — not a calm Wellens observation case. Wellens is usually recognized only *after* chest pain has improved.[1][4]

Ask specifically about recent, resolved angina
Patients rarely volunteer pain that has already resolved. Ask about chest pressure/tightness/burning, dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, or arm/jaw discomfort in the last days to weeks; whether it resolved before the ECG; any exertional or crescendo pattern; cocaine/stimulant use; and whether a prior ECG is available for comparison.[1][2]

2 · ECG pattern & Wellens criteria

TypePatternNotes
Type ABiphasic T waves in V2-V3Positive deflection then terminal negativity; less common, can evolve into Type B
Type BDeep, symmetric T-wave inversion in V2-V3Often extends into V1-V6, I, aVL; more common

Supportive criteria: isoelectric or minimally elevated ST segment, usually <1 mm; no pathologic precordial Q waves; preserved R-wave progression; pattern often appears once pain has resolved.[1][2]

High suspicion requires ALL of the following
Recent angina, now pain-free or improved · biphasic or deep symmetric T-wave inversion in V2-V3 · minimal ST elevation, usually <1 mm · no pathologic precordial Q waves · preserved R-wave progression · troponin normal or only mildly elevated.[1][2][6]
The troponin trap
Troponin may be normal or only mildly elevated — the artery may have nearly closed, then reopened, before the blood test showed major damage. Do not downgrade the ECG because pain resolved, the first troponin is negative, vitals are stable, or the ECG has a classic Wellens pattern: a low troponin does NOT clear a classic Wellens ECG. Use troponin for risk and trend, not to dismiss the ECG.[1][2][6]

3 · What to do NOW (live)

Set the toggles above — unstable overrides everything, then active ischemia, then classic-vs-not. Exactly one recommendation shows below.

Unstable / recurrent ischemia — call cardiology/cath lab now
Recurrent or refractory ischemic pain, hemodynamic instability, VT/VF, acute heart failure/pulmonary edema, dynamic ST elevation/depression, or concern for active LAD occlusion. Call cardiology/cath lab now. Continue aspirin, anticoagulation, statin, telemetry, and repeat ECGs.[4][5]
Active ACS / possible OMI — do not anchor on Wellens
Repeat ECG now and compare with prior. Activate the ACS pathway if STEMI/OMI or refractory ischemia. Aspirin 162-325 mg chewed, anticoagulation per ACS protocol, high-intensity statin, Nitroglycerin if no contraindication, cardiology/cath lab discussion now.[4][5]
Classic Wellens — high-risk NSTE-ACS, no stress test
Treat as high-risk NSTE-ACS / LAD reperfusion warning: Aspirin 162-325 mg chewed, anticoagulation per NSTE-ACS protocol, high-intensity statin, Nitroglycerin for recurrent pain, continuous telemetry, serial ECGs/troponins, cardiology consult for early invasive evaluation. Do NOT discharge, do NOT low-risk pathway, do NOT stress test.[1][3][4][5]
Possible / not classic — serial ECGs, consider mimics
Repeat ECG with any symptom recurrence, or in 15-30 min if uncertain/evolving; compare with old ECGs; watch for evolution from biphasic to deep inversion or conversion to anterior STEMI/OMI. If ECG evolves or symptoms recur, upgrade to urgent cath discussion — do not wait for the next troponin.[1][2][4]

4 · Initial ACS medical therapy

Initial ED management mirrors high-risk NSTE-ACS. Beta blocker only if appropriate and no acute HF/shock/bradycardia. P2Y12 loading follows the local cardiology/cath-lab protocol — some centers defer until coronary anatomy is known if CABG is possible. Telemetry, repeat ECG with any symptoms, serial troponin.[4][5]

5 · No stress test

Do NOT stress test suspected Wellens syndrome
Stress testing asks the heart to work harder when the LAD may already be barely open — analogous to flooring the gas pedal while a bridge ahead is cracked. It can precipitate MI, malignant arrhythmia, or death. The safer next test is coronary angiography or cardiology-directed anatomic evaluation. Remove chest-pain-observation stress-test orders; tell the admitting team: "Wellens pattern. No stress test. Needs cardiology."[1][3][5]

6 · Cath strategy & mimics

Wellens usually needs early invasive evaluation; how urgent the cath discussion is depends on active ischemia or instability, not just the ECG pattern.[1][4][5]

Stable classic Wellens → early invasive pathway
Telemetry admission, cardiology consult from the ED, coronary angiography planning (often within 24 hours depending on local pathway and risk features), continue ACS medical therapy, no stress testing before coronary anatomy is addressed. A normal or low troponin does not make this low risk.[1][3][4]
