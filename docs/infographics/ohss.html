Ovarian Hyperstimulation Syndrome (OHSS) — ED Management

ED patients with suspected ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) after ovarian stimulation for IVF/ART — early OHSS (3-7 days post-hCG trigger, before embryo transfer) or late OHSS (12-17 days post-hCG, up to 2 weeks post-retrieval, driven by endogenous hCG from pregnancy). Assumes ovarian torsion, ectopic pregnancy, ruptured ovarian cyst, and other acute-abdomen mimics have been actively excluded (pelvic ultrasound with Doppler + pregnancy test) before applying this severity/management pathway.

⚠ VTE prophylaxis required for all moderate-severe OHSS (enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily)
⚠ Hematocrit >45% = severe OHSS requiring hospitalization
⚠ Therapeutic paracentesis if respiratory compromise or tense ascites
⚠ Ovarian torsion risk 6-16% in severe OHSS — maintain high suspicion for sudden unilateral pain
⚠ Albumin 25% 50-100 g IV for severe hemoconcentration, or with large-volume paracentesis

Recognition & history

OHSS is an iatrogenic complication of ovarian stimulation — most commonly from IVF/ART. Exaggerated ovarian response to gonadotropin stimulation drives VEGF-mediated capillary leak and third-spacing. Early OHSS: 3-7 days after hCG trigger (before transfer). Late OHSS: 12-17 days after hCG, from endogenous hCG of pregnancy. Can mimic acute abdomen, peritonitis, or ectopic pregnancy — and often presents when the REI clinic is closed.

Pathophysiology

The VEGF cascade: hCG triggers massive ovarian VEGF release → increased vascular permeability → capillary leak → third-spacing of fluid → intravascular depletion despite total body fluid overload. Third-spacing produces ascites and (usually right-sided) pleural effusions. Hemoconcentration (Hct >45% = severe disease) increases blood viscosity. Hemoconcentration plus estrogen drives hypercoagulability — both arterial and venous thrombosis. Prerenal AKI results from volume depletion; tense ascites can cause abdominal compartment syndrome.

Do NOT give diuretics
The paradox: the patient looks fluid-overloaded (ascites, edema, weight gain) but the intravascular space is severely depleted. Diuretics worsen intravascular depletion, worsen hemoconcentration, increase VTE risk, and worsen renal function. Correct approach: volume expansion with IV fluids and paracentesis for symptomatic ascites relief. Diuretics may be considered only after intravascular volume is restored (Hct normalizing), in consultation with REI/nephrology, in the recovery phase.

ED management

  • Volume resuscitation — target UOP >0.5-1 mL/kg/hr, follow Hct trend
  • Pain control — acetaminophen (safe in pregnancy) and opioids; avoid NSAIDs (renal risk)
  • Prevent complications — VTE prophylaxis; monitor for torsion, rupture, ARDS
  • Supportive care — antiemetics, electrolyte management, respiratory support if needed
  • Initial workup — CBC, CMP, LFTs, coagulation studies, UA, pregnancy test, pelvic US with Doppler, CXR if respiratory symptoms
  • Contact the REI clinic — even after hours, they have on-call coverage

Crystalloids (NS or LR) are first-line — 1-2 L bolus, then reassess; may require 2-3 L/day. Colloid (albumin 25%) for severe OHSS with significant hemoconcentration, often given with paracentesis. Avoid: dextrose-containing solutions (worsen hyponatremia), excessive fluid (worsens ascites), and diuretics.

Major complications

  • VTE (DVT, PE most common) — 10-100x increased risk, can occur weeks after onset
  • Arterial thromboembolism — stroke, MI, limb ischemia; less common but devastating
  • Ovarian torsion — enlarged, heavy ovaries prone to twist; surgical emergency
  • Ovarian rupture — may cause significant hemoperitoneum, may require surgery
  • Acute renal failure — prerenal from volume depletion, may require dialysis
  • ARDS — severe capillary leak affecting the lungs, may require intubation
  • Electrolyte disturbances — hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, can cause arrhythmias

All moderate-to-severe OHSS patients need anticoagulation prophylaxis. Risk persists for weeks after OHSS resolves. Mechanical prophylaxis (SCDs, early ambulation) alongside pharmacologic prophylaxis. Longer prophylaxis warranted with personal/family VTE history, immobility, obesity, or age >35.

Reference — REI communication & prognosis (not tied to a decision step)

Always contact the REI (fertility) clinic with: patient identifiers and REI clinic/physician; timeline (hCG trigger date, retrieval date, embryo transfer status, pregnancy status); current clinical status (severity classification, vitals, key labs — Hct, Cr, electrolytes; ultrasound findings); ED management given (fluids, medications, procedures); and the disposition plan. REI can modify future stimulation protocols, has baseline labs for comparison, and this preserves continuity of care.

If not pregnant: OHSS resolves with the next menstrual period, usually 7-10 days after onset, once hCG clears. If pregnant: OHSS may worsen initially (rising endogenous hCG), peaks at 8-10 weeks gestation, then gradually improves — can take weeks to fully resolve. Fertility is preserved; no evidence of long-term ovarian damage or adverse offspring effects. Mortality is rare with modern management (deaths usually from VTE or ARDS) — prevention and early recognition are key. Prevention in future cycles: lower gonadotropin doses, GnRH agonist trigger instead of hCG, freeze-all embryos, dopamine agonists (cabergoline).

Fluid management — NS/LR 1-2L bolus then reassess; albumin 25% 50-100g for severe hemoconcentration; target UOP>30-50 mL/hr, Hct<40-45%; avoid excessive fluids.

Pain control & antiemetics — Avoid NSAIDs (renal risk); acetaminophen and opioids safe; ondansetron for nausea — use pregnancy-safe medications as patients may be pregnant.

Paracentesis — US-guided paracentesis for respiratory compromise or tense ascites — avoid ovaries (10+ cm), slow removal 1-3L, albumin 6-8g/L if >2L removed.

Major complications — Life-threatening complications: VTE (10-100x risk), ovarian torsion (6-16%), ovarian rupture, AKI, ARDS, electrolyte disturbances.

VTE prophylaxis — All moderate-severe OHSS need VTE prophylaxis — enoxaparin 40mg SC daily, continue throughout illness, 3 months postpartum if pregnant.

Ovarian torsion — Torsion risk 6-16% in severe OHSS — sudden unilateral pain, absent Doppler flow, surgical emergency; flow may be present despite torsion.

Renal complications — AKI from prerenal volume depletion — aggressive IV resuscitation, avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs), paracentesis if compartment syndrome, nephrology if Cr>2.0.

Disposition decision — Admit if: Hct>45%, Cr>1.0, intractable N/V, oliguria, respiratory symptoms, torsion concern; discharge only if mild, tolerating PO, reliable REI f/u.

Discharge — mild OHSS — Discharge with strict return precautions. REI follow-up within 24-48 hours. Daily weights and hydration monitoring.

Admit — floor/telemetry — Admit to floor/telemetry. IV fluids, VTE prophylaxis, close lab monitoring. Consult REI.

Admit — ICU — ICU admission required. Multidisciplinary care: REI, nephrology, pulmonary, hematology. Aggressive supportive care.

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for complete history-taking detail, all risk factors, and full complication management.

Reference — REI communication & prognosis (not tied to a decision step)

Always contact the REI (fertility) clinic with: patient identifiers and REI clinic/physician; timeline (hCG trigger date, retrieval date, embryo transfer status, pregnancy status); current clinical status (severity classification, vitals, key labs — Hct, Cr, electrolytes; ultrasound findings); ED management given (fluids, medications, procedures); and the disposition plan. REI can modify future stimulation protocols, has baseline labs for comparison, and this preserves continuity of care.

If not pregnant: OHSS resolves with the next menstrual period, usually 7-10 days after onset, once hCG clears. If pregnant: OHSS may worsen initially (rising endogenous hCG), peaks at 8-10 weeks gestation, then gradually improves — can take weeks to fully resolve. Fertility is preserved; no evidence of long-term ovarian damage or adverse offspring effects. Mortality is rare with modern management (deaths usually from VTE or ARDS) — prevention and early recognition are key. Prevention in future cycles: lower gonadotropin doses, GnRH agonist trigger instead of hCG, freeze-all embryos, dopamine agonists (cabergoline).
