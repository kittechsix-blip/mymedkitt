Cervical Spine Injury: Clear It, Image It, Protect the Cord

Adults and children with blunt trauma and suspected cervical spine injury in the ED. Covers clinical clearance (CCR/NEXUS), CT/MRI strategy, upper and subaxial fracture patterns, spinal cord injury syndromes and resuscitation, and disposition. Excludes penetrating neck trauma and isolated soft-tissue whiplash without red flags.

⚠ Hangman's fracture Type IIa (Levine-Edwards): do NOT apply traction — this is a flexion injury and traction worsens displacement. Treat with halo immobilization instead.
⚠ Atlanto-occipital dislocation is often fatal — rigid immobilization, avoid neck manipulation, and obtain emergency spine surgery consultation immediately; survivors usually have severe permanent neurologic deficit.
⚠ Methylprednisolone is STRONGLY DISCOURAGED for acute traumatic spinal cord injury (AANS/CNS guidelines) — no Level I/II evidence of benefit, and it increases infection, wound complications, and GI bleeding risk. Do not give steroids for acute SCI.

1 · Recognition

Assume injury until cleared
ATLS principle: assume cervical spine injury until cleared in all trauma patients. Secondary injury from hypotension or hypoxemia may exceed the primary injury — prioritize ABCs before spine-specific workup.[1][2]
High-risk mechanism
Fall ≥3 ft or 5 stairs
Axial load (diving, falling object)
MVC >100 km/hr, rollover, ejection
Motorized recreation vehicle collision
Bicycle vs vehicle or ejection

Red flags: midline cervical tenderness, neurologic deficit, altered mental status, intoxication, painful distracting injury.

2 · Clinical decision rules

RuleSensitivityNotes
Canadian C-Spine Rule (CCR)99-100%Includes mechanism; works in intoxicated-if-alert; age ≥65 mandates imaging — preferred
NEXUS Criteria94%Simpler, 5 criteria, no age cutoffs; less sensitive in elderly
  1. High-risk factor? Age ≥65, dangerous mechanism (fall ≥3 ft/5 stairs, axial load, MVC >100 km/hr/rollover/ejection, motorized recreation collision, bicycle collision), or paresthesias in extremities — any YES mandates imaging.
  2. No high-risk factor → any low-risk factor present (simple rear-end MVC, sitting position in ED, ambulatory at any time, delayed-onset neck pain, no midline cervical tenderness)? If none present, imaging required.
  3. Low-risk factor present → patient ACTIVELY rotates neck 45° left and right (never passively manipulate). Full ROM both directions = no imaging required.
NEXUS criterion (all 5 required to clear)
No midline cervical tenderness
No focal neurologic deficit
Normal alertness
No intoxication
No painful distracting injury

Clinically cleared: remove the cervical collar, document which rule was used and all criteria assessed, and give return precautions — new numbness, tingling, or weakness means return immediately.[3][4]

4 · Imaging strategy

CT is the primary modality — axial images occiput to T1 with sagittal/coronal reconstructions, ~98% sensitivity for fracture. Plain films are no longer first-line for screening. If a cervical injury is found, image the full thoracolumbar spine — occult injuries occur in 20%. Consider CTA for vertebral artery dissection.

MRI indication
Neurologic deficit with negative CT
Persistent symptoms/neck pain with negative CT
Ligamentous injury suspected
SCI evaluation — ideally within 72 hours

SyndromeFindingsPrognosis

8 · SCI resuscitation & management

MAP 85-90 mmHg for 3-7 days (SBP ≥90-100 mmHg minimum) is associated with improved neurologic outcomes — strongest evidence in the first 72 hours. Norepinephrine is first-line. In neurogenic shock (hypotension + bradycardia), start with IV fluids (often sufficient); avoid phenylephrine alone (pure alpha-1 → reflex bradycardia worsens); use atropine or glycopyrrolate for bradycardia. C2-C4 injuries carry high risk for respiratory failure — edema peaks days 3-6; consider early tracheostomy.

Methylprednisolone — not recommended
AANS/CNS guidelines: "Administration of methylprednisolone for the treatment of acute spinal cord injury is not recommended." No Level I/II evidence of benefit; NASCIS trials heavily criticized; no FDA approval for this indication. Risks: increased infection, wound complications, GI bleeding, possible increased mortality.

Early decompression (<24 hours) is associated with improved neurologic outcomes, especially for incomplete injuries (STASCIS trial). Urgent surgery indications: progressive neurologic deterioration, unstable fractures, persistent cord compression, incomplete injury with compression. Consult neurosurgery/spine immediately for any SCI, unstable fracture, or uncertain stability.

DVT prophylaxis within 72 hours (24h post-op if surgery), chemical + mechanical. 15% VTE rate despite prophylaxis. Prophylactic IVC filters are NOT recommended.
