Ankle Fractures: Ottawa Rules → Weber Classification → Operative vs Non-Operative

Adults presenting to the ED with acute ankle injury and suspected fracture. Ottawa Ankle Rules criteria are for adults; the tree routes patients with age <2 or >55, intoxication, or a distracting injury straight to X-ray rather than applying OAR. Excludes isolated midfoot injury (Ottawa Foot Rules noted but not modeled as its own branch) and pediatric ankle-fracture-specific patterns (Salter-Harris growth-plate injuries).

⚠ Maisonneuve fracture — do not miss it. Proximal fibula fracture + complete syndesmotic disruption + medial injury. Commonly missed because ankle pain dominates and the proximal fibula is not examined or imaged on ankle-only films. Red flags: medial malleolus fracture WITHOUT a lateral malleolus fracture, syndesmotic widening on ankle films, tenderness over the proximal fibula, high-ankle-sprain findings, or a Weber C pattern. Palpate the ENTIRE fibula from ankle to knee — proximal fibula tenderness is Maisonneuve until proven otherwise. Get full tibia/fibula AP + lateral films. ALL require operative fixation, non-weight-bearing, posterior splint, ortho consult in the ED.
⚠ Bimalleolar / trimalleolar fractures are ALL inherently unstable and ALL require operative fixation — do not manage as an isolated non-operative fracture. Assess neurovascular status, reduce if grossly displaced/dislocated, posterior splint in neutral, strict non-weight-bearing, ortho consult (admit vs next-day OR).
⚠ Weber C (suprasyndesmotic) fractures are almost always unstable by definition — the syndesmosis is disrupted. Virtually all require operative fixation with a syndesmotic screw; rule out Maisonneuve fracture by palpating the entire fibula to the knee before assuming this is an isolated ankle injury.

1 · Ottawa Ankle Rules

Ottawa Ankle Rules (OAR) sensitivity for excluding fracture is 97.6%, with NPV >99%.

  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge of the lateral malleolus (distal 6cm)
  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge of the medial malleolus (distal 6cm)
  • Inability to bear weight (4 steps) immediately AND in the ED

2 · X-ray findings & Weber classification

Standard views: AP, Mortise (15° internal rotation), Lateral. Assess fibula fracture level (Weber classification), medial malleolus (fracture or widened medial clear space), posterior malleolus (lateral view), and mortise alignment.

No fracture on X-ray — consider occult injury
Consider: occult fracture (MRI sensitivity > CT for subtle injury), syndesmotic injury without fracture (high ankle sprain), osteochondral lesion of the talus, or stress fracture. Squeeze test (compress tibia/fibula at mid-calf; ankle pain = positive) and external rotation test (knee bent, externally rotate the foot; pain = positive). If syndesmotic injury is suspected: weight-bearing films if possible, consider outpatient MRI, ortho follow-up within 5-7 days. Otherwise treat as a sprain with close follow-up.
Weber A — infrasyndesmotic
Usually stable, syndesmosis intact, often an avulsion fracture from the lateral ligament, low-energy mechanism. Medial clear space normal ≤4 mm; widened >4 mm = unstable. Stable (isolated) → non-operative (walking boot or short leg cast, weight-bearing as tolerated). Unstable (medial involvement) → operative.

3 · Weber B — stable or unstable?

StableUnstable
Mortise congruentMortise widened
Medial clear space ≤4mmMedial clear space >4mm
No medial tendernessMedial malleolus fracture or deltoid rupture
Negative stress testPositive stress test

Used when stability is uncertain on non-weight-bearing films: weight-bearing mortise view, gravity stress view (external rotation, leg hanging), or manual stress under fluoro (usually ortho). Positive test: medial clear space widens >4-5 mm under stress — indicates deltoid ligament rupture, fracture is unstable. If pain prevents weight-bearing: short-term immobilization, repeat films in 5-7 days, ortho follow-up arranged. Pearl: if in doubt, treat as unstable and involve ortho.

4 · Do not miss — Weber C, Maisonneuve, bimalleolar

Weber C — suprasyndesmotic
Fibula fracture ABOVE the syndesmosis — almost always unstable, syndesmosis disrupted by definition, higher energy mechanism, may have associated medial injury. Rule out Maisonneuve fracture — palpate the entire fibula to the knee; if proximal tenderness, get full leg films. Virtually all require operative fixation with syndesmotic screw placement; posterior splint, strict non-weight-bearing; ortho consult in the ED. Complications if missed: chronic instability, post-traumatic arthritis, poor functional outcomes.
Maisonneuve fracture — do not miss
Proximal fibula fracture + complete syndesmotic disruption + medial injury. Commonly missed because ankle pain dominates and the proximal fibula is not examined, and the proximal fracture is not visible on ankle films.
  • Medial malleolus fracture without a lateral malleolus fracture
  • Syndesmotic widening on ankle films
  • Tenderness over the proximal fibula
  • High ankle sprain findings
  • Weber C fracture pattern
Maisonneuve — management
Exam: palpate the ENTIRE fibula from ankle to knee, squeeze test — proximal fibula tenderness is Maisonneuve until proven otherwise. Full tibia/fibula X-rays (AP + lateral). ALL require operative fixation, non-weight-bearing, posterior splint, ortho consult in the ED.
Bimalleolar / trimalleolar — all unstable, all operative
Bimalleolar = both lateral AND medial malleoli fractured. Trimalleolar = lateral + medial + posterior malleolus (posterior tibial lip). ALL are inherently unstable, ALL require operative fixation, higher complication rates than unimalleolar injury; posterior malleolus involvement affects outcomes. ED management: assess neurovascular status, reduce if grossly displaced/dislocated, posterior splint in neutral, strict non-weight-bearing, ice and elevation, ortho consult (admission vs next-day OR). Timing of surgery: within 24 hours is optimal; delay >12 days is associated with worse outcomes; may delay for severe swelling (wait for the “wrinkle sign”).

6 · Operative management

Indications for ORIF: Weber C (suprasyndesmotic), unstable Weber B, bimalleolar or trimalleolar, Maisonneuve fracture, ankle dislocation (after reduction), intra-articular displacement >2mm.

  1. Neurovascular check — document pulse, sensation, motor
  2. Reduction if dislocated (procedural sedation)
  3. Posterior splint — ankle at 90°, well-padded
  4. Non-weight-bearing
  5. Ice and elevation — critical for swelling
  6. Analgesia — consider nerve block
  7. Ortho consult — same day
Follow-up — operative fractures
Ortho follow-up for operative ankle fractures: 24-48 hours for OR planning. Surgery timing within 24h is optimal; may delay for soft-tissue swelling.

7 · Non-operative management

Indications: stable Weber A, stable Weber B (congruent mortise, no medial injury), isolated lateral malleolus avulsion.

  • Walking boot (preferred) — allows early weight-bearing
  • Posterior splint if significant swelling initially
  • Short leg cast if non-compliance concern
  • Weight-bearing as tolerated from day 1 — modern evidence-based approach
  • Traditional non-weight-bearing ×6 weeks no longer recommended for most stable fractures
Duration & follow-up — non-operative fractures
Boot/cast 4-6 weeks; healing 10-12 weeks. Ortho follow-up within 5-7 days to confirm alignment with repeat films, then progress to ROM exercises as tolerated.

  • Elevate above heart level as much as possible for the first 48-72h
  • Ice 20 min every 2-3 hours while awake
  • Weight-bearing per ortho instructions (usually WBAT in boot for stable fractures)

  • Numbness or tingling in the toes
