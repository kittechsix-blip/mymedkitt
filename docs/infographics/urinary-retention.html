Acute Urinary Retention: Red Flags to Catheterization to Disposition

Adults presenting to the ED with acute urinary retention, predominantly male and BPH-driven. Women and children are addressed only via the red-flag / neurogenic pathway — the source consult notes AUR is rare in these populations without underlying pathology, and neurologic causes should be strongly considered.

⚠ Cauda equina syndrome (saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness, fecal incontinence, progressive back pain) — place a Foley for decompression, obtain emergent MRI lumbar spine, and consult neurosurgery STAT. This is a surgical emergency; time to decompression correlates with neurologic recovery.
⚠ Suspected urethral trauma (blood at the urethral meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal ecchymosis, pelvic fracture mechanism) — do NOT attempt blind urethral catheterization. Obtain a retrograde urethrogram first; suprapubic catheter by urology if disruption is confirmed.
⚠ Urosepsis (fever plus hemodynamic instability with an obstructed, infected system) — place a Foley immediately, draw blood cultures x2, resuscitate with 30 mL/kg crystalloid, and give antibiotics within 1 hour.
⚠ Women and children with acute urinary retention are rare — strongly consider neurologic causes (cauda equina, spinal cord pathology) rather than BPH.
⚠ Do NOT replace 100% of urine output during post-obstructive diuresis — matching the full volume perpetuates the diuresis. Replace only 50-75% of the prior hour's output.
⚠ In uncircumcised men, ALWAYS reduce the foreskin after catheter placement to prevent paraphimosis.
⚠ Rapid vs. gradual bladder drainage is equivalent — evidence does not support clamping at 200-500 mL intervals; complete drainage is safe.

Post-Catheterization & Post-Obstructive Diuresis

Document initial drained volume. Hematuria ex vacuo (brief, self-limited hematuria after rapid decompression) is common and benign, usually resolving within hours. Send urinalysis/culture and BMP (especially if high volume or suspected chronic retention). Post-obstructive diuresis (POD) = urine output >200 mL/hr for 2+ consecutive hours OR >3000 mL in 24 hours.

Physiologic POD self-limits (~24 hours) — excretion of retained fluid/solutes. Pathologic POD persists >48 hours from tubular dysfunction and requires intervention. Risk factors: bilateral obstruction (or unilateral with a solitary kidney), chronic retention with elevated creatinine, CHF/edema. Monitor UOP q2h, vitals q6-8h, BMP q12-24h (Na, K, Mg, Phos); spot urine Na >40 mEq/L suggests salt-wasting.

General principle: replace 50-75% of the prior hour's urine output with 0.45% NS — do NOT match 100%, this perpetuates diuresis. Encourage oral fluids if the patient can drink. When diuresis slows (<200 mL/hr), reduce replacement to 50% of output, then transition to maintenance fluids (80-100 mL/hr). Monitor and replace K+, Mg, and phosphorus as needed.

  • POD with electrolyte abnormalities
  • AKI (elevated creatinine)
  • Hemodynamic instability
  • Unable to maintain oral intake
  • Refractory diuresis >48 hours → nephrology and/or ICU involvement

Common medication causes of AUR to discontinue if possible: anticholinergics, antihistamines, decongestants, opioids, TCAs.

TWOC = removing the catheter and assessing ability to void. Optimal timing: 3-7 days after starting an alpha-blocker — early TWOC (3 days) has similar success to late (7 days); longer catheterization has more complications (UTI, leakage). Success factors: age <70, retention volume <1000 mL, identifiable precipitant, alpha-blocker pretreatment. Failure predictors: age >70, volume >1000 mL, prostate volume >40 mL, previous AUR episode. Procedure: patient takes morning alpha-blocker, remove catheter in AM, void when urge develops, check post-void residual (PVR). Success = void >150 mL with PVR <100 mL.

BPH (most common in men >50): gradual onset of LUTS, history of urinary hesitancy. Medication-induced: recent anticholinergic/opioid/decongestant/antihistamine start, often precipitating in a patient with underlying BPH. Neurogenic: diabetic neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, prior spinal cord injury or surgery. Infectious: prostatitis, UTI. Malignancy (less common): prostate cancer, bladder cancer, new pelvic mass.

  • Catheter leg bag: empty when half to two-thirds full, keep below bladder level, night drainage bag for sleeping
  • Clean connection site with alcohol when switching bags; increase fluid intake to prevent blockage
  • Return precautions: fever/chills, no urine draining, significant hematuria with clots, severe pain, catheter falls out
  • Urology follow-up in 3-7 days for TWOC attempt
  • Discontinue precipitating medication if identified; treat constipation if contributing

Neurogenic bladder workup: urology referral for urodynamic studies, neurology evaluation if not already established, consider EMG/MRI if etiology unclear. May need long-term catheterization (indwelling or intermittent self-catheterization). Anticholinergics for an overactive-bladder component are a specialist decision; botox injections to the bladder are performed by urology. If diabetic neuropathy: emphasize glycemic control and consider intermittent self-catheterization training.

Red flags suggesting malignancy: weight loss, night sweats, bone pain, hard/nodular prostate on DRE, gross hematuria (especially painless), rapidly progressive symptoms, known GU malignancy history. ED workup: BMP, CBC, PSA (note: catheterization may falsely elevate it), CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast if gross hematuria or a palpable mass. Consider admission for significant hematuria requiring irrigation, spinal cord compression concern, or severe pain requiring IV analgesia. Acute urinary retention suspected — Inability to void; bladder scan >300 mL confirms retention. Four cause categories: structural, medication/toxicologic, neurogenic, infectious.

Red flags present? — Screen for cauda equina (saddle anesthesia, bilateral weakness, fecal incontinence), urethral trauma (blood at meatus, pelvic fracture, perineal hematoma), and sepsis (fever + hemodynamic instability).

Neurologic emergency (cauda equina) — Foley for decompression, emergent MRI lumbar spine, neurosurgery STAT. Dexamethasone 10 mg IV if malignancy/epidural abscess suspected. Women/children: strongly consider neuro causes.

Urethral trauma — no blind catheterization — Retrograde urethrogram (RUG) before any catheter attempt. Suprapubic catheter by urology if RUG shows disruption; gentle Foley may proceed if RUG normal.

Urosepsis — emergent decompression — Foley immediately, blood cultures x2, UA/culture, 30 mL/kg crystalloid, antibiotics within 1h. ICU if persistent hypotension; urology for source control if hydronephrosis.

Bladder volume? — Bedside US: volume = height × width × depth × 0.75. <300 mL = not true retention; 300-1000 mL = standard; >1000 mL = high volume, increased POD risk.

Not true retention — evaluate alternatives — Consider UTI/cystitis, prostatitis, pelvic pain syndromes, psychogenic hesitancy. Workup: UA, prostate exam, imaging if indicated.

Standard Foley catheterization — Sterile technique, liberal lubrication, insert to hub before inflating balloon. 16-18 Fr standard; coude tip first-line in older men with suspected BPH.

First attempt failed — troubleshoot — Try coude catheter (curve UP), additional lidocaine jelly, larger or smaller catheter, Seldinger technique. Common failure points: false passage, BPH obstruction, stricture.

Post-catheterization: monitor output — Document initial volume, send UA/culture + BMP. Hematuria ex vacuo is benign and self-limited. POD = >200 mL/hr x2h+ OR >3000 mL/24h.

Urology consult — Flexible cystoscopy with guidewire-assisted placement, or suprapubic catheter. Avoid further blind attempts — risk of false passage and bleeding.

Suprapubic catheter placed — Dressing care, may shower after 24-48h. Urology follow-up 1-2 weeks. Start tamsulosin now for TWOC optimization. Watch for bowel perforation (rare), bleeding, dislodgement.

Start alpha-blocker at time of catheterization — Tamsulosin 0.4 mg PO daily, onset 4-8h, alpha-1A selective. Increases TWOC success 26%→48% (OR 2.47). Finasteride 5 mg is an adjunct — not helpful acutely, takes 3-6 months.

Post-obstructive diuresis — monitor — Physiologic POD self-limits (~24h); pathologic POD persists >48h (tubular dysfunction). Risk factors: bilateral obstruction, chronic retention with elevated Cr, CHF/edema. Monitor UOP q2h, BMP q12-24h.

POD fluid replacement — Replace 50-75% of prior-hour UOP with 0.45% NS — do NOT match 100%. When diuresis slows <200 mL/hr, reduce to 50% then transition to maintenance fluids. Admit if electrolyte abnormality, AKI, instability, or refractory >48h.

Trial without catheter (TWOC) — Optimal 3-7 days after starting alpha-blocker; early (3d) ≈ late (7d) success. Success = void >150 mL with PVR <100 mL. Failure predictors: age >70, volume >1000 mL, prostate >40 mL, prior AUR.

Determine underlying etiology — BPH (most common >50yo), medication-induced, neurogenic (diabetic neuropathy, MS, cord injury), infectious (prostatitis, UTI), or malignancy (prostate/bladder ca, new pelvic mass).

Discharge — BPH / medication-induced — Discharge with Foley in place, tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, leg-bag instructions, urology follow-up 3-7 days for TWOC. ~50% recurrence within 1 year.

Complex / neurogenic — specialist referral — Urology referral for urodynamics; neurology evaluation if not established. May need long-term catheterization (indwelling or intermittent self-cath). Diabetic neuropathy: emphasize glycemic control.
