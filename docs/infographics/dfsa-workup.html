Drug-Facilitated Sexual Assault — ED Workup

Adults and adolescents presenting to the ED after suspected drug-facilitated sexual assault (DFSA). Time-driven forensic evidence workup — GHB and most DFSA agents have narrow detection windows. Covers medical stabilization, tiered informed consent, specimen collection by time-since-assault, forensic drug panel selection, SANE/forensic exam coordination, and prophylaxis (STI, pregnancy, Hepatitis B, HIV PEP) through disposition. HIV PEP regimen selection and 28-day follow-up are detailed in the companion PEP Consult, not reproduced here.

⚠ TIME-CRITICAL: GHB is undetectable after 6 hours in blood and 12 hours in urine — collect specimens immediately, before other forensic procedures.
⚠ Standard hospital drug screens do NOT detect GHB, flunitrazepam metabolites at relevant concentrations, zolpidem/zaleplon/zopiclone, or low-dose ketamine, and are not forensically admissible — specimens must go to a state crime lab or forensic reference laboratory.
⚠ The patient may consent to a forensic exam and specimen collection independently of filing a police report — specimens can be held by the hospital or crime lab pending the patient's decision.
⚠ Place clothing in paper bags, never plastic — plastic promotes bacterial growth and degrades DNA evidence.
⚠ Document objective findings only — do not state whether an assault occurred, characterize injuries as consistent/inconsistent with assault, or offer opinions on patient credibility.
⚠ 75% of DFSA cases involve multiple substances — polydrug facilitation is the norm, not the exception.

Initial assessment

DFSA Steps Summary

This consult is time-driven. Detection windows for DFSA agents are narrow — GHB is undetectable after 6 hours in blood and 12 hours in urine.

Standard hospital drug screens miss most DFSA drugs. Forensic specimens must go to a state crime lab or forensic reference laboratory.

Drug Detection Windows — full reference table
Forensic Specimen Guide — tube types, volumes, chain of custody

  • Amnesia disproportionate to reported alcohol intake
  • Unexplained injuries or soreness (especially genital/anal)
  • Intermittent consciousness or "blackout" periods
  • Waking undressed or in an unfamiliar location
  • Uncertain whether sexual contact occurred
  • Witnesses describe sudden incapacitation
  • Residual sedation, confusion, or ataxia on presentation
  • Reported unusual taste in a drink
Polydrug is the norm
75% of DFSA cases involve multiple substances — suspect drug facilitation whenever any red flag above is present.

Assess vitals, GCS, and perform a trauma assessment. Check for: strangulation signs (petechiae, voice changes, neck tenderness), genital/anal trauma, bite marks, defensive injuries (forearms, hands), and residual drug effects (sedation, respiratory depression).

Medical stabilization required
Address life threats before the forensic workup. Preserve evidence while stabilizing: clothing in paper bags (not plastic); minimize bathing, oral care, and voiding if safely possible; delay urinary catheter and rectal exam if not emergently needed; save the ET tube for toxicology if intubated; document all injuries encountered during resuscitation. Return to this consult once stable — every hour of delay narrows detection windows.

Consent & safety

The patient has the right to consent to each element independently. VAWA Protections — federal rights for sexual assault survivors.

Capacity assessment: can the patient understand the risks, benefits, and alternatives? If residual sedation is impairing capacity, proceed with time-critical specimen collection while documenting the clinical rationale.

  • Forensic medical examination
  • Injury photography
  • Specimen collection (blood, urine, hair)
  • Law enforcement notification
  • Release of evidence to police
No police report required
The patient does NOT need to file a police report to receive a forensic exam or have specimens collected. Specimens can be held by the hospital or crime lab.

Even without a forensic exam, offer: STI prophylaxis (gonorrhea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis); pregnancy prophylaxis (emergency contraception); HIV PEP assessment — PEP Consult; Hepatitis B vaccination (if unvaccinated); a safety assessment; and victim advocacy referral. Consider offering urine collection for toxicology — the patient can hold the specimen while deciding whether to involve law enforcement.

Document the refusal. Specimens can still be collected up to 5 days post-assault (limited utility after 72h). Hair collection is possible at 4-6 weeks post-assault and detects all major DFSA agents. The patient may change their mind at any time. Proceed with the forensic exam (if consented) and prophylaxis.

Forensic Specimen Guide — tube types and volumes

URGENT: < 6 hour collection
GHB IS STILL DETECTABLE — COLLECT IMMEDIATELY. Collect both blood and urine before any other forensic procedures.

Blood: gray-top (NaF/KOx) preserves GHB and prevents in-vitro production; lavender-top (EDTA) for standard forensic toxicology; additional red-top for clinical labs (BAL, hepatitis, HIV).

Urine: first-void specimen, do NOT discard, minimum 50-100 mL, no preservative-containing UA cups.

Chain of custody: label with patient name, DOB, date/time, collector name; seal with tamper-evident tape; document transfer at every step.

Forensic Specimen Guide
6-12 hour collection
GHB may still be detectable in urine (window up to 12h) — collect urine urgently, first-void, 50-100 mL.

Blood: gray-top (NaF) — GHB unlikely but still collect; lavender-top (EDTA) — benzodiazepines, ketamine, zolpidem, opioids still detectable.

Still detectable at 6-12h: flunitrazepam (blood 24-72h), ketamine (blood 24-48h), zolpidem (blood up to 8h, borderline), benzodiazepines (blood 6-12h), antihistamines (blood 24h), opioids (blood 24-48h).

Drug Detection Windows

Urine is the primary specimen. GHB window is closed.

Urine (primary): flunitrazepam metabolite (up to 72h), ketamine/norketamine (up to 96h), benzodiazepines (24-72h), zolpidem (up to 24h), opioids (48-96h), antihistamines (48-72h), EtG/EtS (up to 80h).

Blood (secondary): flunitrazepam (up to 72h), ketamine (up to 48h), opioids (up to 48h).

Collect both gray-top and lavender-top blood tubes plus first-void urine.

Urine only. Blood is unlikely to yield results at this interval.

Still potentially detectable: cannabinoids (weeks, chronic use), EtG/EtS (up to 80h), some long-acting benzodiazepines, opioids (48-96h), norketamine (up to 96h).

Plan hair collection at the 4-6 week follow-up — much longer window, detects all major DFSA agents. Discuss with the patient and document the follow-up plan.

Standard blood and urine toxicology are unlikely to yield positive results at this interval. Hair collection is the primary option: collect at 4-6 weeks post-assault, pencil-thickness bundle from the posterior vertex, cut at the scalp (do NOT pluck), wrap in aluminum foil, label the proximal end, send to a forensic reference laboratory (NMS Labs, Axiom Testing, RTL).

Hair detects: GHB, flunitrazepam, ketamine, zolpidem, benzodiazepines, opioids, cannabinoids, antihistamines, and other DFSA agents.

Still collect urine if the patient presents — some long-acting agents may persist.

Drug panel selection

Hospital drug screens are INADEQUATE for DFSA. Standard hospital UDS/immunoassay does NOT detect GHB, flunitrazepam metabolites at relevant concentrations, zolpidem/zaleplon/zopiclone, or low-dose ketamine — and is not forensically admissible (lacks confirmation testing and chain of custody).

Specimens MUST go to a state crime lab or forensic reference laboratory: NMS Labs (Willow Grove, PA), Axiom Testing (San Diego, CA), Redwood Toxicology Laboratory (Santa Rosa, CA), or your state/regional crime lab. These labs use GC-MS/LC-MS/MS confirmation per SOFT/ANSI Standard 121.

SOFT Recommended Panel — complete analyte list

A hospital tox screen is useful for clinical management (identifying acute intoxication, guiding treatment) but does NOT replace the forensic panel.

  • CBC, CMP
  • Urinalysis
  • Urine pregnancy test
  • Blood alcohol level
  • HIV Ag/Ab combo (4th gen)
  • Hepatitis B panel (HBsAg, HBsAb, HBcAb)
  • Hepatitis C Ab
  • RPR/VDRL
Keep forensic and clinical specimens SEPARATE
Label each specimen with patient name, DOB, date/time, collector. Seal with tamper-evident tape. Complete a chain-of-custody form documenting every transfer. Store refrigerated (NOT frozen) until crime lab pickup — different chain-of-custody requirements from clinical specimens.

SANE & forensic exam

SANE-collected evidence is significantly more likely to be admissible and complete. SANE exams have higher prosecution rates than non-SANE exams.

  • ED role: medical stabilization
  • ED role: time-critical toxicology specimen collection (do NOT wait for SANE if <6h)
  • ED role: prophylaxis initiation (STI, pregnancy, HIV PEP)
  • ED role: clinical lab ordering and medical documentation
  • SANE role: forensic evidence collection kit (swabs, combings)
  • SANE role: detailed injury documentation and photography
  • SANE role: evidence packaging and chain of custody
  • SANE role: patient advocacy coordination and forensic-specific history

Contact RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline: 1-800-656-4673 for guidance. Consider transfer to a SANE-equipped facility if the patient is stable, consents, and transfer will not close critical specimen windows.

If performing evidence collection, use your facility's sexual assault evidence collection kit and follow its instructions exactly — oral/vaginal/anal swabs, fingernail scrapings, pubic hair combings, dried secretion swabs. Maintain chain of custody and document all findings with body diagrams.

  • Each item in a separate PAPER bag — NOT plastic (plastic promotes bacterial growth and degrades DNA)
  • If wearing the same clothes as during the assault, collect all items
  • If patient changed clothes, attempt to obtain original clothing from home/car
  • Paper sheet under the patient while undressing to catch trace evidence
  • Fingernail scrapings/cuttings if scratching occurred
  • Dried secretion swabs (Wood's lamp can identify)
  • Bitemark swabs for saliva DNA

Document in the medical record: patient statements in direct quotes; time of presentation and reported time of assault; all injuries (location, size, color, shape, age estimation); specimens collected with date/time; chain-of-custody transfers; consents obtained and declined; all providers involved. Use body diagrams and measurement scales in all injury documentation.

Document objective findings only
Do NOT state whether you believe an assault occurred, characterize injuries as "consistent with" or "inconsistent with" assault, offer opinions on patient credibility, or include irrelevant social or sexual history.

Prophylaxis

Three categories of prophylaxis, each offered independently — the patient may accept some and decline others: (1) STI prophylaxis — gonorrhea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis; (2) pregnancy prophylaxis — emergency contraception; (3) HIV PEP — based on exposure risk. Plus Hepatitis B vaccination if unvaccinated.

Ulipristal acetate (ella) is preferred — effective up to 5 days post-exposure with no decrease in efficacy over time. Levonorgestrel (Plan B) is an alternative — most effective within 72h, efficacy decreases with time and higher body weight. Only if source is known HBV-positive. Different injection site from the vaccine. Most effective within 24h of exposure, up to 7 days.

Higher risk — PEP recommended: receptive anal intercourse, source known HIV-positive with detectable viral load, mucosal exposure to blood. Lower risk — PEP case-by-case: receptive vaginal intercourse, source HIV status unknown, insertive anal/vaginal intercourse.

PEP must be started within 72 hours of exposure — earlier is better. Preferred regimen: Biktarvy (BIC/TAF/FTC) × 28 days. Start ASAP — do not delay for source testing. Baseline: HIV Ag/Ab, CBC, CMP, Hep B/C. Follow-up HIV testing at 4-6 weeks and 3 months.

Safety & disposition

  • Is the assailant known to the patient?
  • Does the patient share housing with the assailant?
  • Does the patient have a safe place to go tonight?
  • Is there an ongoing threat?
  • Are there children at risk?
  • Does the patient have access to transportation?
Unsafe discharge — activate safety resources
Social work consult (immediate). Victim advocacy — facility advocate or RAINN: 1-800-656-4673. Domestic violence shelter referral — National DV Hotline: 1-800-799-7233. Safety planning with patient and advocate. Law enforcement notification if patient consents and an immediate threat exists.

If no safe discharge option: consider social admit for observation, coordinate emergency housing. Do NOT discharge to an environment where the assailant has access.

Patient Handout — shareable follow-up guide with resources

Follow-up schedule: 2 weeks — STI testing (NAAT GC/CT), pregnancy test. 4-6 weeks — hair specimen collection if planned, HIV test (if PEP started). 3 months — repeat full STI panel (GC, CT, syphilis, HIV, Hep B/C). 6 months — final HIV test, Hep B vaccine dose 3.

Resources: RAINN 1-800-656-4673 (24/7); National DV Hotline 1-800-799-7233; Crisis Text Line — text HOME to 741741; local victim advocacy; follow-up with PCP or GYN.

Medication follow-up: complete doxycycline 7-day course (if STI prophylaxis given); complete PEP 28-day course (if HIV PEP initiated); Hep B vaccine doses 2 and 3 (if initiated).

Workup algorithm

DFSA ED Workup: Initial Assessment — Time-driven. GHB undetectable after 6h blood / 12h urine. Standard hospital drug screens miss most DFSA drugs.

Clinical red flags for DFSA — 75% of DFSA cases involve multiple substances — polydrug facilitation is the norm.

Medically stable? — Assess vitals, GCS, trauma exam — strangulation signs, genital/anal trauma, defensive injuries, residual sedation.

Stabilize first; preserve evidence — Paper bags for clothing, minimize bathing/voiding, delay catheter/rectal exam if safe, save ET tube for toxicology.

Time since assault? — GHB has the shortest detection window (4-6h blood, 3-12h urine) of any DFSA agent.

Informed consent framework — Patient consents to each element independently. Police report NOT required for exam or specimen collection.

Consents to forensic exam? — Patient may decline any component at any time and stop the exam at any point.

Exam declined: still offer STI/EC/HIV PEP/HepB/safety — Offer STI prophylaxis, EC, HIV PEP assessment, Hep B vaccine, safety assessment, advocacy referral, and consider urine collection.

Consents to specimen collection? — Specimens can be held by hospital/crime lab — no police report required now.

Consents to injury photography? — Photo documentation strengthens forensic evidence; written description only if declined.

Specimen collection declined — Document refusal. Specimens still possible up to 5 days; hair collection possible at 4-6 weeks.

Specimen collection priority? — Confirms time since assault to guide blood/urine/hair collection priority.

< 6h: collect blood + urine NOW — Gray-top (NaF/KOx) + lavender-top blood; first-void urine 50-100 mL. Chain of custody mandatory.

6-12h: urine priority, GHB may persist — Urine first-void urgent; blood gray-top + lavender-top for other agents.

12-72h: urine primary, GHB window closed — Urine primary specimen; blood secondary (flunitrazepam, ketamine, opioids up to 48-96h).

72h-5d: urine only, limited panel — Cannabinoids, EtG/EtS, some benzodiazepines/opioids may persist. Plan hair collection at 4-6 weeks.

> 5d: plan hair collection at 4-6 weeks — Standard blood/urine tox unlikely positive. Hair detects all major DFSA agents.

Forensic drug panel (not hospital UDS) — Hospital UDS misses GHB, flunitrazepam, zolpidem, low-dose ketamine — not forensically admissible.

Order hospital tox in parallel? — Useful for clinical management; does not replace the forensic panel.

Baseline labs + specimen handling — CBC, CMP, UA, urine pregnancy, BAL, HIV, Hep B/C, RPR. Keep forensic specimens separate.

SANE nurse available? — SANE-collected evidence has higher admissibility and prosecution rates.

SANE handoff: ED stabilizes + toxicology, SANE collects evidence — ED: stabilization, time-critical tox, prophylaxis, labs. SANE: evidence kit, photography, chain of custody.

No SANE: physician-led evidence collection — RAINN 1-800-656-4673. Consider transfer if stable and consenting. Use facility SA evidence kit.

Clothing & physical evidence — Paper bags only — plastic degrades DNA. Fingernail scrapings, dried secretion swabs, bitemark swabs.

Documentation: objective findings only — Direct quotes, injury detail, specimens/consents/providers. Never opine on whether assault occurred.

Prophylaxis overview (3 categories) — STI, pregnancy, HIV PEP — each offered independently, plus Hep B vaccine if unvaccinated.

STI prophylaxis? — Ceftriaxone 500mg IM + doxycycline 100mg BID x7d + metronidazole 2g PO.

STI prophylaxis ordered — Administer ceftriaxone + metronidazole in ED; prescribe doxycycline. Pregnancy: azithromycin substitution.

Pregnancy risk? — Reproductive-age, vaginal exposure, no reliable contraception.

Emergency contraception choice — Ulipristal preferred (up to 5 days, no efficacy decline); levonorgestrel alternative (best <72h); copper IUD most effective.

EC ordered — Follow-up pregnancy test at 2 weeks. Early OB referral if positive.

Hepatitis B vaccinated / immune? — Check HBsAb from baseline labs. Immune = no further action.

Initiate Hep B vaccine ± HBIG — Dose 1 today, dose 2 at 1 month, dose 3 at 6 months. HBIG 0.06 mL/kg if source known HBV-positive.

HIV PEP risk assessment — Higher risk: receptive anal intercourse, known-positive source, mucosal blood exposure. Full workup in PEP Consult.

Initiate HIV PEP now — Start within 72h. Preferred: Biktarvy x28 days. Baseline HIV Ag/Ab, CBC, CMP, Hep B/C.

Safe to discharge? — Assailant access, safe housing tonight, ongoing threat, children at risk, transportation.

Unsafe: activate safety resources — Social work, victim advocacy (RAINN 1-800-656-4673), DV shelter (1-800-799-7233), safety planning. Do not discharge to assailant access.

Disposition & follow-up — 2wk STI/pregnancy test; 4-6wk hair/HIV test; 3mo full STI panel; 6mo final HIV test + HepB dose 3.
