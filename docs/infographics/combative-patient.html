algorithm

Combative Patient: Assess, Sedate, Restrain, Dispose

Adults ≥18 in the ED with acute agitation of any severity — psychiatric, alcohol/sedative intoxication or withdrawal, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic toxidrome, or undifferentiated. Excludes pediatrics (no pediatric dosing in the source consult).

⚠ Immediate life threat (weapon, actively violent, excited delirium features): restrain and sedate simultaneously — do not delay chemical sedation while applying restraints, and never restrain without also giving medication. Prepare RSI equipment at bedside.
⚠ Do NOT combine IM olanzapine with parenteral (IM/IV) benzodiazepines — risk of fatal respiratory depression and hypotension. Wait ≥60 min, or switch to haloperidol first before adding a benzodiazepine.
⚠ Physostigmine is contraindicated if QRS >120 ms or with known/suspected TCA ingestion — risk of asystole.
⚠ AVOID antipsychotics in anticholinergic delirium and in sympathomimetic/undifferentiated agitation — they worsen anticholinergic toxicity and lower the seizure threshold.
⚠ AVOID typical antipsychotics (haloperidol, droperidol) in Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia — high D2 blockade can trigger life-threatening neuroleptic sensitivity.
⚠ QTc >500 ms or history of torsades: avoid haloperidol and droperidol — use olanzapine (no QTc effect) instead. Obtain an ECG after every antipsychotic dose.
⚠ Antipsychotics alone are NOT adequate for alcohol or sedative withdrawal — benzodiazepines (± phenobarbital) are mandatory; antipsychotics do not treat the GABA deficit and lower the seizure threshold.
⚠ All antipsychotics carry an FDA black-box warning for increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis.
⚠ NEVER place a restrained patient prone, hogtie (wrists/ankles bound together behind the back), or stack mattresses/kneel on the chest — asphyxiation risk. Physical restraints are never a substitute for chemical sedation — administer both concurrently.
⚠ Give thiamine before glucose in alcohol-related agitation to avoid precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy.

1 · Initial assessment & scene safety

Remove sharps and weapons from reach, ensure minimum 5 people for any physical restraint, activate security, position yourself near the exit, and have one person communicate. Show of force alone avoids the need for sedation in up to 27% of cases. Obtain ABCs, vitals, and fingerstick glucose when safe.

Redirectable patients (RASS +1 to +2) may respond to verbal de-escalation and oral medication. Not redirectable (RASS +3 to +4) means severe agitation requiring immediate parenteral sedation.

Reassess 15-20 minutes after any medication or parenteral sedation dose, on either pathway.

2 · What to do now

Immediate life threat
Restrain and sedate simultaneously — do not wait for medication to work before applying restraints, and do not restrain without giving medication. Ketamine 4 mg/kg IM (fastest, onset 3-5 min) OR midazolam 5 mg IM + haloperidol 10 mg IM. Prepare RSI equipment at bedside.
Refractory — escalate
Not responding to combination therapy. Reconsider the diagnosis (hypoglycemia, hypoxia, head injury, sepsis). Escalate to ketamine 4 mg/kg IM (reduce dose 50% if given after other sedatives) or dexmedetomidine 0.2-1.5 mcg/kg/hr infusion, or proceed to RSI/intubation — a valid endpoint, not a failure.
Severe agitation — combination sedation
Select the severe-agitation etiology below to size the combination regimen. Combination therapy (antipsychotic + benzodiazepine) is faster and more effective than either agent alone.
Sedation achieved — check restraint need
RASS 0 to −2 achieved. Physical restraints are a last resort — only if needed for immediate safety while medication takes full effect; never a substitute for chemical sedation.
Restraint protocol
Team of 5 (1 person per limb + team leader who does not touch the patient). Supine, never prone. Face-to-face physician evaluation within 1 hour. Neurovascular checks every 15 minutes.
No restraints needed
Chemical sedation alone is controlling agitation — proceed directly to post-sedation monitoring.
Mild/moderate — assess special population & etiology
Verbal de-escalation is always first-line. Select special population and etiology below to size the regimen; PO is preferred when the patient will accept it. Target: RASS 0 to −1 (calm, cooperative).
Partial response — redose or add agent
Redose the same agent (antipsychotics ≥20 min apart, benzodiazepines ≥10 min apart) OR add the other class (antipsychotic alone → add a benzodiazepine; benzodiazepine alone → add an antipsychotic). Exception: if IM olanzapine was given, switch to haloperidol first — do NOT add a parenteral benzodiazepine to olanzapine (wait ≥60 min if switching agents entirely).
Plan disposition
Disposition depends on etiology, treatment response, and ongoing safety — do not attribute agitation to "psych" until medical causes are excluded.

  1. Team of 5 — 1 person per limb + 1 team leader who does not touch the patient
  2. Arms first (greatest threat), then legs
  3. Secure each limb to the bed frame — never the side rails
  4. Patient supine — NEVER prone (asphyxiation risk)
  5. Diagonal pattern: right wrist to right upper rail, left wrist to left lower rail
  6. Head of bed elevated
  7. NEVER hogtie, stack mattresses, place a knee on the chest or back, or leave a restrained patient unmonitored
  8. Remove watches, glasses, and other potential weapons from reach
  9. Always administer chemical sedation concurrently — restraints are not a substitute
Order time limits
Adults (≥18): 4 hours maximum per order. Remove restraints as soon as the patient is calm enough to be safe — partial release (one limb at a time) to test tolerance.

Every 15 minutes: circulation checks, sensation/skin integrity, respiratory status, offer food/water/toileting. Every 1-2 hours: physician reassessment, attempt partial release. Face-to-face physician evaluation within 1 hour of initiation.

9 · Post-sedation monitoring & workup

Respiratory: continuous pulse oximetry, capnography if available, BVM and suction at bedside. Cardiac: continuous telemetry if antipsychotic given; ECG for QTc after haloperidol or droperidol — QTc >500 ms or increase >60 ms → discontinue agent, correct K+/Mg2+. Neurologic: watch for dystonia (treat with diphenhydramine or benztropine) and akathisia (do NOT escalate the antipsychotic — give a benzodiazepine instead).

Diphenhydramine
50 mg IV or IM
Treats acute dystonia. Alternative: benztropine 1-2 mg IV.
  • Fingerstick glucose (recheck)
  • BMP, CBC, VBG with lactate
  • ECG
  • Urine drug screen (interpret cautiously)
  • CT head if trauma, anticoagulation, or focal deficits
  • CK and renal function if prolonged agitation or restraints (rhabdomyolysis)

10 · Disposition

ICU admission
Intubation/RSI, refractory agitation on continuous sedation, hemodynamic instability, rhabdomyolysis (CK >5000), excited delirium features, or QTc >500 ms with arrhythmia risk.
Floor / observation admission
Ongoing alcohol withdrawal requiring serial benzodiazepine dosing, active medical workup pending, need for continued restraints/reassessment, or unclear etiology.
Psychiatric evaluation
Only after medical causes are excluded — vitals normal, labs unremarkable, baseline mental status confirmed. Refer for suicidal ideation, psychotic decompensation, or grave disability.
Discharge
Agitation resolved, at baseline mental status, vitals stable, no medical cause requiring admission, safe disposition. Minimum 1 hour observation after the last sedation dose.
Other pitfalls4 items
Do NOT give antipsychotic monotherapy for alcohol/sedative withdrawal — it does not treat the GABA deficit and lowers the seizure threshold.
Do NOT escalate the antipsychotic dose for akathisia (restlessness mistaken for worsening agitation) — give a benzodiazepine instead.
Do NOT attribute agitation to "psych" until reversible medical causes (glucose, hypoxia, head injury, sepsis) are excluded.
Do NOT use physical restraints as a substitute for chemical sedation — always administer both concurrently.

7 · Refractory agitation

Reconsider the diagnosis — untreated hypoglycemia, hypoxia, head injury, or sepsis? If still refractory to combination therapy:

Ketamine: 4 mg/kg IM or 1-2 mg/kg IV IM preferred, IV if access — Fastest, most reliable. Onset 3-5 min IM. Reduce dose 50% if given after other sedatives. Avoid in elderly and known schizophrenia. Prepare for intubation.

8 · Physical restraints

Type | Use | Caution

Soft limb restraints (4-point) | Standard for acute agitation | Allows IV access and monitoring

Mittens / hand covers | Supplement limb restraints | Use when patient is removing IVs/tubes

Vest / torso restraints | Prevents sitting up | Asphyxiation risk — monitor closely

Hard restraints (leather) | Extreme agitation | Increased injury risk — reserve for refractory cases

Spit mask / hood | Spitting/biting (biosafety) | Must allow adequate ventilation

  • Team of 5 — 1 person per limb + 1 team leader who does not touch the patient
  • Arms first (greatest threat), then legs
  • Secure each limb to the bed frame — never the side rails
  • Patient supine — NEVER prone (asphyxiation risk)
  • Diagonal pattern: right wrist to right upper rail, left wrist to left lower rail
  • Head of bed elevated
  • NEVER hogtie, stack mattresses, place a knee on the chest or back, or leave a restrained patient unmonitored
  • Remove watches, glasses, and other potential weapons from reach
  • Always administer chemical sedation concurrently — restraints are not a substitute

Adults (≥18): 4 hours maximum per order. Remove restraints as soon as the patient is calm enough to be safe — partial release (one limb at a time) to test tolerance.

Every 15 minutes: circulation checks, sensation/skin integrity, respiratory status, offer food/water/toileting. Every 1-2 hours: physician reassessment, attempt partial release. Face-to-face physician evaluation within 1 hour of initiation.

  • Behavior necessitating restraints
  • Less restrictive alternatives attempted (verbal de-escalation, PO meds)
  • Type of restraint applied
  • Time applied
  • Names of all staff involved
  • Neurovascular checks q15 min (documented)
  • Physician face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour
  • Physician reassessment q1-2h (documented)
  • Ongoing need assessment at each check
  • Patient notification of behavior criteria for discontinuation
  • Family notification (if applicable)
  • Time of removal and total duration
  • Post-restraint debriefing with patient and staff
  • Revisions to care plan

9 · Post-sedation monitoring & workup

Respiratory: continuous pulse oximetry, capnography if available, BVM and suction at bedside. Cardiac: continuous telemetry if antipsychotic given; ECG for QTc after haloperidol or droperidol — QTc >500 ms or increase >60 ms → discontinue agent, correct K+/Mg2+. Neurologic: watch for dystonia (treat with diphenhydramine or benztropine) and akathisia (do NOT escalate the antipsychotic — give a benzodiazepine instead).

Diphenhydramine: 50 mg IV or IM — Treats acute dystonia. Alternative: benztropine 1-2 mg IV.

  • Fingerstick glucose (recheck)
  • BMP, CBC, VBG with lactate
  • ECG
  • Urine drug screen (interpret cautiously)
  • CT head if trauma, anticoagulation, or focal deficits
  • CK and renal function if prolonged agitation or restraints (rhabdomyolysis)

10 · Disposition

Intubation/RSI, refractory agitation on continuous sedation, hemodynamic instability, rhabdomyolysis (CK >5000), excited delirium features, or QTc >500 ms with arrhythmia risk.

Ongoing alcohol withdrawal requiring serial benzodiazepine dosing, active medical workup pending, need for continued restraints/reassessment, or unclear etiology.

Only after medical causes are excluded — vitals normal, labs unremarkable, baseline mental status confirmed. Refer for suicidal ideation, psychotic decompensation, or grave disability.

Agitation resolved, at baseline mental status, vitals stable, no medical cause requiring admission, safe disposition. Minimum 1 hour observation after the last sedation dose.

Other pitfalls

Do NOT give antipsychotic monotherapy for alcohol/sedative withdrawal — it does not treat the GABA deficit and lowers the seizure threshold.

Do NOT escalate the antipsychotic dose for akathisia (restlessness mistaken for worsening agitation) — give a benzodiazepine instead.

Do NOT attribute agitation to "psych" until reversible medical causes (glucose, hypoxia, head injury, sepsis) are excluded.

Do NOT use physical restraints as a substitute for chemical sedation — always administer both concurrently.
