VP Shunt Complications — ED Management

Pediatric and adult ED patients with a ventriculoperitoneal (or other CSF) shunt presenting with possible malfunction, infection, or overdrainage. Excludes definitive neurosurgical management (shunt revision, externalization, removal) — this is always performed by neurosurgery. ED shunt tap is reserved for peri-arrest / impending herniation when neurosurgery is unavailable and local policy supports it.

⚠ Normal CT, MRI, shunt series, or ONSD ultrasound does NOT exclude shunt malfunction — if clinical suspicion persists, consult neurosurgery regardless of imaging
⚠ Cushing triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations), pupil dilation, or decerebrate/decorticate posturing = impending herniation — treat as an emergent ICP crisis immediately
⚠ Shunt infection can present as malfunction ALONE, without fever — do not exclude infection just because the patient is afebrile
⚠ Suspected shunt infection: obtain blood cultures BEFORE starting empiric antibiotics; empiric regimen is vancomycin PLUS an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (cefepime, ceftazidime, or meropenem) — antibiotics alone are not curative, hardware source control is usually required
⚠ Impending herniation: HOB 30°, head midline, target MAP >80 mmHg; hypertonic saline is preferred over mannitol; hyperventilation (pCO2 30-35 mmHg) is a BRIDGE ONLY; call neurosurgery STAT
⚠ Shunt tap should ideally be performed by neurosurgery; ED tap is reserved for peri-arrest/impending herniation when neurosurgery is unavailable — do NOT force aspiration if no CSF returns (risk of choroid plexus injury)

Recognition & scope

ED recognition and stabilization of pediatric and adult patients with VP shunt complications: mechanical malfunction, infection/ventriculitis, overdrainage, distal catheter complications, and skin/hardware problems.

  • Mechanical malfunction: proximal/distal obstruction, valve failure, disconnection, fracture, kinking, migration
  • Infection / ventriculitis — especially early after placement or revision, but can occur later
  • Overdrainage: slit ventricles, postural headache, subdural hygroma/hematoma
  • Distal catheter complications: abdominal pseudocyst, peritonitis, bowel perforation, migration, pleural complications
  • Skin/hardware problems: erosion, exposure, CSF leak, tract cellulitis

Pediatric symptoms are often nonspecific: headache, vomiting, lethargy, irritability, seizure, poor feeding, or caregiver concern the child is behaving like a prior malfunction. Infants may show bulging fontanelle, increasing head circumference, poor feeding, sunset eyes, or irritability.

Normal imaging does NOT exclude shunt malfunction
Chronically shunted patients may have small or stiff ventricles and can remain symptomatic with unchanged imaging. If the clinical pattern is concerning, involve neurosurgery even when imaging is unchanged. Normal imaging does NOT exclude shunt malfunction.

Clinical presentation

  • Headache (most common presenting symptom)
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Lethargy, irritability, drowsiness
  • Altered mental status
  • Visual changes (diplopia, blurred vision)
  • Abdominal pain (distal catheter issues)
  • Bulging fontanelle (if open)
  • Increasing head circumference
  • Sunset eyes (upward gaze palsy)
  • Macewen sign ("cracked pot" sound on percussion)
  • Poor feeding, irritability
Red flags for impending herniation
Cushing triad: hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations. Unilateral or bilateral pupil dilation. Decerebrate/decorticate posturing. Seizures. Rapid neurologic decline. Symptoms may be intermittent in partial obstruction — caregiver assessment of "acting like previous malfunction" should be taken seriously.

Exam & imaging strategy

FindingSuggests
Flat or slow refill (>3-5 sec)Proximal obstruction — CSF not draining from ventricles
Firm or difficult to compressDistal obstruction — CSF not draining to peritoneum
Easy to compress, refills quicklyMay be functional but NOT reliable
Shunt pumping has poor sensitivity and specificity
A normal-feeling shunt does NOT exclude malfunction. Do not rely solely on this exam — complete a full neurologic exam and inspect the shunt tract for redness, swelling, or CSF leak.

Stable child: rapid/limited brain MRI without sedation when available in a clinically useful time frame. Unstable child, altered mental status, herniation concern, or MRI delay: non-contrast head CT now. Compare to prior imaging whenever possible. Shunt series (AP/lateral skull, neck/chest, abdomen/pelvis) is best for discontinuity, fracture, kinking, disconnection, migration, and valve setting — low sensitivity alone but a positive result is highly actionable.

Abdominal pain, tenderness, distension, fever, or unexplained malfunction → abdominal ultrasound or CT for pseudocyst, loculated CSF, abscess, or distal catheter complication.

Interpreting imaging

  • Ventricular enlargement compared to baseline
  • New periventricular edema/hypodensity
  • Discontinuity, fracture, or kinking on shunt series
  • Catheter migration (proximal or distal)
  • Distal catheter in unexpected location (bowel, liver, chest)
  • Intermittent obstruction
  • Chronically shunted "stiff" ventricles
  • Early obstruction before ventricular dilation

Additional testing when imaging is normal

ONSD ultrasound: bedside adjunct for raised ICP when the child is unstable or imaging is delayed. A dilated ONSD supports elevated ICP but cannot rule out malfunction. Measure 3 mm posterior to the globe in both eyes.

Abdominal ultrasound: use for abdominal pain, tenderness, distension, fever, or distal catheter concern. Can identify CSF pseudocyst, loculated fluid, abscess, ascites, or distal catheter malposition. IDSA specifically recommends abdominal US or CT for VP shunt infection with abdominal symptoms.

MRI brain: more sensitive for subtle transependymal flow, posterior fossa findings, subdural collections, and radiation-sparing pediatric follow-up — limited by availability, sedation need, and urgency.

Shunt tap measures opening pressure and obtains CSF for infection workup — generally performed by neurosurgery; ED tap is reserved for peri-arrest/impending herniation when neurosurgery is unavailable and local policy supports it. If clinical concern persists despite normal testing, consult neurosurgery.

Infection evaluation & treatment

Shunt infection: 5-12% annual incidence, 90% occur within 6 months of surgery/revision. Most common organism: *Staphylococcus epidermidis*. Other: S. aureus, gram-negative rods, Propionibacterium.

  • Fever (but may be absent)
  • Wound erythema, swelling, drainage
  • Abdominal pain/tenderness (peritonitis from distal catheter)
  • Meningismus
  • Altered mental status with fever
  • Skin breakdown over shunt tract
Malfunction can be caused by infection
Shunt infection may present solely as malfunction without classic infection signs. Suspect infection with recent surgery (<6 months), fever + malfunction symptoms, CSF leak/wound breakdown, abdominal symptoms, or malfunction without a clear mechanical cause.

ICP management

  1. Airway: prepare for emergent intubation if GCS <8 or declining
  2. Breathing: maintain SpO2 >94%, avoid hypoxia
  3. Circulation: target MAP >80 mmHg (CPP = MAP - ICP)
  4. Position: HOB elevated 30 degrees, head midline, loosen cervical collar if present
Hyperventilation is a bridge only
Target pCO2 30-35 mmHg. Short-term only — causes cerebral vasoconstriction; prolonged hyperventilation causes rebound edema.

Normal ICP: 5-15 mmHg. Treatment threshold: ICP 20-22 mmHg sustained >10-15 min. CPP goal: 60-70 mmHg (CPP = MAP - ICP). ICP management is a BRIDGE to neurosurgical shunt revision, not definitive treatment.

Tiered ICP escalation (foundation → escalation → refractory)
  • Tier 1 (foundation): HOB 30°, head midline, adequate sedation, treat fever/seizures/pain, avoid hypotension (MAP >80), normocapnia (pCO2 35-40)
  • Tier 2 (escalation): hyperosmolar therapy (hypertonic saline preferred), mild hyperventilation (pCO2 32-35, bridge only), deeper sedation (propofol)
  • Tier 3 (refractory): aggressive hyperventilation (pCO2 25-30, bridge only), barbiturate coma, decompressive craniectomy, therapeutic hypothermia (select cases)

Shunt tap

  • Diagnose infection (obtain CSF)
  • Measure opening pressure (assess obstruction)
  • Therapeutically reduce ICP in emergencies
  • Evaluate shunt function

Ideally performed by a neurosurgeon. An emergency physician may perform the tap if the patient is critically ill AND neurosurgery is unavailable. Best performed on shunts with an accessible reservoir.

  • Infection overlying the tap site
  • Unknown shunt design/reservoir location
  • Coagulopathy (relative)

Complications: infection introduction (<1% but devastating), choroid plexus occlusion of the catheter tip (slit ventricles), hemorrhage, pneumocephalus.

  1. Position patient supine, head turned so reservoir faces up; do NOT shave hair (increases infection risk)
  2. Prep with antiseptic, allow to dry
  3. Insert 25G butterfly at 45-degree angle into center of reservoir; observe for spontaneous CSF flow
  4. Measure opening pressure with an LP manometer, patient lateral decubitus, manometer at ear level
  5. Withdraw slowly (<1 mL/min); maximum 10 mL/kg total volume
  6. Send CSF for cell count, glucose, protein, gram stain, culture
Pressure (mmHg)Interpretation
8-12Normal (lateral decubitus, ear level)
>15Elevated — suggests obstruction or dysfunction
Very low or no flowProximal obstruction or slit ventricles
Do NOT force aspiration
If no CSF is obtained, the proximal catheter may be obstructed — do not force aspiration (risks choroid plexus injury). After obtaining CSF, compress the reservoir repeatedly: difficult to compress or slow refill suggests distal obstruction.

CSF WBC >10 cells/microL = concerning for infection. CSF WBC >50 cells/microL = strongly suggests infection (rule out HIV, Lyme). Low glucose (<40 mg/dL or <60% serum) = infection. Gram stain positive = infection (low sensitivity). Culture is definitive (may take 10-14 days).

  • Shunt series findings and any shunt tap results
  • Current interventions (hyperosmolar therapy, intubation)

External ventricular drain (EVD)

  • Shunt infection requiring hardware removal
  • Temporizing while awaiting definitive shunt revision
  • ICP monitoring and CSF drainage
  • Complex cases requiring staged management

Height measured from the tragus (external auditory meatus). Typical starting height: 10-15 cmH2O, adjustable based on ICP goals and CSF drainage. Normal CSF output: ~20 mL/hour.

  • Hourly neuro checks and ICP readings
  • Daily CSF sampling for infection monitoring
  • Watch for overdrainage (headache worse sitting up)

Complications: infection/ventriculitis (5-10%), hemorrhage at insertion, overdrainage (collapsed ventricles), catheter occlusion.

Special populations & shunt types

Pediatric considerations, slit ventricle syndrome, programmable valves, VA/VPl shunts
  • Open fontanelle: bulging indicates elevated ICP; suture diastasis on imaging
  • Irritability may be the primary presentation; more rapid decompensation than adults
  • Caregiver history is often the most reliable data point

Slit ventricle syndrome: chronic overdrainage leads to small, non-compliant ventricles. May present with intermittent symptoms; headaches often positional (worse upright). Shunt tap may be difficult (small target). May require valve revision to a higher pressure setting.

Programmable shunts: valve setting can be changed with an external programmer. MRI can reset some programmable valves — check the setting after any MRI.

Ventriculoatrial (VA) shunts drain to the right atrium — risk of pulmonary embolism, endocarditis, nephritis; infection may present as fever with a heart murmur. Ventriculopleural (VPl) shunts drain to the pleural space — risk of hydrothorax and respiratory distress; CXR may show a pleural effusion.

Intubation for elevated ICP

  • GCS <8 or declining mental status
  • Inability to protect airway
  • Respiratory failure
  • Need for emergent surgical intervention

Pre-treatment (optional, controversial): lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg IV blunts the ICP response; fentanyl 1-3 mcg/kg blunts the sympathetic response.

Disposition

  • Signs of herniation or elevated ICP
  • Hemodynamic instability
  • Post-operative monitoring
  • EVD in place
  • Confirmed infection requiring IV antibiotics
  • Declining neurologic status
  • Confirmed malfunction awaiting OR

Only with neurosurgery agreement, reliable follow-up within 24-48 hours, clear return precautions, and a low threshold for return with any worsening.

ICU management

  • Continuous telemetry
  • Arterial line if hemodynamically unstable
  • Frequent neuro checks (q1-2h or per protocol)
  • ICP monitoring if EVD in place
  • HOB elevation 30 degrees
  • Normothermia (treat fever aggressively)
  • Seizure prophylaxis if indicated
  • Continued hyperosmolar therapy as needed
  • EVD drainage per neurosurgery protocol
  • Normocapnia or mild hypocapnia
  • Adequate sedation to prevent coughing
  • Avoid high PEEP if possible (may increase ICP)
  • DVT prophylaxis (mechanical if bleeding concern)
  • Stress ulcer prophylaxis
  • Glycemic control, enteral nutrition preferred
  • NPO status and type-and-screen for surgery prep

Observation protocol

  • Neurologic checks every 2-4 hours
  • Serial vital signs (watch for Cushing response)
  • Repeat imaging if symptoms recur or worsen
  • Pain assessment and treatment
  • Any change in mental status
  • New or worsening headache
  • New vomiting
  • Vital sign changes (bradycardia, hypertension)
  • Seizures

Minimum 24 hours observation if discharged; longer if intermittent symptoms or borderline findings. Before discharge: clear follow-up with neurosurgery in 24-72 hours, written return precautions, caregiver education, low threshold for return.

Transfer to neurosurgical center

  • Secure airway if GCS <8 or declining
  • Establish IV access (two large-bore)
  • Begin hyperosmolar therapy if elevated ICP
  • Stabilize hemodynamics
  • Complete H&P with neuro exam
  • All imaging (CT, shunt series) and prior imaging for comparison
  • Shunt tap results if performed
  • Labs (CBC, BMP, coags)
  • List of interventions and response
  • Direct physician-to-physician handoff, confirm accepting neurosurgeon
  • Continuous monitoring, HOB elevated if possible
  • Airway equipment and additional hyperosmolar therapy available
  • Transport with RN/paramedic capable of RSI

Key takeaways

Bottom line
Maintain high suspicion for shunt malfunction in any patient with a VP shunt presenting with headache, vomiting, or altered mental status. Normal imaging does not exclude obstruction. Consult neurosurgery early. Use hypertonic saline for ICP management. Differentiate malfunction from infection. All patients require admission unless cleared by neurosurgery.

Observation protocol — Neuro checks q2-4h, minimum 24h observation, clear neurosurgery follow-up 24-72h before any discharge.

Transfer to neurosurgical center — Secure airway, begin hyperosmolar therapy if elevated ICP, physician-to-physician handoff, send imaging/labs/intervention details.

Disposition & key takeaways — Maintain high suspicion for malfunction; normal imaging does not exclude it; differentiate infection from malfunction; ICP management bridges to neurosurgery; all suspected malfunction requires consultation.

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for complete pediatric-specific detail, all shunt-subtype considerations, and the full disposition documentation checklist.

Exam & imaging strategy

Finding | Suggests

Flat or slow refill (>3-5 sec) | Proximal obstruction — CSF not draining from ventricles

Firm or difficult to compress | Distal obstruction — CSF not draining to peritoneum

Easy to compress, refills quickly | May be functional but NOT reliable

A normal-feeling shunt does NOT exclude malfunction. Do not rely solely on this exam — complete a full neurologic exam and inspect the shunt tract for redness, swelling, or CSF leak.

Stable child: rapid/limited brain MRI without sedation when available in a clinically useful time frame. Unstable child, altered mental status, herniation concern, or MRI delay: non-contrast head CT now. Compare to prior imaging whenever possible. Shunt series (AP/lateral skull, neck/chest, abdomen/pelvis) is best for discontinuity, fracture, kinking, disconnection, migration, and valve setting — low sensitivity alone but a positive result is highly actionable.

Abdominal pain, tenderness, distension, fever, or unexplained malfunction → abdominal ultrasound or CT for pseudocyst, loculated CSF, abscess, or distal catheter complication.

Interpreting imaging

  • Ventricular enlargement compared to baseline
  • New periventricular edema/hypodensity
  • Discontinuity, fracture, or kinking on shunt series
  • Catheter migration (proximal or distal)
  • Distal catheter in unexpected location (bowel, liver, chest)
  • Intermittent obstruction
  • Chronically shunted "stiff" ventricles
  • Early obstruction before ventricular dilation

Additional testing when imaging is normal

ONSD ultrasound: bedside adjunct for raised ICP when the child is unstable or imaging is delayed. A dilated ONSD supports elevated ICP but cannot rule out malfunction. Measure 3 mm posterior to the globe in both eyes.

Abdominal ultrasound: use for abdominal pain, tenderness, distension, fever, or distal catheter concern. Can identify CSF pseudocyst, loculated fluid, abscess, ascites, or distal catheter malposition. IDSA specifically recommends abdominal US or CT for VP shunt infection with abdominal symptoms.

MRI brain: more sensitive for subtle transependymal flow, posterior fossa findings, subdural collections, and radiation-sparing pediatric follow-up — limited by availability, sedation need, and urgency.

Shunt tap measures opening pressure and obtains CSF for infection workup — generally performed by neurosurgery; ED tap is reserved for peri-arrest/impending herniation when neurosurgery is unavailable and local policy supports it. If clinical concern persists despite normal testing, consult neurosurgery.

Infection evaluation & treatment

Shunt infection: 5-12% annual incidence, 90% occur within 6 months of surgery/revision. Most common organism: *Staphylococcus epidermidis*. Other: S. aureus, gram-negative rods, Propionibacterium.

  • Fever (but may be absent)
  • Wound erythema, swelling, drainage
  • Abdominal pain/tenderness (peritonitis from distal catheter)
  • Meningismus
  • Altered mental status with fever
  • Skin breakdown over shunt tract

Shunt infection may present solely as malfunction without classic infection signs. Suspect infection with recent surgery (<6 months), fever + malfunction symptoms, CSF leak/wound breakdown, abdominal symptoms, or malfunction without a clear mechanical cause.

Parameter | Infection | Malfunction

Fever | Often present | Usually absent

WBC | Elevated | Normal

CRP | Elevated | Normal

CSF glucose | Decreased | Normal

CSF WBC | Elevated | Normal or mildly elevated

CSF cultures have a 90% negative rate when obtained via reservoir puncture, BUT cultures of removed shunts are positive in 59% of cases — half of infected shunts have negative CSF cultures but positive shunt hardware cultures.

Serum CBC with differential, CRP, and blood cultures x2 sets — obtain BEFORE starting empiric antibiotics whenever possible.

Vancomycin: 15 mg/kg IV (typical serious-infection dose) IV — Target trough 15-20 mcg/mL if trough-based monitoring is used; AUC-guided preferred when available. Combine with an antipseudomonal beta-lactam.

Vancomycin (higher initial dose): 20 mg/kg IV (higher initial dose) IV — Use when critically ill or high MRSA concern.

Cefepime: 2 g IV q8h, adjusted for renal function IV — Antipseudomonal beta-lactam paired with vancomycin. Fixed adult dose — no weight-based calculator (pediatric dosing follows local pediatric ID/pharmacy protocol).

Meropenem: 2 g IV q8h, adjusted for renal function IV — Alternative to cefepime/ceftazidime when MDR/ESBL risk or local practice supports it. Fixed adult dose — no weight-based calculator.

Anaphylactic beta-lactam allergy alternative

If anaphylactic beta-lactam allergy and meropenem is contraindicated: vancomycin plus aztreonam or ciprofloxacin per IDSA/local susceptibility.

Biofilm and retained hardware make source control central to cure. Definitive management requires neurosurgery + ID: shunt externalization or removal, EVD or alternate temporary CSF drainage, culture-directed IV antibiotics, and reimplantation after organism-specific treatment interval and CSF sterilization.

ICP management

  • Airway: prepare for emergent intubation if GCS <8 or declining
  • Breathing: maintain SpO2 >94%, avoid hypoxia
  • Circulation: target MAP >80 mmHg (CPP = MAP - ICP)
  • Position: HOB elevated 30 degrees, head midline, loosen cervical collar if present

Target pCO2 30-35 mmHg. Short-term only — causes cerebral vasoconstriction; prolonged hyperventilation causes rebound edema.

Normal ICP: 5-15 mmHg. Treatment threshold: ICP 20-22 mmHg sustained >10-15 min. CPP goal: 60-70 mmHg (CPP = MAP - ICP). ICP management is a BRIDGE to neurosurgical shunt revision, not definitive treatment.

Tiered ICP escalation (foundation → escalation → refractory)

  • Tier 1 (foundation): HOB 30°, head midline, adequate sedation, treat fever/seizures/pain, avoid hypotension (MAP >80), normocapnia (pCO2 35-40)
  • Tier 2 (escalation): hyperosmolar therapy (hypertonic saline preferred), mild hyperventilation (pCO2 32-35, bridge only), deeper sedation (propofol)
  • Tier 3 (refractory): aggressive hyperventilation (pCO2 25-30, bridge only), barbiturate coma, decompressive craniectomy, therapeutic hypothermia (select cases)

Hypertonic Saline 3% (pediatric): 5 mL/kg IV over 10-15 minutes IV (peripheral OK) — Preferred over mannitol. May repeat q4-6h. Target sodium <=155 mEq/L. Can give via peripheral IV.

Hypertonic Saline 3% (adult): 250-500 mL IV over 15-20 minutes IV (peripheral OK) — Preferred over mannitol. May repeat q4-6h. Target sodium <=155 mEq/L.

Hypertonic Saline 23.4%: 30 mL IV over 15-20 minutes IV — central line preferred — For severe/refractory ICP elevation. Requires central line (preferred) or large-bore peripheral.

Mannitol 20%: 0.5 g/kg IV bolus (range 0.25-1 g/kg) IV bolus via in-line filter — Alternative to hypertonic saline. Ensure euvolemia. Monitor osmolarity <320 mOsm. May cause rebound ICP elevation.

Hypertonic saline: target sodium <=155 mEq/L. Mannitol: monitor osmolarity <320 mOsm and osmolar gap (<20 mOsm preferred) before redosing.

Acetazolamide (adjunct only): 25 mg/kg/day (pediatric); 250-500 mg IV/PO BID (adult) IV/PO — Reduces CSF production by 40-50%. NOT for acute emergencies — bridge/adjunct only. Consult text states a 100 mg/kg/day pediatric ceiling. Causes metabolic acidosis.

Sedation for intubated patients: propofol reduces cerebral metabolic rate. Ketamine's older reputation for raising ICP is addressed directly in the intubation section below — it is not absolutely contraindicated for ICP.

Shunt tap

  • Diagnose infection (obtain CSF)
  • Measure opening pressure (assess obstruction)
  • Therapeutically reduce ICP in emergencies
  • Evaluate shunt function

Ideally performed by a neurosurgeon. An emergency physician may perform the tap if the patient is critically ill AND neurosurgery is unavailable. Best performed on shunts with an accessible reservoir.

  • Infection overlying the tap site
  • Unknown shunt design/reservoir location
  • Coagulopathy (relative)

Complications: infection introduction (<1% but devastating), choroid plexus occlusion of the catheter tip (slit ventricles), hemorrhage, pneumocephalus.

  • Position patient supine, head turned so reservoir faces up; do NOT shave hair (increases infection risk)
  • Prep with antiseptic, allow to dry
  • Insert 25G butterfly at 45-degree angle into center of reservoir; observe for spontaneous CSF flow
  • Measure opening pressure with an LP manometer, patient lateral decubitus, manometer at ear level
  • Withdraw slowly (<1 mL/min); maximum 10 mL/kg total volume
  • Send CSF for cell count, glucose, protein, gram stain, culture

Pressure (mmHg) | Interpretation

8-12 | Normal (lateral decubitus, ear level)

>15 | Elevated — suggests obstruction or dysfunction

Very low or no flow | Proximal obstruction or slit ventricles

If no CSF is obtained, the proximal catheter may be obstructed — do not force aspiration (risks choroid plexus injury). After obtaining CSF, compress the reservoir repeatedly: difficult to compress or slow refill suggests distal obstruction.

CSF WBC >10 cells/microL = concerning for infection. CSF WBC >50 cells/microL = strongly suggests infection (rule out HIV, Lyme). Low glucose (<40 mg/dL or <60% serum) = infection. Gram stain positive = infection (low sensitivity). Culture is definitive (may take 10-14 days).

Neurosurgery consultation

  • Patient age, shunt type if known, date of last revision
  • Presenting symptoms and duration
  • Vital signs including any Cushing response
  • GCS, neurologic examination
  • Current imaging findings vs baseline
  • Shunt series findings and any shunt tap results
  • Current interventions (hyperosmolar therapy, intubation)

Scenario | Neurosurgical intervention

Malfunction | Shunt revision (proximal catheter, valve, or distal catheter) or complete replacement; EVD as temporizing measure

Infection | Shunt externalization or removal; EVD placement; staged replacement after CSF sterilization

  • Clinical signs of herniation
  • Rapidly declining neurologic status
  • Refractory elevated ICP despite medical management

Stabilize the patient (airway, ICP management), arrange emergent transfer to a neurosurgical center, and consider emergent shunt tap only if the patient is peri-coding.

External ventricular drain (EVD)

  • Shunt infection requiring hardware removal
  • Temporizing while awaiting definitive shunt revision
  • ICP monitoring and CSF drainage
  • Complex cases requiring staged management

Height measured from the tragus (external auditory meatus). Typical starting height: 10-15 cmH2O, adjustable based on ICP goals and CSF drainage. Normal CSF output: ~20 mL/hour.

  • Hourly neuro checks and ICP readings
  • Daily CSF sampling for infection monitoring
  • Watch for overdrainage (headache worse sitting up)

Complications: infection/ventriculitis (5-10%), hemorrhage at insertion, overdrainage (collapsed ventricles), catheter occlusion.

Special populations & shunt types

Pediatric considerations, slit ventricle syndrome, programmable valves, VA/VPl shunts

  • Open fontanelle: bulging indicates elevated ICP; suture diastasis on imaging
  • Irritability may be the primary presentation; more rapid decompensation than adults
  • Caregiver history is often the most reliable data point

Slit ventricle syndrome: chronic overdrainage leads to small, non-compliant ventricles. May present with intermittent symptoms; headaches often positional (worse upright). Shunt tap may be difficult (small target). May require valve revision to a higher pressure setting.

Programmable shunts: valve setting can be changed with an external programmer. MRI can reset some programmable valves — check the setting after any MRI.

Ventriculoatrial (VA) shunts drain to the right atrium — risk of pulmonary embolism, endocarditis, nephritis; infection may present as fever with a heart murmur. Ventriculopleural (VPl) shunts drain to the pleural space — risk of hydrothorax and respiratory distress; CXR may show a pleural effusion.

Intubation for elevated ICP

  • GCS <8 or declining mental status
  • Inability to protect airway
  • Respiratory failure
  • Need for emergent surgical intervention

Pre-treatment (optional, controversial): lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg IV blunts the ICP response; fentanyl 1-3 mcg/kg blunts the sympathetic response.

Etomidate: 0.3 mg/kg IV push IV — Hemodynamically neutral — does not cause hypotension. Onset 15-45 sec, duration 5-15 min.

Ketamine: 1-2 mg/kg IV push IV — Hemodynamically supportive — preferred in hypotension. Previously avoided but now considered safe for ICP; the head-injury contraindication myth has been debunked. Weight-based calculator below uses the drug-store's 1.5 mg/kg standard RSI anchor within this range.

Propofol: 1-2 mg/kg IV push IV — Reduces ICP but causes hypotension — reduce dose in hemodynamically fragile patients.

Paralytic: rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg (preferred for rapid onset) or succinylcholine 1.5 mg/kg (OK unless concern for hyperkalemia).

  • Verify tube placement
  • Target normocapnia (pCO2 35-40) or mild hypocapnia (30-35)
  • Continue sedation to prevent coughing/straining
  • Elevate HOB 30 degrees

Disposition

  • Signs of herniation or elevated ICP
  • Hemodynamic instability
  • Post-operative monitoring
  • EVD in place
  • Confirmed infection requiring IV antibiotics
  • Declining neurologic status
  • Confirmed malfunction awaiting OR
  • Stable with mild symptoms, normal ICP
  • Observation for possible intermittent malfunction

Only with neurosurgery agreement, reliable follow-up within 24-48 hours, clear return precautions, and a low threshold for return with any worsening.

ICU management

  • Continuous telemetry
  • Arterial line if hemodynamically unstable
  • Frequent neuro checks (q1-2h or per protocol)
  • ICP monitoring if EVD in place
  • HOB elevation 30 degrees
  • Normothermia (treat fever aggressively)
  • Seizure prophylaxis if indicated
  • Continued hyperosmolar therapy as needed
  • EVD drainage per neurosurgery protocol
  • Normocapnia or mild hypocapnia
  • Adequate sedation to prevent coughing
  • Avoid high PEEP if possible (may increase ICP)
  • DVT prophylaxis (mechanical if bleeding concern)
  • Stress ulcer prophylaxis
  • Glycemic control, enteral nutrition preferred
  • NPO status and type-and-screen for surgery prep

Observation protocol

  • Neurologic checks every 2-4 hours
  • Serial vital signs (watch for Cushing response)
  • Repeat imaging if symptoms recur or worsen
  • Pain assessment and treatment
  • Any change in mental status
  • New or worsening headache
  • New vomiting
  • Vital sign changes (bradycardia, hypertension)
  • Seizures

Minimum 24 hours observation if discharged; longer if intermittent symptoms or borderline findings. Before discharge: clear follow-up with neurosurgery in 24-72 hours, written return precautions, caregiver education, low threshold for return.

Transfer to neurosurgical center

  • Secure airway if GCS <8 or declining
  • Establish IV access (two large-bore)
  • Begin hyperosmolar therapy if elevated ICP
  • Stabilize hemodynamics
  • Complete H&P with neuro exam
  • All imaging (CT, shunt series) and prior imaging for comparison
  • Shunt tap results if performed
  • Labs (CBC, BMP, coags)
  • List of interventions and response
  • Direct physician-to-physician handoff, confirm accepting neurosurgeon
  • Continuous monitoring, HOB elevated if possible
  • Airway equipment and additional hyperosmolar therapy available
  • Transport with RN/paramedic capable of RSI

Key takeaways

Maintain high suspicion for shunt malfunction in any patient with a VP shunt presenting with headache, vomiting, or altered mental status. Normal imaging does not exclude obstruction. Consult neurosurgery early. Use hypertonic saline for ICP management. Differentiate malfunction from infection. All patients require admission unless cleared by neurosurgery.
