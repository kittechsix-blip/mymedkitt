Vertigo: Timing & Triggers, Not Symptom Quality

Adults in the ED presenting with dizziness/vertigo. Written for the adult population — pediatric vertigo is not addressed by this pathway. Excludes patients already meeting stroke-code criteria on arrival (go straight to the stroke pathway). Bedside triage tool — not a substitute for neurology consult when uncertain.

⚠ Screen for central features (Deadly D's + red flags) BEFORE HINTS. ANY positive finding skips HINTS and routes straight to stroke workup — HINTS is not needed and must not be used.
⚠ Counterintuitive HIT: an ABNORMAL head-impulse test (catch-up saccade) is reassuring/peripheral; a NORMAL HIT in an AVS patient is WORRISOME for stroke.
⚠ HINTS is only valid in continuous (AVS) vertigo with spontaneous nystagmus — never apply it to BPPV or episodic vertigo, where it gives false central results.
⚠ CT head is inadequate for posterior stroke (7–16% sensitive); MRI-DWI misses ~20% in the first 24–48 h (up to 35% within 6 h for small strokes) — repeat at 48–72 h if clinical suspicion persists.
⚠ New unilateral hearing loss with vertigo = AICA stroke until proven otherwise (HINTS+), NOT labyrinthitis.
⚠ Limit vestibular suppressants to 48–72 h — longer use delays central compensation and prolongs symptoms.

1 · Classify by timing & triggers (TITRATE)

The core move
The word "dizzy" is unreliable — patients change their descriptor when re-asked minutes later.[9] Classify by timing (constant vs episodic) and triggers (spontaneous vs positional) plus exam, NOT by "is it vertigo or lightheadedness."[1]
  • Full vitals including orthostatic BP/HR — orthostatic hypotension and POTS masquerade as vertigo.
  • Fingerstick glucose.
  • Full neuro exam: cranial nerves, cerebellar (finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin), gait.
  • If altered, focal deficit, or truncal ataxia (cannot sit/stand unassisted) → stroke pathway now.

Rule out non-vestibular first
True vertigo = illusory motion (spinning/tilting). Lightheaded / presyncope / "about to pass out" points to orthostatic, cardiac, metabolic, or anemia causes — work those up instead. A "vertigo" that is worse on standing and lasts seconds is orthostatic intolerance, not peripheral vestibular disease.

2 · What to do now

STOP — central feature present
Do NOT perform HINTS. Treat as posterior circulation stroke until proven otherwise: activate stroke team / neurology, establish last-known-well, cardiac monitoring, IV × 2, NPO, NIHSS. Posterior strokes have a ~35% ED miss rate.[4] MRI-DWI, not CT.
AVS → central screen, then HINTS
Constant vertigo >24 h with spontaneous nystagmus. First screen the Deadly D's; if negative, perform HINTS (only valid here). Peripheral pattern = vestibular neuritis; any central finding = stroke. See section 3.
t-EVS → Dix-Hallpike → Epley
Brief (<1 min) positional episodes, asymptomatic between = BPPV (85% posterior canal). Confirm with Dix-Hallpike; treat posterior-canal BPPV with the Epley maneuver. See section 4. Beware: positional ≠ automatically benign — pure vertical nystagmus is a red flag.
s-EVS → migraine criteria vs TIA
Spontaneous episodes lasting minutes–hours, normal between. Apply vestibular migraine criteria[6] (50% have NO headache). In vascular-risk patients, weigh posterior circulation TIA — recurrent episodes can herald large-vessel stroke. See sections 5–6.

3 · The HINTS exam (AVS only)

Valid only when ALL are true
Continuous (AVS) vertigo · spontaneous nystagmus present at rest · no central red flags · symptoms active now. With no spontaneous nystagmus the head-impulse test looks "normal" in healthy people and falsely suggests a central lesion — HINTS is then dangerous.
The counterintuitive rule
Abnormal HIT (catch-up saccade) = PERIPHERAL / reassuring. The peripheral lesion disables the VOR, so the eyes drift and make a corrective saccade. A NORMAL HIT (eyes stay locked) in an AVS patient is CENTRAL / worrisome — the exact opposite of intuition.
StepPeripheral (reassuring)Central (worrisome)
HI — Head ImpulseAbnormal — catch-up saccadeNormal — no saccade
N — NystagmusUnidirectional, horizontalDirection-changing / vertical / torsional
TS — Test of SkewAbsentVertical skew present (most specific)
(+) HearingNormal bilaterallyNew unilateral loss = AICA stroke
100% sens / 96% specHINTS sensitivity for stroke in AVS (Kattah 2009)
Bedside technique detailfixation removal · HIT · skew

Remove visual fixation — peripheral nystagmus suppresses with fixation. Use Frenzel goggles, the ophthalmoscope trick (view one fundus while the other eye is covered), or hold blank white paper ~10 cm from the face.

  1. Patient fixates on your nose; hold the head with both hands.
  2. Rapidly turn the head ~20° (high-velocity, small-amplitude thrust).
  3. Watch the eyes: do they stay locked, or drift and make a catch-up saccade?
  4. Repeat to the other side.
HINTS+ adds bedside hearing. New unilateral hearing loss extends detection to AICA strokes (labyrinthine artery) — this is stroke, NOT labyrinthitis.

4 · BPPV: Dix-Hallpike → Epley

  1. Sit the patient upright, legs extended; turn the head 45° toward the tested side.
  2. Rapidly lay supine with the head extending 20° off the table edge.
  3. Observe for nystagmus 30–45 s.
  4. Posterior-canal BPPV = upbeat-torsional nystagmus, 5–20 s latency, <1 min, fatigable.
Red flag: pure vertical (downbeat) nystagmus, or nystagmus with no latency that does not fatigue → central positional vertigo (cerebellar). Do not treat as BPPV — reassess for a central cause.
  1. Position 1: hold the Dix-Hallpike position 30–60 s (or until nystagmus stops).
  2. Position 2: rotate the head 90° to the opposite side; hold 30–60 s.
  3. Position 3: roll the whole body onto that side, head rotated another 90° to face the floor; hold 30–60 s.
  4. Position 4: sit the patient up. No post-maneuver activity restrictions are needed.
NNT = 2Epley maneuver — NNT for resolution at 24 h (Cochrane 2014)
BPPV discharge
Meclizine 25 mg PO q8h PRN — max 3–5 days (not curative; overuse delays compensation). Antiemetic: Ondansetron 4–8 mg ODT PRN. Avoid benzodiazepines. Recurrence 15–30%/yr — teach return for repeat maneuver. For horizontal canal, use the Gufoni / barbecue roll, NOT Epley.

6 · Imaging & disposition

CT does not rule out posterior stroke
CT sensitivity for acute posterior-fossa ischemia is only 7–16% — useful just to exclude hemorrhage before treatment.[8] MRI-DWI is the test of choice, but it misses ~20% of posterior strokes in the first 24–48 h (up to 35% within 6 h for small strokes).[3] Repeat MRI at 48–72 h if the first is negative and suspicion persists. Do not be reassured by young age — vertebral dissection strikes patients in their 30s–40s.

Vestibular neuritis is supportive: it resolves over 1–2 weeks as central compensation develops. Ondansetron 4 mg IV or 4–8 mg ODT q6-8h PRN is preferred (does not suppress compensation). Meclizine is capped at 3–5 days; Diazepam 2–5 mg PO/IV q8-12h PRN is reserved for severe refractory symptoms with a strict 48–72 hours limit.
