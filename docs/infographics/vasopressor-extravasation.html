Peripheral Vasopressor Extravasation Rescue

Adults in the ED/ICU with suspected or confirmed extravasation of a peripheral norepinephrine, epinephrine, phenylephrine, dopamine, vasopressin, or mixed vasopressor infusion.

⚠ Stop the vasopressor infusion immediately, leave the catheter in place initially, disconnect tubing and aspirate residual drug through the catheter — do NOT flush the line. Notify pharmacy/clinician lead and obtain the antidote kit; assess for ischemia, compartment risk, and hand/foot involvement.
⚠ Phentolamine is the labeled antidote for norepinephrine extravasation and is commonly used for alpha-adrenergic vasopressor injury generally. Vasopressin-mediated injury is NOT alpha-mediated — do not assume phentolamine will reverse isolated vasopressin extravasation.
⚠ Avoid cold compresses for vasopressor extravasation — additional vasoconstriction may worsen ischemia. Use dry warm compresses and elevation instead.
⚠ Ischemia (blanching, coolness, poor capillary refill, severe pain, numbness, hand/foot involvement) is time-sensitive: obtain phentolamine if an alpha-adrenergic agent is involved AND escalate early to pharmacy, toxicology/poison center, and surgery/plastics for hand, foot, large-volume, delayed, or progressive ischemia. Do not delay surgical consultation while waiting to see whether pharmacologic treatment works.
⚠ Necrosis, blistering, tense compartments, progressive ischemia, or a neurovascular deficit requires IMMEDIATE surgery/plastics/hand-surgery consultation in parallel with continued local rescue and antidote — this is never a watch-and-wait presentation.
⚠ Phentolamine 5-10 mg diluted in 10 mL normal saline, infiltrated locally, should be given as soon as possible — DailyMed labeling specifies treatment within 12 hours for norepinephrine extravasation. Hypotension/tachycardia can occur from systemic absorption. Do not delay surgical consultation for progressive ischemia even after phentolamine is given.
⚠ If ischemia persists after initial rescue: confirm the vasopressor has been moved to reliable alternate access, verify antidote reached the entire affected area, involve poison center/toxicology and surgery/plastics/hand surgery early, and do NOT repeatedly infiltrate medications without a clear plan for dose, territory, and monitoring.
⚠ STOP and escalate immediately for: hand, foot, genital/perineal, or large-volume extravasation; blanching/coolness with poor capillary refill; numbness, weakness, severe pain, or tense compartments; blistering, necrosis, or rapidly expanding skin changes; delayed recognition with ongoing ischemia; or need for ongoing high-dose peripheral vasopressors without reliable access. Do not rely on antidote alone for progressive ischemia.

2 · Recognition

Severity is determined by perfusion, neurovascular findings, location, and progression. Concerning findings include pain, blanching, cool skin, swelling, mottling, delayed capillary refill, numbness, paresthesia, tense compartments, blistering, or rapidly expanding discoloration.

▶ What to do now

No ischemia — nonpharmacologic rescue
Stop, leave catheter, aspirate (do not flush), remove catheter after aspiration unless pharmacy/toxicology directs otherwise, elevate, dry warm compresses (avoid cold), mark/photograph/document, establish alternate access and restart the vasopressor.
Ischemia present — time-sensitive
Perform immediate rescue steps, obtain phentolamine if an alpha-adrenergic agent is involved, and escalate early to pharmacy, toxicology/poison center, and surgery/plastics for hand, foot, large-volume, delayed, or progressive ischemia. Reassess every 15-30 minutes initially. Do not delay surgical consultation while waiting to see whether pharmacologic treatment works.
Necrosis / compartment concern — escalate now
Continue local rescue and antidote steps when appropriate; consult surgery/plastics/hand surgery based on location; consider compartment pressure assessment if the exam suggests compartment syndrome; continue systemic resuscitation and alternative vasopressor access; document serial neurovascular exams.
Alpha-adrenergic (or unknown/mixed) — phentolamine protocol
If mixed infusion or uncertain, treat as alpha-adrenergic until clarified with pharmacy/toxicology. Phentolamine 5-10 mg diluted in 10 mL normal saline, infiltrated locally, is the primary pharmacologic rescue.
Vasopressin alone — not alpha-mediated
Phentolamine is not a direct reversal agent for isolated vasopressin extravasation. Perform rescue steps, restart the pressor through reliable alternate access, and consult pharmacy/toxicology early if ischemia is present. If vasopressin was co-infused with norepinephrine/epinephrine/phenylephrine, treat as mixed alpha-adrenergic exposure and consider phentolamine.
Phentolamine unavailable — reported alternatives
Evidence is limited and largely case-based. Terbutaline 1 mg diluted in 10 mL normal saline SQ, or topical nitroglycerin 2% ointment, per pharmacy/toxicology or local protocol. Phentolamine remains preferred when available.
Perfusion improving
Proceed to monitoring and documentation, then disposition.
Persistent ischemia — escalate
Confirm the vasopressor has been moved to reliable alternate access, verify antidote reached the entire affected area, and consult surgery/plastics/hand surgery early. Do not repeatedly infiltrate medications without a clear plan for dose, territory, and monitoring.
Worsening / tissue injury
Blisters, necrosis, increasing pain, neurologic deficit, or tense compartments — this is not a watch-and-wait problem. Escalate to surgery/plastics/hand surgery immediately.
Resolved or clearly improving
Continue observation until stable. Document the improving exam, secure alternate vasopressor access, and provide wound instructions and escalation triggers before handoff.
Persistent abnormal exam
Do not discharge without a plan. Continue serial neurovascular checks, involve pharmacy/toxicology and surgery/plastics/hand surgery based on site, and consider admission/observation for distal extremity involvement or unreliable follow-up.

Hand, wrist, foot, ankle, antecubital fossa with large swelling, central-line attempt sites, or genital/perineal areas — plus any site with a neurologic deficit, tense compartments, or impaired baseline perfusion.
