algorithm

Neurogenic Shock: Exclude Hemorrhage First, Norepinephrine First-Line, MAP 75-95 mmHg × 3-7 Days

Adults with acute traumatic spinal cord injury at or above T6 in the ED/ICU with hypotension and/or bradycardia. Excludes primary hemorrhagic/hypovolemic shock, pediatric SCI, and non-traumatic causes of bradycardia — each needs a separate pathway. Not a substitute for trauma-team hemorrhage workup.

⚠ Neurogenic shock is a DIAGNOSIS OF EXCLUSION — complete E-FAST, pelvic X-ray, CXR, physical exam for long-bone/external bleeding, and labs (Hgb, lactate, base deficit) BEFORE attributing hypotension/bradycardia to neurogenic shock. Bradycardia masks the usual tachycardic warning sign of hemorrhage.
⚠ AVOID phenylephrine monotherapy — pure α₁ agonism triggers baroreceptor-mediated reflex bradycardia and WORSENS the bradycardia already present in neurogenic shock. Norepinephrine (combined α+β) is first-line.
⚠ MAP target per the 2024 AO Spine/AANS/CNS guideline (Kwon et al.): lower limit 75-80 mmHg, upper limit 90-95 mmHg, sustained for 3-7 days — many centers default to MAP ≥85 mmHg in practice. Avoiding hypotension, especially in the first 72 hours, is the priority; even transient dips are linked to worse neurologic recovery.
⚠ Not all hypotension after spinal cord injury is neurogenic — also consider hemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, and medication effect. Neurogenic shock generally requires a lesion at T6 or above.
⚠ Spinal shock ≠ neurogenic shock — spinal shock is a NEUROLOGIC phenomenon (transient areflexia/flaccid paralysis below the lesion); neurogenic shock is a HEMODYNAMIC (circulatory) phenomenon. They can coexist but are different entities and are managed differently.

1 · Recognize & differentiate

Neurogenic shock — classic triad
Loss of sympathetic tone below a spinal lesion at T6 or above produces: hypotension (peripheral vasodilation), bradycardia (unopposed vagal tone), and hypothermia (loss of thermoregulation). 90% of complete injuries above T6 develop neurogenic shock.[1][2]
FeatureNeurogenic shockHypovolemic shock
Heart rate↓ Bradycardia↑ Tachycardia
SkinWarm, dry, pinkCool, pale, clammy
SVR↓ Low↑ High
Capillary refillNormal/briskDelayed

Mechanism: preganglionic sympathetic neurons run T1-L2; cardiac sympathetic innervation is T1-T5. Loss of descending sympathetic tracts → peripheral vasodilation → venous pooling → ↓ venous return/cardiac output, plus unopposed vagal tone → bradycardia. Key: warm extremities + bradycardia = think neurogenic, not hypovolemic.[1][2]

Not all post-SCI hypotension is neurogenic
Also consider hemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, and medication effect — neurogenic shock generally requires a lesion at T6 or above.[1][3]

3 · Fluids, vasopressors & bradycardia

Hemorrhage not yet excluded — do this first
Do NOT start vasopressor therapy for "neurogenic shock" until E-FAST, pelvic X-ray, CXR, exam, and labs have excluded hemorrhage. Bradycardia removes the usual tachycardic warning sign of bleeding.[1][3]
Hemorrhage excluded — cautious fluids only
Patient is vasodilated, not volume-depleted. Small boluses 250-500 mL with reassessment, maximum 1-2 L before starting vasopressors. Avoid hypotonic fluids and aggressive crystalloid (pulmonary edema, cord edema risk). UOP goal >30 mL/hr.[1][4]
Start norepinephrine first-line
Norepinephrine — combined α₁ + β₁ activity treats BOTH hypotension AND bradycardia. Start 0.05-0.1 mcg/kg/min, titrate q5-15min, range up to 2 mcg/kg/min, target MAP ≥ 85 mmHg. Second-line if unavailable/inadequate: Epinephrine (2-10 mcg/min, prominent bradycardia), Dopamine (5-20 mcg/kg/min, higher complication rate), Vasopressin (0.03-0.04 units/min, adjunct only — pure vasoconstrictor, no chronotropic effect).[1][4][5]
AVOID phenylephrine monotherapy
Pure α₁ agonist — no β activity. Raises BP → baroreceptors sense hypertension → reflex ↓HR → worsens the existing bradycardia. Below-T6 injuries with less bradycardia MAY tolerate it only with co-administered atropine (HR 60-100) — norepinephrine is still preferred.[1][4]

Bradycardia (target HR >60 with adequate perfusion): first-line anticholinergics — Atropine 0.4-0.6 mg IV q4h PRN (max 3 mg total, often inadequate in neurogenic shock) or glycopyrrolate 0.1-0.2 mg IV (less CNS penetration). 4 · MAP targets & monitoring

2024 AO Spine/AANS/CNS guideline (Kwon et al.)
MAP lower limit 75-80 mmHg, upper limit 90-95 mmHg, sustained 3-7 days (broadened from the old 2013 narrow 85-90 mmHg × 7-day target because that band was impractical and the supporting evidence is very-low quality — weak recommendation). Many centers default to MAP ≥ 85 mmHg in practice. No formal vasopressor recommendation was made (evidence gap) — norepinephrine remains the common practical first-line.[6]

6 · Spinal shock (neurologic — not circulatory)

Areflexia pattern — this is spinal shock, not neurogenic shock
Transient loss of ALL cord function below the lesion: flaccid paralysis, anesthesia, areflexia (absent DTRs, absent Babinski, absent bulbocavernosus reflex), loss of bladder/bowel/vascular tone. NOT a circulatory shock state. Duration days to weeks (average 4-12 weeks).[2][8]
PhaseTimingCharacteristics
10-24 hoursAreflexia; motor neuron hyperpolarization
21-3 daysInitial reflex return; denervation supersensitivity
34 days - 1 monthDTRs return; Babinski may appear
41-12 monthsHyperreflexia, spasticity; autonomic dysreflexia

Reflex return order: delayed plantar reflex first, then bulbocavernosus reflex (BCR) typically 1-3 days (marks end of spinal shock phase but has no prognostic value for neurologic recovery per 2023 data), then cremasteric, then ankle/knee jerk (weeks).[2][8][9]

Sacral sparing = incomplete injury = better prognosis
After spinal shock resolves, look for preserved perianal sensation, voluntary toe flexion, and rectal tone. Complete injury (no sacral sparing): <5% regain meaningful function below the level. Incomplete injury (sacral sparing present): may recover significant function — central cord syndrome has the best prognosis.[2][8]

7 · Disposition

All patients with neurogenic shock require ICU admission — vasopressor requirement, MAP augmentation protocol, high cervical injury (C2-C4) respiratory monitoring, continuous cardiac monitoring (arrhythmia risk), potential for decompensation. Consult Neurosurgery/Ortho Spine EMERGENTLY, plus PM&R and respiratory therapy for C2-C4 injuries.[1]

  • Continuous arterial line (MAP monitoring) + continuous telemetry.
  • Temperature-sensing Foley, Q1h neuro checks, strict I/Os.
  • MAP goal per 2024 guideline; norepinephrine infusion first-line.
  • Atropine 0.4-0.6 mg IV PRN for HR <60; push-dose epi at bedside.
  • Foley catheter (neurogenic bladder), NG tube (paralytic ileus common).
  • DVT prophylaxis within 72h, bowel regimen, log-roll precautions, turn Q2h.
  • AVOID phenylephrine monotherapy and steroids; avoid excessive fluid resuscitation.
  • Consults: Neurosurgery/Spine STAT, PM&R.

1 | 0-24 hours | Areflexia; motor neuron hyperpolarization

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT skip the hemorrhage workup — neurogenic shock is a diagnosis of exclusion; bradycardia masks the usual tachycardic response to bleeding.

Do NOT fluid-overload — patients are vasodilated, not hypovolemic. Max 1-2 L crystalloid before vasopressors; aggressive fluids risk pulmonary edema and worsened cord edema.

Do NOT use phenylephrine monotherapy — pure alpha causes reflex bradycardia and worsens the existing bradycardia.

Do NOT undertreat MAP — differences of 2-3 mmHg affect neurologic outcomes; target 75-80/90-95 mmHg for 3-7 days with an arterial line.

Do NOT attribute all post-SCI hypotension to neurogenic shock — also consider hemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, and medications; neurogenic shock usually requires a lesion at T6 or above.
