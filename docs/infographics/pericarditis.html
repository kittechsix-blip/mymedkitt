algorithm

Acute Pericarditis: Diagnose, Risk-Stratify, Treat

Adults ≥18 with suspected acute pericarditis in the ED. Excludes pediatrics, pregnancy-specific NSAID/colchicine adjustments, and chronic constrictive pericarditis — each needs a modified pathway.

⚠ Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis — do NOT wait for echo confirmation if unstable. Beck's triad (hypotension, elevated JVP, muffled heart sounds) is present in only ~30%. IV fluids (500-1000 mL) are temporizing only; emergent pericardiocentesis is definitive.
⚠ Purulent (bacterial) pericarditis is a medical emergency — 20-30% mortality even with treatment; requires drainage plus IV antibiotics. Tuberculous pericarditis carries a 30-50% risk of progression to constriction; ADA >40 U/L suggests TB.
⚠ Avoid corticosteroids first-line — they increase recurrence risk 2-4x. Reserve for NSAID/colchicine contraindication or failure; if used, low-dose (0.25-0.5 mg/kg/day, max ~25 mg) with a very slow taper over 3-6 months.
⚠ Colchicine has a narrow therapeutic index — doses above 0.5 mg/kg have been associated with fatal multi-organ toxicity and there is no antidote. Reduce dose 50% for CrCl <50 mL/min or a concurrent CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitor (clarithromycin, ketoconazole, ritonavir).
⚠ Avoid ibuprofen and indomethacin post-MI — they may impair infarct scar formation; use aspirin instead for post-cardiac-injury (Dressler) pericarditis.
⚠ Hold anticoagulation when possible in large effusion or tamponade — it increases the risk of hemorrhagic conversion; if anticoagulation is indicated (mechanical valve, recent VTE), coordinate closely with cardiology.
⚠ Large pericardial effusion (>20 mm) can progress rapidly to tamponade — admit for monitoring and serial echocardiograms even without current hemodynamic compromise.

Diagnosis requires ≥2 of 4 criteria: chest pain (sharp, pleuritic, positional, sitting-forward relief, ~85-90% sensitive), a pericardial friction rub (scratchy, 3-component, best at LLSB, ~30% sensitive), diffuse ECG changes (~60% sensitive), or a new/worsening pericardial effusion (~60% sensitive).

Distinguishing from STEMI
PR depression in lead II is the most sensitive single finding. Spodick sign (downsloping TP segment) is highly specific but low-sensitivity. ST elevation is diffuse (not territorial) and concave ("smiley face"), with no reciprocal depression except aVR.

Labs for all patients: CRP (best for monitoring response and guiding taper), ESR, troponin (elevated in 30-50% = myopericarditis, does not change the diagnosis but tightens activity restriction), BMP (renal function drives colchicine dosing), CBC.

Echo is indicated for all suspected pericarditis. Effusion size: small <10 mm, moderate 10-20 mm, large >20 mm. Tamponade physiology: RV diastolic collapse (early, sensitive), RA systolic collapse (specific), IVC plethora, respirophasic mitral/tricuspid inflow variation.

6 · Recurrent & refractory pericarditis

This section covers management once a patient has already failed or recurred after the initial NSAID+colchicine course — it is reached through follow-up/subspecialty care rather than the ED disposition algorithm above.

Recurrence occurs in 15-30% despite optimal therapy
Recurrent: symptom-free interval ≥4-6 weeks between episodes. Incessant: symptoms persist >4-6 weeks or recur within 6 weeks of stopping therapy. Risk factors: incomplete initial treatment, premature NSAID taper, no colchicine, steroid use, female sex, autoimmune etiology.

First recurrence: same therapy as the initial episode, but colchicine for 6 months (vs 3 months first episode). Confirm CRP normalization before tapering the NSAID; slower NSAID taper over 4-6 weeks.

Corticosteroids — second-line, only if needed
Indicated for NSAID/colchicine contraindication or failure, autoimmune etiology, or pregnancy. Avoid first-line — increases recurrence 2-4x. If used: Prednisone 0.25-0.5 mg/kg/day (max ~25 mg), continue colchicine throughout, and taper very slowly over 3-6 months. A flare during taper means returning to the last effective dose.
