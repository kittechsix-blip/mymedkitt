Hand Infections — ED Evaluation & Management

ED evaluation and management of hand infections: felon, paronychia (acute and chronic), flexor tenosynovitis, fight bite (clenched-fist injury), herpetic whitlow, and deep space infection. Assumes initial ED assessment of location/extent, mechanism (puncture/laceration/bite), time since onset, prior treatment attempts, and immunocompromise status has been completed.

⚠ Flexor tenosynovitis is a surgical emergency — pain with passive extension is the earliest and most sensitive Kanavel sign; high suspicion plus ANY 2 of the 4 Kanavel signs warrants urgent hand surgery consult. ALL flexor tenosynovitis requires admission.
⚠ Deep space infections (thenar, midpalmar, web space): IV antibiotics ALONE will NOT control the abscess — operative drainage is required and all cases are admitted.
⚠ Fight bite (clenched-fist injury): NEVER primary close — heal by secondary intention. Explore the wound through the full range of motion and obtain an X-ray for foreign body, fracture, or subcutaneous air.
⚠ Herpetic whitlow: DO NOT incise — incision risks secondary bacterial infection and viral dissemination.
⚠ Chronic paronychia (>6 weeks) is typically inflammatory, not primarily infectious — oral antibiotics and repeated drainage are NOT recommended.

Initial assessment

Determine the location and extent of infection, the mechanism (puncture, laceration, bite), time since onset, prior treatment attempts, and whether the patient is immunocompromised (diabetes, HIV, chemotherapy).

  1. Location and extent of infection
  2. Mechanism (puncture, laceration, bite?)
  3. Time since onset
  4. Prior treatment attempts
  5. Immunocompromise (diabetes, HIV, chemotherapy)
  • Is there purulence or fluctuance?
  • Is the flexor sheath involved (Kanavel signs)?
  • Is this a human bite (fight bite)?
  • Are there signs of deep space infection?

▶ What's the presentation?

Felon — pulp space abscess
Closed pulp compartment with 15-20 fibrous septa and minimal expansion room — pressure rises quickly, causing throbbing pain out of proportion to exam. Staph aureus most common. Untreated risks osteomyelitis, septic DIP arthritis, and pulp necrosis.
Paronychia — periungual infection
Infection of the lateral or proximal nail fold. Acute (<6 weeks) is bacterial (Staph ~50%, often polymicrobial); chronic (>6 weeks) is inflammatory ± Candida.
Flexor tenosynovitis — surgical emergency
Kanavel's 4 signs (fusiform swelling, finger held in flexion, tenderness along the flexor sheath, pain with passive extension) are 91-97% sensitive when all 4 are present, but only 54% of patients have all 4. Pain with passive extension is the EARLIEST sign. High suspicion + ANY 2 signs = urgent hand surgery consult — tendon ischemia occurs rapidly.
Fight bite — clenched-fist injury
A tooth penetrates the MCP joint when a fist strikes a mouth. Skin retraction when the fist unclenches covers the deep contamination in an airless, anaerobe-friendly space. Up to 75% have bone/joint/tendon involvement. Patients often deny the mechanism and may present 24-48h later with infection.
Herpetic whitlow
HSV-1 or HSV-2. Vesicles (clear, may turn turbid); unlike a felon, the pulp is NOT tense. Pain is out of proportion. DO NOT incise — risks secondary bacterial infection and viral dissemination.
Deep space infection — surgical emergency
Thenar, midpalmar, or web ('collar button') space. Severe pain and swelling, loss of normal hand contours, finger held in flexion, pain with passive motion. IV antibiotics ALONE will NOT control the abscess.

Felon — pulp space abscess

The distal pulp is a closed compartment divided by 15-20 fibrous septa with minimal room to expand, so pressure rises quickly and produces throbbing pain out of proportion to exam. Staph aureus is the most common organism. Untreated felon risks osteomyelitis of the distal phalanx, septic DIP arthritis, and pulp necrosis.

  1. Digital block anesthesia + digital tourniquet (Penrose drain at base)
  2. Volar longitudinal incision over the point of maximal fluctuance (superficial) OR lateral incision 0.5 cm distal to the DIP crease, parallel to the nail plate (deep) — do not cross the DIP flexion crease, do not fishmouth
  3. Blunt dissection to break ALL fibrous septa — required for complete drainage
  4. Pack loosely with iodoform gauze; remove packing in 24-48h
  5. Oral antibiotics × 7-10 days; warm soaks TID starting day 2; hand surgery follow-up 48-72h
Bupivacaine 0.5% (plain)
Digital block, no epinephrine
Local/digital block
Preferred anesthetic for the digital block prior to I&D.
Early felon without abscess
Warm soaks 3-4x daily for 20 minutes, elevation, oral antibiotics x 7-10 days, close follow-up in 24-48h. First-line: Cephalexin 500 mg PO QID or Dicloxacillin 500 mg PO QID. MRSA risk: TMP-SMX DS 1-2 tabs PO BID or Doxycycline 100 mg PO BID. Severe: Clindamycin 300-450 mg PO TID. Return precautions: worsening pain, new fluctuance, fever, red streaking. If no improvement in 24-48h, re-examine for abscess — may need I&D.

Paronychia — periungual infection

Infection of the lateral or proximal nail fold. Acute paronychia (<6 weeks) is bacterial — Staph in about half of cases, often polymicrobial. Chronic paronychia (>6 weeks) is inflammatory ± Candida. Assess location (lateral fold vs eponychium vs subungual), fluctuance, and extent.

  • Needle elevation (superficial): 21-23g needle parallel to the nail, lift the lateral fold, allow pus to drain — minimal tissue trauma
  • Incision (more extensive): 11-blade at the point of fluctuance, parallel to the nail fold; lift the eponychial fold off the nail if needed
  • Partial nail removal (subungual extension): remove the lateral 1/4 of the nail if pus tracks under it — do NOT remove the entire nail unless necessary
  • Post-drainage: loose mesh gauze, remove in 24-48h, warm soaks TID thereafter. Antibiotics usually NOT needed after drainage unless extensive cellulitis
Paronychia — conservative management
Warm water soaks 3-4x daily for 15-20 min, elevation, keep clean and dry between soaks. Antibiotics are often NOT needed for early, localized paronychia — consider if extensive cellulitis: Cephalexin 500 mg PO QID, or TMP-SMX DS PO BID if MRSA concern. Recheck in 48-72h; if an abscess develops, drain it.
Chronic paronychia (>6 weeks)
Typically inflammatory rather than primarily infectious: repeated wet exposure, Candida colonization (secondary), contact dermatitis, eczema/psoriasis. Management: avoid wet work (cotton-lined gloves), topical steroid (triamcinolone 0.1% BID x 3 weeks), keep dry, topical antifungal if Candida suspected (nystatin, clotrimazole). Oral antibiotics and repeated drainage are NOT recommended — repeated drainage worsens inflammation. Dermatology referral if refractory; hand surgery only after >6 months of failed medical therapy.

Fight bite — clenched-fist injury

A tooth penetrates the MCP joint when a fist strikes a mouth, inoculating oral flora (Eikenella, anaerobes) plus skin flora. Skin retraction when the fist unclenches covers the deep contamination, and the airless space favors anaerobic growth. Up to 75% have bone/joint/tendon involvement. Patients often deny the mechanism and may present 24-48h later with infection. Examine through the FULL range of motion — the wound may track deeper in extension — and assess for joint capsule violation; crepitus suggests a gas-forming organism.

  1. Explore the wound through the full range of motion
  2. Irrigate copiously with saline
  3. Debride nonviable tissue
  4. NEVER primary close — heal by secondary intention
  5. Loose packing if deep
  6. X-ray for fracture (Bennett, boxer's), foreign body (tooth fragment), or subcutaneous air

PO
First choice; duration 7-10 days.

PCN allergy: Moxifloxacin 400 mg PO daily + Metronidazole 500 mg PO TID. MRSA risk: add TMP-SMX or doxycycline.

Admission criteria
Joint capsule violation, tendon involvement, fracture with contamination, purulence or established infection, failed outpatient treatment, unreliable patient, or immunocompromised. IV: Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV q6h or Cefoxitin 2 g IV q6h; add Vancomycin if MRSA concern. Hand surgery consult — likely operative washout. Splint in position of function, elevate, update tetanus. Operative indications: confirmed joint violation, deep tissue necrosis, retained foreign body, abscess, septic arthritis/osteomyelitis.
Outpatient criteria — ALL must be met
Early presentation (<24h), no purulence, no joint/tendon/bone involvement, no systemic signs, reliable follow-up, immunocompetent. Leave the wound OPEN (no closure), daily dressing changes, warm soaks TID starting day 2, keep elevated. Hand surgery or ED recheck in 24-48h. Return IMMEDIATELY for fever, increased swelling, red streaking, or increased pain. Update tetanus if needed.

Herpetic whitlow

Differentiate from felon: vesicles (initially clear, may turn turbid), pulp NOT tense, pain out of proportion — often healthcare workers or autoinoculation. Diagnosis is clinical; a Tzanck smear shows multinucleated giant cells, or use viral culture/PCR if uncertain. Recurrence is 30-50% — consider suppressive therapy.

DO NOT INCISE
Incision risks secondary bacterial infection and viral dissemination. Antibiotics only if secondary bacterial superinfection develops.

If presenting <48h from onset: Valacyclovir 1 g PO TID x 7 days or Acyclovir 400 mg PO 5x/day. Self-limited — resolves in 2-4 weeks. Keep covered to prevent spread.

Outpatient disposition

Discharge — outpatient management
Criteria: no deep space involvement, no flexor tenosynovitis, no systemic toxicity, reliable follow-up available, able to comply with elevation/wound care. Complete the full antibiotic course; NSAIDs for pain (avoid if diabetic/renal disease); warm soaks 3-4x daily for 15-20 min; keep elevated; daily dressing changes if packed. Follow-up: primary care or hand surgery 24-48h post-drainage, or 5-7 days for cellulitis without drainage. Return to ED for worsening pain/swelling, fever >101°F, red streaking up the arm, new drainage or pus, or a numb/white finger.

Deep space | Pip-tazo 4.5g q6h | Vancomycin + pip-tazo

Fight bite | Amp-sulbactam 3g q6h | Vancomycin + ceftriaxone

Severe cellulitis | Cefazolin 2g q8h | Vancomycin + ceftriaxone

  • Prior MRSA infection
  • Recent hospitalization
  • IV drug use
  • Close contact with MRSA
  • Failure of first-line antibiotics

Outpatient disposition

Criteria: no deep space involvement, no flexor tenosynovitis, no systemic toxicity, reliable follow-up available, able to comply with elevation/wound care. Complete the full antibiotic course; NSAIDs for pain (avoid if diabetic/renal disease); warm soaks 3-4x daily for 15-20 min; keep elevated; daily dressing changes if packed. Follow-up: primary care or hand surgery 24-48h post-drainage, or 5-7 days for cellulitis without drainage. Return to ED for worsening pain/swelling, fever >101°F, red streaking up the arm, new drainage or pus, or a numb/white finger.
