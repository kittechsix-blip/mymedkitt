ED evaluation and repair of fingertip and nail bed injuries — subungual hematoma, nail bed laceration, nail avulsion, and complex/amputation injuries — including associated distal phalanx fracture and pediatric Seymour fracture recognition. Anatomy overview

Nail plate: hard keratinized structure; grows ~3mm/month (fingernails); complete regrowth 4–6 months (fingers). Nail bed: germinal matrix (proximal 1/3) produces 90% of the nail plate; sterile matrix (distal 2/3) adds to the undersurface and gives adherence. Eponychium (cuticle), paronychium (lateral folds), hyponychium (beneath the free edge), and the nail fold (proximal skin overlying germinal matrix) round out the anatomy.

Germinal matrix injuries (proximal): nail growth abnormalities — split, ridged, or absent nail; require meticulous repair under magnification; higher risk of permanent deformity. Sterile matrix injuries (distal): non-adherence, nail lifts/catches; generally more forgiving. Key relationship: the nail bed sits on the periosteum of the distal phalanx, so a fracture beneath a nail bed laceration is an OPEN fracture — 50% of nail bed injuries have an associated distal phalanx fracture. Paired digital arteries give redundant blood supply, so a digital block does not compromise healing.

Assessment & imaging

History: mechanism, time of injury, hand dominance, occupation (higher cosmetic expectations), tetanus status. Exam: vascular status (capillary refill, color, temperature), sensory (two-point discrimination, normal <5mm), motor (FDP/FDS function), extensor tendon at the DIP (mallet finger), nail plate integrity, hematoma size, associated skin/fracture injury. Cool/pale finger with poor cap refill or absent sensation → consider hand surgery consultation for neurovascular compromise.

X-ray (AP, lateral, oblique) for most nail bed injuries — evaluate for distal phalanx fracture (~50%), tuft fracture, transverse shaft fracture, DIP joint involvement, or foreign body. Nail bed laceration + fracture = OPEN fracture (antibiotics indicated; unstable fractures may need pinning). Get an X-ray even if the nail appears intact when the mechanism is a significant crush.

Seymour fracture (pediatric)
Physeal (Salter-Harris I/II) fracture of the distal phalanx — the nail plate is often avulsed from the eponychial fold and lies superficial, mimicking mallet finger. This is an open injury requiring irrigation, reduction, and antibiotics, and usually needs OR for formal I&D.

Subungual hematoma pathway

Subungual hematoma is blood trapped between the nail plate and nail bed. Assess hematoma size, nail plate integrity, and check for an associated fracture on X-ray. Traditional teaching graded management by size; modern evidence (2024) shows trephination alone is sufficient for MOST subungual hematomas, even >50% — nail removal is rarely needed if the nail plate is intact with adherent margins.

  • Electrocautery (preferred): touch battery-powered cautery to nail surface, stop when blood wells up — painless, no digital block needed
  • 18-gauge needle: rotate between fingers with gentle pressure to drill through the nail plate
  • Heated paperclip (historical): heat until red-hot, touch to nail surface; multiple applications may be needed, less controlled
Do NOT remove an intact, adherent nail
Remove the nail plate only if it is avulsed/significantly disrupted, edges are non-adherent, decompression is inadequate, or there is nail fold disruption. Prospective evidence (Roser & Gellman) shows trephination alone — even for hematomas >50% — had equivalent cosmetic outcomes to nail removal and repair. The nail plate acts as a biological splint; unnecessary removal may cause more harm.

Wound care: clean with saline, antibiotic ointment, non-adherent dressing, protective splint if fracture present. Meds: tetanus if indicated, antibiotics if fracture present, NSAIDs/acetaminophen for pain. Follow-up: wound check 48–72h, warm-water soaks daily. Old nail grows out and falls off over 2–4 months.

  • Increasing pain, redness, or swelling
  • Fever
  • Pus or drainage

Nail bed laceration & repair

Indications: visible nail bed laceration requiring repair, significantly disrupted/avulsed nail plate, nail fold injury, or subungual hematoma with non-adherent margins. Technique: digital block (wait 5–10 min, test the block), elevate the nail from distal to proximal with a hemostat/Freer elevator, release from the eponychium with a spreading motion, remove and SAVE the nail plate for splinting.

Goals: anatomic alignment of nail bed edges, prevent scarring/deformity, preserve germinal matrix function, splint the nail fold. Meticulous hemostasis (digital tourniquet), magnification (loupes/microscope), fine absorbable sutures (6-0/7-0 chromic/Vicryl), minimal tissue handling. Simple linear lacerations get primary edge-to-edge repair; stellate lacerations need fragment realignment; avulsion with tissue loss may need grafting. The nail plate is the best splint — always replace it or a substitute.

  1. Irrigate thoroughly (saline, remove debris)
  2. Identify and align nail bed edges anatomically — germinal matrix alignment especially important
  3. Place interrupted sutures: simple interrupted, minimal tension, bites just through nail bed avoiding periosteum, 3-4 sutures spaced 2-3mm apart
  4. Check alignment: smooth surface, no step-off at wound edges
  5. Splint the nail fold: replace the original nail plate (clean, trim) or use a substitute (foil suture packet, silicone sheet), secure with a mattress suture through the eponychium
Nail fold must be stented
Without a stent, the eponychium adheres to the nail bed (synechiae), the new nail cannot grow out properly, and a split or absent nail results. Options in order of preference: original nail plate (cleaned, trimmed, secured with a mattress suture — holes can be drilled for drainage), foil suture packet, silicone sheet, or Xeroform gauze. The stent stays in place until the new nail grows out and pushes it off (4–6 weeks).

Nail bed laceration + phalanx fracture = OPEN fracture. Tuft fractures (most common) are usually stable — the nail bed repair and replaced nail plate provide splinting; add a protective aluminum splint and antibiotics for the open fracture. Transverse shaft fractures: assess stability after bed repair; unstable patterns may need K-wire fixation and hand surgery consultation. Seymour fracture (pediatric): Salter-Harris I/II with the nail plate lying superficial to the eponychium — requires nail removal, irrigation, reduction, nail replacement, and usually OR for formal I&D; high infection risk if not properly treated.

Avulsion & complex/amputation injuries

Partial avulsion: remove the entire nail plate for bed inspection, repair any laceration, replace nail as a stent. Complete avulsion: inspect the bed thoroughly, repair lacerations; if the original nail is available, clean and replace it as a stent, otherwise use a substitute. Avulsion from the proximal nail fold (common in pediatric Seymour fractures): the nail plate lies ON TOP of the eponychium and must be reduced back under the nail fold. Germinal matrix avulsion may require microsurgical replantation or matrix grafting — consult hand surgery if significant matrix loss. Document exactly what is avulsed and what remains attached.

Amputation zones: Zone 1 (tip only) often heals by secondary intention; Zone 2 (through nail bed) needs revision amputation vs grafting (composite grafts possible in children <2 years). Initial ED management: irrigate, control bleeding, moist sterile dressing, splint for protection, tetanus, antibiotics, urgent hand surgery consultation. Preserve amputated parts: wrap in saline-moistened gauze, place in a plastic bag on ice (never directly on ice), document ischemia time.

  • Significant tissue loss (>50% nail bed)
  • Complete germinal matrix loss
  • Amputation through nail bed
  • Unstable fractures requiring fixation
  • Tendon involvement (FDP/extensor)
  • Vascular compromise
  • Multiple digit involvement
Refer hand surgery
Any of the criteria above warrant hand surgery referral. Most nail bed repairs can be done within 12–24 hours; true emergencies are vascular compromise or heavily contaminated wounds.

Disposition

Determine disposition based on injury complexity: simple repair with a stable fracture (or none) can discharge home; complex injury, tissue loss, unstable fracture, or amputation needs hand surgery consultation.

Wound care: dressing clean/dry 48h, then daily warm-water soaks + antibiotic ointment; leave the nail stent until it falls off naturally. Splint: aluminum splint 2–3 weeks, may remove for gentle ROM after 1 week. Meds: complete antibiotic course if open fracture, NSAIDs/acetaminophen PRN, tetanus if indicated. Activity: elevate hand, no heavy gripping x2–3 weeks. Follow-up: primary care or hand surgery in 7–10 days; final cosmetic result visible at 6 months.

  • Increasing pain, redness, or swelling
  • Purulent drainage
  • Fever
  • Numbness or color change in the fingertip

While awaiting consultation: irrigate, moist sterile dressing, splint for protection, pain control, NPO if OR anticipated, antibiotics if open fracture, tetanus prophylaxis. Provide to hand surgery: mechanism and time of injury, digit(s) involved, dominant hand, neurovascular status, fracture pattern, tissue loss description, what was done in the ED, patient occupation/functional needs.
