Non-Accidental Trauma (NAT) Screening — Pediatric ED

Children in the ED with bruising, fractures, or head trauma concerning for non-accidental trauma (physical abuse). TEN-4-FACESp bruising rule validated for children <4 years with bruising; skeletal survey mandatory for all children <2 years with suspected abuse; head CT mandatory for all children <1 year with suspected abuse.

⚠ Head CT mandatory for all children <1 year with suspected abuse

⚠ Do NOT use PECARN rules — validated for accidental trauma only, NOT applicable when abuse suspected
⚠ Mandatory CPS report for all suspected abuse — suspicion sufficient, diagnosis NOT required
⚠ Negative labs do NOT exclude abuse

Recognition & red flags

25% of abused children have prior unrecognized (sentinel) injuries. Bruising in non-mobile infants is rare (<0.6% accidental). 80% of sentinel injuries are bruises; 11% are intraoral.

  • Vague, changing, or inconsistent story
  • History inconsistent with developmental stage
  • Delay in seeking care without explanation
  • History doesn't match injury severity/pattern
High-risk presentations
Any bruising in infant <6 months. Multiple injuries in various stages of healing. Injuries in unusual locations (torso, ears, neck). Patterned injuries (loop marks, hand prints, bite marks).

TEN-4-FACESp bruising rule

First validated clinical decision rule for abuse (2021) — sensitivity 96%, specificity 87%. Applicable to children <4 years with bruising.

  • T — Torso (chest, abdomen, back, buttocks, genitalia)
  • E — Ear (pinna or behind ear)
  • N — Neck (front or sides)
  • 4 — Any bruise in infant ≤4.99 months
  • F — Frenulum tear (labial or lingual)
  • A — Angle of jaw
  • C — Cheek (fleshy part)
  • E — Eyelid or periorbital
  • S — Subconjunctival hemorrhage
  • p — Patterned bruising (hand, loop, object shape)
Interpretation
ANY positive finding = increased abuse risk. Torso, Ear, Neck alone identified 81% of abuse cases. A single TEN-4-FACESp bruise warrants full evaluation.
Important limitation
TEN-4-FACESp does NOT distinguish bleeding disorders from abuse — always screen for coagulopathy. Rule only applies to children WITH bruising.
If TEN-4-FACESp POSITIVE
Initiate comprehensive NAT workup. Social work consultation. Consider skeletal survey (all children <2 years). Labs for bleeding disorders. Mandatory CPS report.
If TEN-4-FACESp negative but still concerned
Trust clinical judgment. Document concerns and reasoning. Consider workup if other red flags present. Low threshold in non-mobile infants.

Low suspicion — documentation

  • Complete physical exam findings
  • Location, size, color of any injuries
  • Developmental stage of child
  • Caregiver explanation for injuries
  • Consistency of history with findings
  • Why NAT workup was NOT pursued
Consider accidental trauma if
Developmentally appropriate injury. Consistent, plausible history. Injury location typical for accidental trauma. No pattern or concerning features. First-time presentation.
  • Safety assessment
  • Follow-up arranged
  • Clear discharge instructions

Fracture patterns in abuse

Infants <18 months: 80% of abuse-related fractures occur in this age group; accidental fractures are uncommon. 100% of humeral fractures in infants <18 months are abusive.

Fracture typeAbuse probability
Rib fractures (no major trauma)96%
Classic metaphyseal lesionsVery high
Scapular / spinous processVery high
Multiple fractures, different healing stagesVery high
Concerning patterns
Femoral shaft fracture in a non-ambulatory child. Multiple fractures: 70% abuse if <36 months. Long bone fractures without explanation.

Less specific (but still concerning): linear skull fracture, clavicle fracture (unless birth-related), distal extremity fractures.

Skeletal survey

  • All children <2 years with suspected abuse
  • Any child with skull fracture/SDH without clear cause
  • Metaphyseal lesions, rib, spine, or scapular fractures
  • Abusive head trauma findings
Mandatory <2 years
Skeletal survey is mandatory for this age group when abuse is suspected.
  • AP and lateral skull
  • AP chest (oblique views for ribs)
  • AP pelvis
  • Lateral spine
  • AP humeri, forearms, femurs, tibias
  • PA hands and feet
Follow-up imaging
If initial survey negative but high suspicion: repeat at 10-14 days — some fractures are only visible during healing. Consider bone scan for equivocal survey findings or high clinical suspicion with a normal survey.

Abusive head trauma (AHT)

Poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy. Seizures, apnea, altered mental status. Bulging fontanelle, increasing head circumference. Retinal hemorrhages. Subdural hematomas (especially interhemispheric).

Imaging findings highly associated with AHT: multiple SDH locations (interhemispheric + convexity + posterior fossa), hypoxic-ischemic injury on MRI, skull fractures (especially complex or multiple).

Head CT — mandatory
Mandatory for all suspected abuse in children <1 year, and for any child with external head trauma plus an abnormal neuro exam.

MRI brain required if CT abnormal — better for shear injury, ischemia, lesion timing. Include cervical spine (75% have unsuspected spinal injuries).

Ophthalmology consult
Dilated fundoscopic exam. Retinal hemorrhages strongly associated with AHT. Grades 3A/3B highly specific for abuse.

AHT imaging protocol

  • Non-contrast, axial images
  • Identifies acute blood, fractures, mass effect
  • Fast, no sedation needed
  • T1, T2, FLAIR
  • Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with ADC
  • Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI)
  • DWI important for hypoxic-ischemic injury (prognostic)
MRI c-spine
Routine with AHT evaluation — unsuspected injuries in 75% of AHT cases. Include whole spine if abnormal neuro exam.
Key imaging points
Do NOT attempt to "date" subdural hematomas. Multiple locations of SDH are significant. Brain parenchymal injury is a major cause of morbidity/mortality.

Retinal exam: ophthalmology within 24-48 hours. Document retinal hemorrhage location, extent, character.

Comprehensive NAT workup

When to initiate full workup
Multiple concerning findings. Single highly suspicious finding (e.g., classic metaphyseal lesion). History significantly inconsistent with injury. Pattern suggesting repeated trauma.
  1. Complete physical exam — head to toe, including oral mucosa, genitalia
  2. Skeletal survey — all children <2 years
  3. Head CT — all children <1 year
  4. Laboratory studies — bleeding disorders, metabolic bone disease
  5. Ophthalmology consult — retinal exam for AHT
  6. Social work involvement
  7. Child abuse pediatrician consult (if available)
  8. Mandatory CPS report
  • Interview caregivers separately
  • Document direct quotations
  • Note demeanor, affect, inconsistencies
  • Do NOT conduct an investigative interview (CPS role)

Imaging summary

StudyIndication
Skeletal surveyAll <2 years with suspected abuse; repeat at 10-14 days if negative but high suspicion
Head CTAll <1 year with suspected abuse; >1 year with head trauma or neuro signs
MRI brainAbnormal CT findings; high suspicion for AHT; persistent neurologic symptoms
MRI spineAccompanies brain MRI for AHT; abnormal neurologic exam
3D CTComplex skull fractures (better characterization)
Do NOT use PECARN rules
PECARN is validated for accidental trauma only — NOT applicable when abuse is suspected.

Laboratory workup

CategoryStudies
Coagulation (bruising/bleeding)CBC with platelets, PT, aPTT, INR, Factor VIII and IX, fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor
Intracranial hemorrhage add-onD-dimer; mixing studies if aPTT prolonged
Metabolic bone disease (fractures)Calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, intact PTH; consider genetics for osteogenesis imperfecta

Order for all cases with unexplained bruising, all cases with intracranial hemorrhage, and all cases with unexplained fractures.

Note
Coagulation values may be falsely elevated acutely. Pediatric hematology consultation recommended. Negative labs do NOT exclude abuse.

Documentation requirements

  • Complete head-to-toe exam (include oral, genital)
  • Body diagram or photodocumentation
  • Photos: measurement device, color scale, location ID
Photo requirements (medical evaluation)
Permission NOT required. Include ruler/measurement. Gray scale for color reference. An identifying photo of the child is helpful.
  • Interview caregivers SEPARATELY
  • Document who was present
  • Use DIRECT QUOTATIONS
  • Note inconsistencies between accounts
  • Document caregiver demeanor/affect
  • Record developmental stage of child

Critical documentation: mechanism described vs. injury observed, consistency of explanation, presence of injuries in various healing stages, prior visits for similar concerns.

Mandatory reporting

Legal requirement
ALL 50 states + DC require reporting of suspected abuse. Suspicion sufficient — diagnosis NOT required. "Reasonable cause to believe" standard.
  1. Contact state CPS hotline immediately
  2. File written report (usually within 24-48 hours)
  3. Document report in medical record

Reporter identity confidential (except to law enforcement). Good faith reports protected from liability. Reporting to a supervisor does NOT relieve individual duty.

Many states: physician can retain custody until CPS responds. The ED is a protected/sanctuary space for the patient.

Disposition

ADMIT
All children <1 year with suspected abuse. Serious injuries requiring workup. Unsafe discharge environment. Neurologic injuries, fractures, ICH. CPS involvement pending.
Do NOT discharge
Without CPS involvement/safety plan. If unsafe home environment suspected. If siblings are at risk (evaluate them too).
  • Child abuse pediatrician (if available)
  • Social work (ALWAYS)
  • Ophthalmology (if AHT suspected)
  • Orthopedics (if fractures)
  • Neurosurgery (if ICH)
  • Pediatric hematology (for labs interpretation)
  • Repeat skeletal survey at 10-14 days (if <24 months)
  • Outpatient child abuse pediatrician
  • Ensure CPS case opened
  • Document all plans in chart

Team communication: professional, non-accusatory with family. Transparent about concerns and reporting obligation. Do NOT conduct extended investigative interviews.

Differential diagnosis — abuse mimics (reference)

Easy bruising differential: hemophilia (3-12% develop symptomatic ICH), von Willebrand disease, ITP, leukemia, Henoch-Schönlein purpura, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Mongolian spots (congenital dermal melanosis).

Bone fragility differential: osteogenesis imperfecta, rickets (nutritional, hypophosphatemic), metabolic bone disease of infancy (MBDI), osteopenia.

AHT differential: accidental head trauma, coagulopathies, glutaric aciduria type 1, cerebral sinovenous thrombosis, AVM, birth trauma, bacterial meningitis.

Critical balance
Misdiagnosis → wrongful family separation. Missed diagnosis → continued abuse, disability, death. A thorough workup protects the child AND the family.
