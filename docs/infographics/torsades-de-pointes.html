Torsades de Pointes: Magnesium, Not Amiodarone

Adults in the ED with polymorphic VT on a prolonged-QT background (Torsades de Pointes). Excludes polymorphic VT with a NORMAL QT (that is ischemic — treat with amiodarone/revascularization, the opposite of this pathway) and stable non-arrhythmic QT prolongation without TdP.

⚠ Do NOT give amiodarone, procainamide, sotalol, or any Class IA/III antiarrhythmic. They prolong QT and WILL worsen TdP — this is the most common lethal error in TdP management.
⚠ Isoproterenol is CONTRAINDICATED in congenital LQTS — it paradoxically prolongs QT and increases TdP risk. In congenital LQTS, beta-blockers (nadolol, propranolol) are FIRST-LINE instead — the opposite approach from acquired TdP.
⚠ Give magnesium 4 g IV to EVERY TdP patient regardless of serum Mg level — serum Mg does not reflect intracellular stores, and repleting it suppresses the early afterdepolarizations that drive TdP.
⚠ Pulseless TdP → unsynchronized defibrillation, magnesium 4 g IV push, and standard ACLS otherwise. Do NOT give amiodarone even during arrest.
⚠ Correct hypomagnesemia BEFORE potassium repletion — hypokalemia stays refractory to correction until magnesium is repleted.

1 · Recognize TdP

What it is
Torsades de Pointes is polymorphic ventricular tachycardia on a background of a prolonged QT interval — QRS amplitude waxes and wanes, "twisting" around the isoelectric line, typically 150–300 bpm and often self-terminating. Classically initiated by a short-long-short sequence.[1]
The number that matters
QTc >500 ms carries a 2–3× increased TdP risk. Measure it on a clean 12-lead the moment the patient is stable.[1][2][3]

Critical distinction: TdP (prolonged QT) requires the opposite treatment from polymorphic VT with a *normal* QT. Normal-QT polymorphic VT is ischemic — treat with amiodarone and revascularization. In TdP, amiodarone is harmful.[2]

2 · What to do right now

Set the toggles above to match your patient. Precedence: pulseless → unstable → stable; a congenital overlay layers on top when relevant.

Pulseless TdP → DEFIBRILLATE
Unsynchronized defibrillation at standard ACLS energy. Give Magnesium Sulfate 4 g IV push (rapid is acceptable in arrest). Run standard ACLS (CPR, epinephrine) and repeat defibrillation as needed. Do NOT give amiodarone. After ROSC → magnesium protocol.[1]
Unstable TdP → SYNCHRONIZED CARDIOVERSION
Synchronized cardioversion at standard energy. Simultaneously give Magnesium Sulfate 4 g IV over 20 min (faster if deteriorating), stop all QT-prolonging drugs, draw STAT K⁺/Mg/Ca/iCa. Do NOT give amiodarone or procainamide.[1]
Stable TdP → MAGNESIUM + WORK-UP
Start Magnesium Sulfate 4 g IV over 20 min immediately, regardless of serum Mg level. Stop all QT-prolonging meds, draw STAT electrolytes (K⁺, Mg, Ca, iCa, phosphate), place continuous monitoring, and get a 12-lead for baseline QTc. Then determine acquired vs congenital.[1]
Congenital LQTS overlay — reverse two things
If congenital LQTS is suspected: beta-blocker (esmolol or propranolol) is first-line, and isoproterenol is CONTRAINDICATED (it paradoxically prolongs QT here). Magnesium dosing is unchanged. Avoid ALL sympathomimetics.[7]

3 · Magnesium — the cornerstone

Give it to everyone
Magnesium is first-line for all TdP regardless of serum level — serum Mg does not reflect intracellular stores, and repleting it suppresses the early afterdepolarizations (EADs) that trigger TdP. Also covers the drug washout period (e.g., dofetilide t½ = 10h).[1][8]

8 · Disposition & risk factors

Disposition | Criteria | Key actions

ICU — resolved | Single episode, now sinus, cause identified | Telemetry 48–72h, continue Mg infusion (24h total), cardiology consult, full medication reconciliation

ICU — recurrent | Multiple episodes despite Mg, needed pacing/isoproterenol | Overdrive pacing until QT normalizes, EP consult, serial QTc q4-6h, extended stay >72h

ICU — refractory | Persistent TdP despite all measures | ECMO consideration, emergent EP consult, transfer to tertiary center if no EP/ECMO capability

Every TdP patient needs **ICU admission with continuous telemetry**. Downgrade only when QTc <480 ms for 24+ hours, electrolytes are stable, and the offending agent has cleared.{{ref1}}{{ref5}}

Risk factors (risk is multiplicative)

Hypokalemia (most important), hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, bradycardia, QT-prolonging drugs (especially several at once), CYP3A4/2D6 inhibitor + QT substrate, IV administration and rapid infusion of QT drugs.

Female sex, age >65, congenital LQTS, structural heart disease (LVH, CHF with reduced EF), hepatic/renal impairment. The classic scenario is **drug + electrolyte + structural heart disease** stacking together.

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT give amiodarone or procainamide** — Class IA/III antiarrhythmics prolong QT and WILL worsen TdP. The most common lethal error.

**Do NOT give isoproterenol in congenital LQTS** — it paradoxically prolongs QT. Beta-blockers are first-line there instead.

**Do NOT withhold magnesium because serum Mg is "normal"** — intracellular stores are depleted regardless; give 4 g IV over 20 min to everyone.

**Do NOT replete potassium before magnesium** — hypokalemia is refractory until Mg is corrected first.

**Do NOT forget overdrive pacing** for recurrent/refractory TdP — it is preferred over pharmacologic isoproterenol when transvenous pacing is available.
