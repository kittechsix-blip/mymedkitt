CRAO: Stroke of the Eye

Adults with acute painless monocular vision loss suggestive of central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) or branch RAO (BRAO), ED setting. Excludes pediatrics and cases where exam/fundoscopy point to an alternative diagnosis (CRVO, optic neuritis, retinal detachment, cortical stroke) — see Differential.

⚠ Arteritic (GCA) CRAO: do NOT give tPA — start IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg immediately without waiting for temporal artery biopsy; fellow eye is at risk (25-50% bilateral within days-weeks).
⚠ Rule out GCA in ALL CRAO patients ≥50 years old (ESR, CRP, jaw claudication, temporal artery exam) before committing to the non-arteritic pathway.
⚠ IV thrombolysis for non-arteritic CRAO within 4.5h is Class 2b / C-LD (uncertain benefit) per 2026 AHA/ASA — THEIA and TenCRAOS RCTs were negative vs aspirin. Same contraindications as stroke tPA apply; document shared decision-making/equipoise.
⚠ Do not delay the treatment-window decision for ocular massage, eye drops, anterior chamber paracentesis, or detailed fundoscopy — none has proven benefit and all cost time.
⚠ All CRAO patients are admitted for stroke workup — 20-32% have concurrent cerebral infarcts. Outpatient management is not appropriate; do not discharge from the ED.

1 · Recognition & exam

CRAO is a CNS stroke equivalent. Per the AHA/ASA, central retinal artery occlusion is classified as a central nervous system infarction. 30%+ of CRAO patients have concurrent cerebral ischemia, with stroke risk peaking 1-7 days after CRAO. Classic presentation: painless, sudden (seconds to minutes), monocular vision loss.

WindowSignificance
90-100 minIrreversible retinal damage begins
4.5 hoursIV tPA treatment window
12+ hoursTreatment benefit unlikely

Document the precise time vision loss began — if the patient woke with symptoms, use the last known well time. This later determines tPA eligibility (Reperfusion section).

Visual acuity is often counting fingers or hand motion. Afferent pupillary defect (APD) on swinging flashlight testing is 92-98% sensitive for asymmetric afferent pathway disease — if there is NO APD with 'complete' vision loss, reconsider the diagnosis. Visual fields usually show dense central/total loss; peripheral sparing suggests cilioretinal artery preservation.

2 · What to do now

GCA suspected — start steroids now
Do NOT give tPA. Start IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg immediately — do not wait for temporal artery biopsy. Fellow eye is at risk (25-50% bilateral within days-weeks).
GCA uncertain — treat empirically
Start steroids empirically — risk of fellow-eye loss outweighs steroid risk. Send ESR/CRP/platelets and continue to the stroke-protocol pathway; steroids and tPA are not mutually exclusive here if GCA is ultimately ruled out.
Age ≥50 — evaluate for GCA
Ask about jaw claudication, new headache, scalp tenderness, PMR/constitutional symptoms; palpate temporal arteries; send ESR, CRP, platelets. Must rule out GCA before committing to the non-arteritic pathway.
Non-arteritic CRAO — tPA eligible
≤4.5h from onset, GCA excluded, CT head negative, no standard stroke-tPA contraindications. Document shared decision-making — 2026 AHA/ASA Class 2b/C-LD; THEIA and TenCRAOS were negative vs aspirin.
Non-arteritic CRAO — tPA not indicated
>4.5h from onset or contraindicated. No conservative treatment (ocular massage, paracentesis, IOP drops) has proven benefit; consider HBO if <12h. Focus on secondary prevention and the embolic workup.

**Do NOT discharge CRAO from the ED** — all patients are admitted for stroke workup (20-32% have concurrent infarcts).
