VAD / LVAD Emergency Management

Adult ED patients with an implanted continuous-flow ventricular assist device (LVAD) — HeartMate 3, HeartMate II, or HVAD — presenting for any complaint. Covers initial assessment, device evaluation, complication triage/management, cardiac arrest, and disposition.

⚠ No pulse is NORMAL in continuous-flow LVADs — use Doppler for MAP, not a standard BP cuff
⚠ Contact the VAD coordinator IMMEDIATELY for every ED visit — no exceptions
⚠ Do NOT clamp the outflow graft — causes acute aortic regurgitation and cardiogenic shock
⚠ MRI is CONTRAINDICATED in all current LVAD patients
⚠ NEVER pull, manipulate, or rotate the driveline — surgically anchored
⚠ Use ONLY leukoreduced, irradiated blood products — preserves future transplant candidacy
⚠ Avoid IV beta-blockers in VAD patients — reduce native cardiac contractility

Initial assessment — key principles

A Ventricular Assist Device (VAD/LVAD) patient presenting to the ED. No pulse is NORMAL in continuous-flow LVADs — they produce minimal/zero pulsatility. Contact the VAD coordinator immediately, every visit, no exceptions. Listen for pump hum — absence is a pump-failure emergency. MAP via Doppler — traditional BP cuffs are unreliable.

  1. Call the VAD center IMMEDIATELY while beginning assessment.
  2. Gather device type (HeartMate 3, HeartMate II, HVAD).
  3. Gather baseline pump parameters (speed, power, flow, PI).
  4. Gather current INR target and last INR.
  5. Gather recent device issues or hospitalizations.
  6. Gather VAD center contact number and transplant candidacy status.

Listen over the precordium/epigastrium for the pump hum — a steady high-pitched mechanical whirr confirms the pump is running. Simultaneous assessment: SpO₂ may be unreliable (non-pulsatile flow) — use waveform capnography if available. Assess perfusion by mental status, skin color/temp, capillary refill, urine output. Full neuro exam screening for stroke. Inspect driveline exit site, controller display, battery status.

No pump hum = device failure
No hum = pump not running — a critical emergency. Check all connections, reseat driveline to controller, swap to backup batteries/AC power, use backup controller if available. Do NOT clamp the outflow graft — causes acute aortic regurgitation and cardiogenic shock. Emergent transfer to VAD center required.
  1. Check all connections — reseat driveline to controller.
  2. Swap to backup batteries or connect to AC power.
  3. Use patient's backup controller if available.
  4. If pump cannot be restarted → native cardiac function only (severely impaired).

Complication triage

Select the most acute/urgent complaint if multiple are present. Common VAD ED presentations by frequency: GI bleeding (~30% of readmissions), infection (20-50%), arrhythmia (~50% lifetime), pump thrombosis (1.5-5%/year), RV failure (9-40%), stroke (~10% by 2 years).

Bleeding management

GI bleeding is the #1 cause of ED visits for VAD patients. Mechanism: continuous flow → acquired von Willebrand disease (shear forces destroy large VWF multimers) + intestinal AVMs from non-pulsatile flow + chronic anticoagulation.

Transfusion rule
Use ONLY leukoreduced, irradiated blood products — prevents HLA allosensitization that could preclude future heart transplant.
  1. Large-bore IV access, type & crossmatch.
  2. Transfuse leukoreduced, irradiated pRBCs — target Hgb >7 g/dL.
  3. Hold warfarin.
  4. Reversal: warfarin reversal carries LOW risk for acute pump thrombosis — do not withhold in life-threatening bleeding.
  5. GI consult for endoscopy.
Vitamin K
2.5-5 mg IV (moderate bleeding); 10 mg IV for life-threatening bleeding + 4-factor PCC

20% of VAD patients develop device-related infection within the first year. Common organisms: Staph aureus, coag-negative Staph, Pseudomonas, Candida. Initial workup: blood cultures ×2, wound culture, CBC, CRP, procalcitonin.

  1. Wound culture before antibiotics.
  2. Blood cultures ×2 (rule out bacteremia).
  3. Local wound care — cleanse with chlorhexidine, dry dressing.
  4. Superficial: oral anti-staphylococcal agent (cephalexin, TMP-SMX). Extending/deep: IV vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam.
  5. CT abdomen if concern for driveline tract or pocket infection.
  6. Immobilize driveline to prevent further trauma.
Never manipulate the driveline
NEVER pull, manipulate, or rotate the driveline — it is surgically anchored, and disruption risks catastrophic bleeding.
  1. Blood cultures ×2 (peripheral sites).
  2. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics: vancomycin + cefepime or piperacillin-tazobactam; add antifungal if prior Candida or prolonged antibiotics.
  3. TTE/TEE — evaluate for vegetations on pump components.
  4. CT chest/abdomen — driveline tract abscess, pump pocket fluid.
  5. ID consult for long-term antibiotic plan.

Duration: typically 6+ weeks IV antibiotics for device-related BSI. Device removal is definitive but may not be feasible — suppressive antibiotics are often required indefinitely.

RV failure management

RV failure is the Achilles heel of LVAD therapy — the LVAD only supports the left ventricle. Signs: elevated JVP, peripheral edema, ascites, hepatic congestion, low LVAD flow despite adequate speed.

Suction event

The LV is too empty — the pump pulls the septum/ventricular wall against the inflow cannula. Causes: hypovolemia, RV failure, tamponade, arrhythmia, excessive pump speed. Controller signs: low flow, power fluctuations, low PI.

  1. Volume challenge: 250-500 mL NS bolus — LVADs are preload dependent.
  2. HeartMate 3 has automatic low-speed mode when suction detected — allow it to function.
  3. Do NOT increase pump speed (worsens suction).
  4. Assess for underlying cause with bedside echo.
  5. If recurrent despite volume → suspect RV failure.

Stroke in VAD patient

  1. STAT CT head (non-contrast) — determine type.
  2. CTA head/neck if ischemic.
  3. Check INR — patients are on warfarin.
  4. Full neurologic exam.
  5. Check pump parameters — high power may indicate underlying thrombosis.

Hemorrhagic stroke: hold all anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy. Balance reversal vs pump thrombosis risk (discuss with VAD team). Neurosurgery consult.

Ischemic stroke: systemic tPA generally CONTRAINDICATED (already anticoagulated). Mechanical thrombectomy may be an option — neuro-IR consult. Evaluate for pump thrombosis as embolic source.

MRI contraindicated
MRI is CONTRAINDICATED in all current LVAD patients. Consult neurology AND VAD team simultaneously.

Arrhythmia in VAD patient

VAD patients may TOLERATE sustained VT/VF because the pump maintains forward flow independent of rhythm. Prolonged arrhythmias → decreased RV filling → decreased LVAD preload → low flow → end-organ damage. Check electrolytes (K⁺ >4.0, Mg²⁺ >2.0); interrogate ICD; review pump parameters (suction events can trigger arrhythmias).

IV

Atrial fibrillation/flutter: rate control cautiously — avoid IV beta-blockers (reduce contractility). Amiodarone preferred for rate + rhythm control. Maintain therapeutic anticoagulation.

Cardioversion/defibrillation
Anterior-posterior pad placement — avoid pads directly over device or driveline. Standard biphasic energy. Never disconnect LVAD during cardioversion. All VAD arrhythmias warrant admission for monitoring.

VAD cardiac arrest protocol

  1. Assess perfusion, NOT pulse: responsiveness, MAP by Doppler (perfusion if >50), ETCO₂ if intubated (perfusion if >20), skin perfusion.
  2. Check the pump: auscultate for hum, check controller power/flow/alarms, troubleshoot connections/batteries/backup controller.
  3. Start chest compressions if unresponsive AND MAP <50 mmHg and/or ETCO₂ <20 mmHg and/or pump not running (no hum).
CPR is safe in VAD patients
Chest compressions ARE safe in VAD patients — AHA endorsed 2017. Autopsy studies show no cannula dislodgement.

Defibrillation is SAFE in VAD patients. Pad placement: anterior-posterior preferred — do NOT place pads directly over the LVAD device or driveline. Standard biphasic energy (200J). Disconnect from AC power during shock (run on batteries only). Never disconnect the LVAD controller during defibrillation.

Assess for return of perfusion: MAP >60 by Doppler, ETCO₂ >20 and rising, return of consciousness, improving pump parameters (flow, PI), improving skin perfusion. If restored → post-arrest care with targeted temperature management, hemodynamic optimization, identify the precipitating cause.

  1. Continue CPR and ACLS.
  2. Exhaust device troubleshooting — swap controller, check all connections.
  3. Consider VA-ECMO if available and patient is a candidate — initiation within 60 minutes improves outcomes.
  4. Address all reversible causes (Hs and Ts + device-specific).
  5. Contact VAD center emergently for guidance.
Termination of resuscitation
Same general principles as standard ACLS. Discuss with VAD team and family. Consider goals of care and device deactivation — a comfort-care decision that involves turning off pump speed.

Disposition

Nearly ALL VAD patients presenting to the ED require admission or transfer. Discharge is rare and requires explicit VAD team approval.

  1. ICU/CCU or telemetry floor based on acuity.
  2. Continuous telemetry.
  3. Doppler MAP checks q4h (or continuous arterial line if critical).
  4. Record pump parameters q shift (speed, power, flow, PI).
  5. Heparin bridge if warfarin is held — target PTT per VAD team guidance.
  6. Daily labs: CBC, BMP, coags, LDH, haptoglobin.
  7. Daily driveline site assessment with sterile technique.
  8. VAD coordinator notified and co-managing.
  • Pump malfunction or failure.
  • Suspected pump thrombosis requiring exchange.
  • Stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic).
  • Device infection requiring surgical management.
  • Hemodynamic instability refractory to ED management.
  • Driveline damage or electrical fault.
  • Need for pump speed adjustment (only VAD team can do this).

Transfer checklist: direct physician-to-physician call with accepting VAD center; stabilize (pressors, volume, blood products); document/send pump parameters, labs, imaging, drips; bring backup controller and extra batteries; ensure continuous pump power during transport.

May be appropriate for a minor driveline issue managed with local wound care, a therapeutic INR check with stable parameters, a resolved alarm with confirmed normal parameters, or a medication refill with no acute complaints. Requires: VAD team explicit approval, parameters at/near baseline, MAP in goal range (70-90 mmHg), no thrombosis/active infection/ongoing bleeding, therapeutic INR, follow-up within 24-48 hours.

RV failure is the Achilles heel of LVAD therapy — the LVAD only supports the left ventricle. Signs: elevated JVP, peripheral edema, ascites, hepatic congestion, low LVAD flow despite adequate speed.

Milrinone: 0.125-0.375 mcg/kg/min IV (no loading dose in VAD) IV — RV afterload reduction.

Dobutamine: 2.5-5 mcg/kg/min IV IV — RV contractility support.

Epinephrine (low-dose): 0.01-0.03 mcg/kg/min IV IV — RV contractility, alternative/adjunct to dobutamine.

Vasopressin: 0.02-0.04 units/min IV IV — Preferred — does not increase pulmonary vascular resistance.

Norepinephrine: 0.1-0.2 mcg/kg/min IV IV — If additional MAP support needed.

AVOID intubation/positive-pressure ventilation if possible — increases RV afterload and can precipitate an RV-failure spiral. Escalation: RVAD or VA-ECMO at VAD center.

Suction event

The LV is too empty — the pump pulls the septum/ventricular wall against the inflow cannula. Causes: hypovolemia, RV failure, tamponade, arrhythmia, excessive pump speed. Controller signs: low flow, power fluctuations, low PI.

  • Volume challenge: 250-500 mL NS bolus — LVADs are preload dependent.
  • HeartMate 3 has automatic low-speed mode when suction detected — allow it to function.
  • Do NOT increase pump speed (worsens suction).
  • Assess for underlying cause with bedside echo.
  • If recurrent despite volume → suspect RV failure.

Stroke in VAD patient

  • STAT CT head (non-contrast) — determine type.
  • CTA head/neck if ischemic.
  • Check INR — patients are on warfarin.
  • Full neurologic exam.
  • Check pump parameters — high power may indicate underlying thrombosis.

Hemorrhagic stroke: hold all anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy. Balance reversal vs pump thrombosis risk (discuss with VAD team). Neurosurgery consult.

Vitamin K: 10 mg IV + 4-factor PCC IV — For hemorrhagic stroke reversal.

Ischemic stroke: systemic tPA generally CONTRAINDICATED (already anticoagulated). Mechanical thrombectomy may be an option — neuro-IR consult. Evaluate for pump thrombosis as embolic source.

MRI is CONTRAINDICATED in all current LVAD patients. Consult neurology AND VAD team simultaneously.

Arrhythmia in VAD patient

VAD patients may TOLERATE sustained VT/VF because the pump maintains forward flow independent of rhythm. Prolonged arrhythmias → decreased RV filling → decreased LVAD preload → low flow → end-organ damage. Check electrolytes (K⁺ >4.0, Mg²⁺ >2.0); interrogate ICD; review pump parameters (suction events can trigger arrhythmias).

Amiodarone: 150 mg IV over 10 min, then 1 mg/min ×6h, then 0.5 mg/min ×18h IV — Preferred antiarrhythmic — hemodynamically stable, effective for atrial and ventricular arrhythmias.

Lidocaine: 1-1.5 mg/kg IV bolus, then 1-4 mg/min infusion IV — Alternative antiarrhythmic for hemodynamically tolerated VT.

Atrial fibrillation/flutter: rate control cautiously — avoid IV beta-blockers (reduce contractility). Amiodarone preferred for rate + rhythm control. Maintain therapeutic anticoagulation.

Anterior-posterior pad placement — avoid pads directly over device or driveline. Standard biphasic energy. Never disconnect LVAD during cardioversion. All VAD arrhythmias warrant admission for monitoring.

VAD cardiac arrest protocol

  • Assess perfusion, NOT pulse: responsiveness, MAP by Doppler (perfusion if >50), ETCO₂ if intubated (perfusion if >20), skin perfusion.
  • Check the pump: auscultate for hum, check controller power/flow/alarms, troubleshoot connections/batteries/backup controller.
  • Start chest compressions if unresponsive AND MAP <50 mmHg and/or ETCO₂ <20 mmHg and/or pump not running (no hum).

Chest compressions ARE safe in VAD patients — AHA endorsed 2017. Autopsy studies show no cannula dislodgement.

Defibrillation is SAFE in VAD patients. Pad placement: anterior-posterior preferred — do NOT place pads directly over the LVAD device or driveline. Standard biphasic energy (200J). Disconnect from AC power during shock (run on batteries only). Never disconnect the LVAD controller during defibrillation.

Epinephrine: 1 mg (1:10,000) IV/IO every 3-5 minutes IV/IO — Standard ACLS — all ACLS medications are permitted in VAD arrest.

Amiodarone: 300 mg IV first dose, 150 mg IV second dose IV

Lidocaine: 1-1.5 mg/kg IV — Alternative antiarrhythmic.

Tenecteplase: Weight-based bolus (30-50 mg by weight tier) IV — If massive PE suspected as cause of VAD cardiac arrest.

Additional considerations: volume resuscitation (hypovolemia is a common precipitant), bedside echo (RV/LV size, tamponade, cannula position), rule out tension pneumothorax/PE/tamponade.

Small RV + Small LV | Hypovolemia | Volume resuscitation

Large RV + Small LV | PE, RV infarct | Consider thrombolytics

Large RV + Large LV | Pump thrombosis | Heparin, VAD team

Pericardial effusion | Tamponade | Pericardiocentesis

Small LV, septal shift | Suction event | Volume challenge

  • Hypovolemia / Hemorrhage (most common)
  • Hydrogen ion (acidosis)
  • Hypo/Hyperkalemia
  • Hypothermia
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Tamponade
  • Thrombosis (pump or coronary)
  • Toxins
  • Device failure (check power, connections)
  • Suction event (volume challenge)

Assess for return of perfusion: MAP >60 by Doppler, ETCO₂ >20 and rising, return of consciousness, improving pump parameters (flow, PI), improving skin perfusion. If restored → post-arrest care with targeted temperature management, hemodynamic optimization, identify the precipitating cause.

  • Continue CPR and ACLS.
  • Exhaust device troubleshooting — swap controller, check all connections.
  • Consider VA-ECMO if available and patient is a candidate — initiation within 60 minutes improves outcomes.
  • Address all reversible causes (Hs and Ts + device-specific).
  • Contact VAD center emergently for guidance.

Same general principles as standard ACLS. Discuss with VAD team and family. Consider goals of care and device deactivation — a comfort-care decision that involves turning off pump speed.

Disposition

Nearly ALL VAD patients presenting to the ED require admission or transfer. Discharge is rare and requires explicit VAD team approval.

  • ICU/CCU or telemetry floor based on acuity.
  • Continuous telemetry.
  • Doppler MAP checks q4h (or continuous arterial line if critical).
  • Record pump parameters q shift (speed, power, flow, PI).
  • Heparin bridge if warfarin is held — target PTT per VAD team guidance.
  • Daily labs: CBC, BMP, coags, LDH, haptoglobin.
  • Daily driveline site assessment with sterile technique.
  • VAD coordinator notified and co-managing.
  • Pump malfunction or failure.
  • Suspected pump thrombosis requiring exchange.
  • Stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic).
  • Device infection requiring surgical management.
  • Hemodynamic instability refractory to ED management.
  • Driveline damage or electrical fault.
  • Need for pump speed adjustment (only VAD team can do this).

Transfer checklist: direct physician-to-physician call with accepting VAD center; stabilize (pressors, volume, blood products); document/send pump parameters, labs, imaging, drips; bring backup controller and extra batteries; ensure continuous pump power during transport.

May be appropriate for a minor driveline issue managed with local wound care, a therapeutic INR check with stable parameters, a resolved alarm with confirmed normal parameters, or a medication refill with no acute complaints. Requires: VAD team explicit approval, parameters at/near baseline, MAP in goal range (70-90 mmHg), no thrombosis/active infection/ongoing bleeding, therapeutic INR, follow-up within 24-48 hours.

Management algorithm

VAD patient presenting to ED — No pulse is NORMAL in continuous-flow LVADs. Contact VAD coordinator immediately. Listen for pump hum. MAP via Doppler.

Contact VAD coordinator immediately — Gather device type, baseline parameters, INR target/last INR, VAD center number, transplant candidacy.

Auscultate for pump hum — Steady high-pitched whirr = pump running. SpO2 unreliable — use capnography/perfusion signs.

No hum → device failure, do NOT clamp outflow graft — Troubleshoot connections/batteries/backup controller. Dobutamine + norepinephrine support. Emergent transfer.

MAP assessment (Doppler) — Cuff + pencil Doppler over brachial artery; first return of flow signal = MAP. Goal 70-90 mmHg.

MAP >90: treat hypertension — Hydralazine first-line; avoid IV beta-blockers. Goal MAP 70-80.

Controller parameter check — Compare speed/power/flow/PI to baseline. Do NOT change speed.

Low flow (<3 L/min) evaluation — Key discriminator is volume status — JVP/IVC on bedside ultrasound.

Complication triage — GI bleeding ~30% of readmissions; infection 20-50%; arrhythmia ~50% lifetime; pump thrombosis 1.5-5%/yr; RV failure 9-40%; stroke ~10% by 2 years.

Alarm troubleshooting — Connections, batteries, pump hum, backup controller. Position flat before battery swap.

Bleeding management — #1 cause of ED visits — acquired vWD + AVMs + anticoagulation. Use ONLY leukoreduced, irradiated blood.

Pump thrombosis — High power + hemolysis until proven otherwise — 48% six-month mortality untreated. HM3 can thrombose with normal power/LDH.

Device infection — 20% of VAD patients develop device infection in year 1. Driveline most common; BSI life-threatening.

RV failure management — Achilles heel of LVAD therapy — optimize preload/afterload/contractility. Avoid intubation if possible.

Suction event — LV too empty — septum pulled against cannula. Volume challenge; do NOT increase pump speed.

Stroke in VAD patient — Ischemic 6.8%, hemorrhagic 8.4%. Systemic tPA contraindicated. MRI contraindicated in all current LVAD patients.

Arrhythmia in VAD patient — VAD patients may TOLERATE VT/VF — pump maintains flow independent of rhythm. Assess hemodynamics, not just rhythm.

Active bleeding management — Leukoreduced/irradiated pRBCs, hold warfarin, DDAVP, octreotide for GI AVMs. Reversal carries LOW pump-thrombosis risk.

Minor / controlled bleeding — Check INR, local hemostasis, consider DDAVP. Monitor 4-6h — can escalate rapidly.

Driveline exit-site infection — ~50% of device infections. NEVER pull, manipulate, or rotate the driveline.

Bloodstream / deep infection — Broad-spectrum IV abx, TEE for vegetations, 6+ weeks therapy.

Stable arrhythmia management — Amiodarone preferred; avoid IV beta-blockers; AP pad placement away from device. All VAD arrhythmias admit.

Disposition decision — Nearly ALL VAD ED visits require admission or transfer — discharge is rare and needs explicit VAD team approval.

VAD cardiac arrest protocol — Assess perfusion, NOT pulse. Check the pump. CPR if unresponsive + MAP <50 and/or ETCO2 <20 and/or pump not running. CPR is safe.

Defibrillation & ACLS medications — Defibrillation safe; AP pads away from device; disconnect AC power; all standard ACLS meds permitted.

Arrest echo & reversible causes — Bedside echo differentiates hypovolemia, PE, pump thrombosis, tamponade, suction event. Hs and Ts plus device failure/suction.

Post-arrest assessment — MAP >60, ETCO2 >20 rising, return of consciousness, improving pump parameters.

Refractory arrest — Continue CPR/ACLS, exhaust device troubleshooting, consider VA-ECMO within 60 min, discuss goals of care.

Admit — ICU/CCU or telemetry with VAD team co-management. Doppler MAP q4h, pump parameters q shift.

Transfer to VAD center — Pump malfunction, thrombosis needing exchange, stroke, surgical infection, or refractory instability all require transfer.

Discharge (rare) — Requires explicit VAD team approval, parameters near baseline, MAP in goal range, follow-up in 24-48h.
