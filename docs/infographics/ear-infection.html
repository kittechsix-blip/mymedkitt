Ear Infection: AOM, Otitis Externa, Malignant OE, Otomycosis, Perforated TM, Mastoiditis

Emergency/urgent care evaluation and management of ear infections across all ages — acute otitis media, otitis externa (including malignant/necrotizing OE), otomycosis, traumatic or infectious tympanic membrane perforation, and acute mastoiditis.

⚠ Malignant (necrotizing) OE is an EMERGENCY until excluded — granulation tissue at the bony-cartilaginous junction, cranial nerve palsy (CN VII most common), severe disproportionate pain, trismus, or OE failing topical treatment after 7-10 days in a diabetic/immunocompromised patient. Mortality up to 20% if untreated.
⚠ Mastoiditis classic triad — postauricular swelling/erythema, mastoid tenderness, pinna protrusion — is a surgical emergency if a subperiosteal abscess is present. 6-23% of cases have intracranial complications.
⚠ Never use ototoxic aminoglycoside drops (neomycin, tobramycin, polymyxin B, gentamicin) or alcohol-containing drops in a perforated TM — use only fluoroquinolones (ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin ± dexamethasone).
⚠ AOM cannot be diagnosed on TM erythema alone — requires bulging TM (92% sensitive) or new otorrhea, PLUS confirmed middle ear effusion by pneumatic otoscopy or tympanometry.
⚠ Watchful waiting for AOM requires ALL of: age ≥6 months, mild symptoms, reliable follow-up in 48-72h, and a safety-net prescription — excluded if age <6 months, tympanostomy tubes, immune deficiency, craniofacial abnormality, or recurrent/chronic OM.
⚠ Otomycosis needs antifungal treatment, not antibacterial drops — suspect when OE fails topical antibacterials and itching exceeds pain; do NOT irrigate fungal debris (promotes spread).

5 · Perforated tympanic membrane

Traumatic perforation
Direct trauma (Q-tips, bobby pins, pencils), barotrauma, blast, or slap injury — usually clean edges, central location. Use only fluoroquinolone drops (ciprofloxacin 0.3% 3-4 drops BID, ofloxacin 0.3% 5-10 drops daily, or cipro/dexamethasone 4 drops BID) — avoid neomycin, tobramycin, polymyxin B, gentamicin, and alcohol-containing drops (ototoxic). Urgent ENT referral for sensorineural hearing loss, vertigo, suspected ossicular damage, facial nerve palsy, or CSF leak.[11][12]
Infectious perforation (AOM complication)
Spontaneous rupture with purulent drainage; pain typically improves after rupture (pressure release). Treat as AOM: amoxicillin 80-90 mg/kg/day divided BID × 10 days (extended course), or amox-clav if indicated, plus ofloxacin 0.3% 5-10 drops daily × 7 days or cipro/dexamethasone 4 drops BID. Do NOT use benzocaine drops (TM perforated). Recheck in 2-4 weeks — most AOM perforations heal within 1 month.[1][11]

7 · Disposition

Return immediately if: swelling/redness behind the ear, facial weakness or drooping, severe headache or stiff neck, high fever (>39°C), worsening pain despite treatment, or dizziness/hearing loss. Keep ear dry; complete full antibiotic course; no swimming until cleared. Follow-up: primary care in 2-4 weeks, sooner if no improvement in 48-72 hours; ENT referral if recurrent.[1][6]

Potential diagnoses: acute mastoiditis, necrotizing (malignant) otitis externa, or intracranial extension. Immediate actions: IV antibiotics (per specific condition), CT temporal bone with contrast, urgent ENT consult, blood cultures, baseline labs (CBC, BMP, ESR, CRP). Consults as needed: Infectious Disease, Neurosurgery, Endocrinology.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls6 items
Do NOT diagnose AOM on TM erythema alone — crying or fever can cause erythema without effusion; require bulging TM or new otorrhea PLUS confirmed middle ear effusion.
Do NOT use ototoxic aminoglycoside drops (neomycin, tobramycin, polymyxin B, gentamicin) or alcohol-containing drops in a perforated TM — use fluoroquinolones only.
Do NOT delay CT and IV antibiotics for malignant OE red flags (granulation tissue, CN palsy, severe disproportionate pain, trismus) in a diabetic/immunocompromised patient — mortality up to 20% if untreated.
Do NOT miss the mastoiditis triad (postauricular swelling, mastoid tenderness, pinna protrusion) — surgical emergency if a subperiosteal abscess is present.
Do NOT use antibacterial-only drops for suspected otomycosis — fungal OE (fluffy debris, itching > pain, failed antibacterials) needs an antifungal (clotrimazole) and aural toilet without irrigation.
Do NOT offer watchful waiting outside its criteria — age <6 months, tympanostomy tubes, immune deficiency, craniofacial abnormality, or recurrent/chronic OM all exclude watchful waiting; give antibiotics instead.

Overall: 80-94% heal spontaneously. Keep ear dry (cotton ball + petroleum jelly for showering); no swimming; avoid forceful nose blowing. Tympanoplasty if perforation persists >2-3 months (success rate 90-95%).[12]

6 · Mastoiditis

Classic triad: postauricular swelling/erythema, mastoid tenderness, pinna protrusion. Intracranial complications in 6-23% of cases (headache, altered mental status, nuchal rigidity, seizures, focal neuro signs).[13]

7 · Disposition

Return immediately if: swelling/redness behind the ear, facial weakness or drooping, severe headache or stiff neck, high fever (>39°C), worsening pain despite treatment, or dizziness/hearing loss. Keep ear dry; complete full antibiotic course; no swimming until cleared. Follow-up: primary care in 2-4 weeks, sooner if no improvement in 48-72 hours; ENT referral if recurrent.[1][6]

Potential diagnoses: acute mastoiditis, necrotizing (malignant) otitis externa, or intracranial extension. Immediate actions: IV antibiotics (per specific condition), CT temporal bone with contrast, urgent ENT consult, blood cultures, baseline labs (CBC, BMP, ESR, CRP). Consults as needed: Infectious Disease, Neurosurgery, Endocrinology.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT diagnose AOM on TM erythema alone — crying or fever can cause erythema without effusion; require bulging TM or new otorrhea PLUS confirmed middle ear effusion.

Do NOT use ototoxic aminoglycoside drops (neomycin, tobramycin, polymyxin B, gentamicin) or alcohol-containing drops in a perforated TM — use fluoroquinolones only.

Do NOT delay CT and IV antibiotics for malignant OE red flags (granulation tissue, CN palsy, severe disproportionate pain, trismus) in a diabetic/immunocompromised patient — mortality up to 20% if untreated.

Do NOT miss the mastoiditis triad (postauricular swelling, mastoid tenderness, pinna protrusion) — surgical emergency if a subperiosteal abscess is present.

Do NOT use antibacterial-only drops for suspected otomycosis — fungal OE (fluffy debris, itching > pain, failed antibacterials) needs an antifungal (clotrimazole) and aural toilet without irrigation.

Do NOT offer watchful waiting outside its criteria — age <6 months, tympanostomy tubes, immune deficiency, craniofacial abnormality, or recurrent/chronic OM all exclude watchful waiting; give antibiotics instead.
