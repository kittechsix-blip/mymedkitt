Thrombocytopenia: Confirm, Screen Danger, Classify, Dispo

Adults ≥18 in the ED with a low platelet count (<150,000/microL). Excludes neonatal/pediatric thrombocytopenia and detailed inpatient hematology-oncology chemotherapy protocols — this is the ED recognition-and-first-action layer, not a hematology consult replacement.

⚠ Do not label isolated thrombocytopenia as ITP until TTP/TMA, DIC/sepsis, HIT, pregnancy emergencies, acute leukemia/marrow failure, and pseudothrombocytopenia have been addressed.
⚠ Critical bleeding with thrombocytopenia: resuscitate, activate blood bank early, transfuse platelets, and correct coagulopathy while treating the cause. Avoid routine platelet transfusion in suspected TTP or HIT unless there is life-threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure.
⚠ Screen every new/unexplained thrombocytopenia for TTP/TMA, DIC/sepsis, HIT, pregnancy danger patterns, and marrow failure before anchoring on a benign diagnosis — these are the patterns that change ED management now.
⚠ MAHA plus thrombocytopenia is TTP/TMA until proven otherwise. Send ADAMTS13 before plasma if possible, call hematology, and do not delay plasma exchange for results.
⚠ Intermediate or high probability HIT (4Ts ≥4): stop all heparin products — UFH, LMWH, flushes, heparin-coated lines — and start non-heparin anticoagulation unless bleeding risk prohibits it.
⚠ HIT action bundle: send PF4/heparin immunoassay, start a non-heparin anticoagulant, and do NOT start warfarin until platelet recovery — HIT patients clot; the danger is thrombosis more than bleeding.
⚠ Pregnant or postpartum thrombocytopenia with hypertension, RUQ pain, headache, hemolysis, renal injury, or elevated LFTs needs urgent OB/MFM evaluation for HELLP/preeclampsia/TMA — do not assume benign gestational thrombocytopenia when the patient is sick or the count is very low.

1 · First look, confirm, bleeding triage

Definition: platelet count <150,000/microL. ED risk depends less on the number alone and more on bleeding, acuity, trajectory, smear findings, coagulopathy, pregnancy, heparin exposure, thrombosis, infection, and marrow failure clues.

First 60 seconds: Is the patient actively bleeding or going to an urgent procedure? Is this a new severe drop or chronic baseline? Does the smear show clumping, schistocytes, or blasts? Are PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, creatinine, LDH, bilirubin, and haptoglobin abnormal? Any heparin exposure in the last 5-14 days, new thrombosis, pregnancy/postpartum state, sepsis, cancer/chemo, liver disease, alcohol use, or new medication?

ED principle: do not label isolated thrombocytopenia as ITP until TTP/TMA, DIC/sepsis, HIT, pregnancy emergencies, acute leukemia/marrow failure, and pseudothrombocytopenia have been addressed.

Before escalating a stable patient, confirm the result is real. Check prior platelet counts and rate of fall, repeat CBC if unexpected or discordant, and review the peripheral smear for clumping, giant platelets, schistocytes, blasts, or dysplasia. If clumping is reported or suspected, repeat the platelet count in a citrate or heparin tube.

Pseudothrombocytopenia
Platelet clumping can falsely lower the automated platelet count. Confirm with a smear showing clumps and a repeat CBC in a citrate or heparin tube that normalizes or improves. There is no platelet-directed treatment — document the artifact clearly so future clinicians do not transfuse or cancel needed care based on a false count.

Critical bleeding: intracranial, airway, massive GI, hemoptysis with compromise, postpartum/obstetric hemorrhage, uncontrolled surgical/procedural bleeding, shock, or bleeding needing emergent intervention.

Important exceptions: avoid routine platelet transfusion in suspected TTP or HIT unless life-threatening bleeding or an urgent procedure.

2 · Critical bleeding bundle

Critical bleeding — stabilize first
ABCs, two large-bore IVs, type and cross, activate MTP if shock or massive bleeding. Transfuse platelets if thrombocytopenic and bleeding is major, procedural, or critical site. Send CBC with smear, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, CMP, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, retic, type/screen. Correct fibrinogen with cryoprecipitate when low and bleeding. Reverse anticoagulant/antiplatelet effect when relevant. Control source: procedure, endoscopy, IR, surgery, OB, ENT, dental, or local measures.

Do not stop at transfusion. Platelets are supportive — the diagnosis may still be DIC, TTP, HIT, leukemia, drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, liver failure, or ITP.

3 · Dangerous syndrome screen

PatternKey clues
TTP/TMASchistocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, renal injury, neuro symptoms; coags often normal

MAHA/TTP/TMA pattern
Treat as TTP/TMA until proven otherwise. Send CBC with smear, LDH, haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, retic, creatinine, UA, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, ADAMTS13 activity/inhibitor before plasma exposure if feasible, and a pregnancy test when applicable. Call hematology urgently. Calculate the PLASMIC score, but do not let scoring delay escalation in a sick patient — if high suspicion, start the plasma exchange pathway urgently; steroids and caplacizumab are specialist-guided adjuncts. Avoid platelet transfusion unless life-threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure. Parallel differential: HUS, complement-mediated TMA, malignant hypertension, pregnancy TMA/HELLP, DIC, drug-induced TMA, transplant/cancer-associated TMA.
DIC / sepsis / consumption pattern
DIC is always secondary — the primary treatment is source control and reversal of the trigger (sepsis, trauma, obstetric hemorrhage, malignancy/APL, liver failure). Send serial CBC, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, CMP, lactate. Transfuse for bleeding or procedures, not just abnormal labs — platelets often target >50k for active bleeding/procedure; fibrinogen target usually >150-200 mg/dL depending on context, higher in obstetric hemorrhage. Call hematology early when DIC is severe, unclear, thrombotic-dominant, or APL is possible.
Pregnant or postpartum
Do not assume gestational thrombocytopenia when the patient is sick or the count is very low. Danger patterns: preeclampsia/HELLP (hypertension, headache, RUQ/epigastric pain, elevated AST/ALT, hemolysis, renal injury), DIC (abruption, fetal demise, sepsis, postpartum hemorrhage, amniotic fluid embolism), TTP/HUS (MAHA, neuro symptoms, renal injury, coags often less abnormal than DIC), or ITP (isolated thrombocytopenia, often prior history). OB/MFM consultation early; send CBC with smear, CMP/LFTs, creatinine, urine protein, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer. Platelet thresholds are context-specific — delivery, neuraxial anesthesia, hemorrhage, and surgery require specialist-specific targets.
Blasts, pancytopenia, or marrow failure pattern
Red flags: blasts or promyelocytes on smear; pancytopenia or severe anemia/neutropenia with thrombocytopenia; new bruising/bleeding with fatigue, fever, weight loss, night sweats, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, or splenomegaly; chemotherapy/radiation, known cancer, marrow infiltration, aplastic anemia, MDS. Call hematology/oncology when blasts, pancytopenia, or severe unexplained cytopenias are present. Add uric acid, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, LDH if acute leukemia or tumor lysis risk. If APL is possible and DIC/bleeding is present, escalate immediately — APL is a hemorrhagic emergency. Avoid anchoring on ITP when there are other cytopenias or abnormal cells.

4Ts score: thrombocytopenia magnitude, timing of platelet fall, thrombosis or skin/systemic reaction, other causes of thrombocytopenia.

Management by probability: Low 4Ts (0-3): HIT unlikely, do not reflexively test. Intermediate/high 4Ts (>=4): stop all heparin, send PF4 immunoassay, and start a non-heparin anticoagulant unless bleeding risk is prohibitive. Use SRA or functional assay when immunoassay is positive or clinical uncertainty remains. Avoid platelet transfusion unless life-threatening bleeding or urgent procedure.

HIT action bundle — intermediate/high probability
1) Stop all heparin products: UFH, LMWH, flushes, heparin-coated lines when possible. 2) Send PF4/heparin immunoassay; send functional assay if available or immunoassay positive. 3) Start a non-heparin anticoagulant unless major bleeding risk: argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, or a DOAC in selected stable patients per local protocol and specialist guidance. 4) Do not start warfarin until platelet recovery — if already on warfarin early in HIT, discuss vitamin K reversal with hematology. 5) Document heparin allergy/intolerance clearly. Call pharmacy and hematology early — HIT patients clot; the danger is thrombosis more than bleeding.
HIT possible but active bleeding dominates
Bedside risk balance with hematology/pharmacy — bleeding risk may prohibit immediate non-heparin anticoagulation even with intermediate/high 4Ts probability. Route to specialist call for individualized management.

4 · Isolated/chronic mechanism & core labs

If the dangerous screen is negative, classify by mechanism. Isolated thrombocytopenia: normal hemoglobin/WBC, no schistocytes/blasts, normal PT/aPTT/fibrinogen, no systemic illness. Pattern questions: any new medication within days to 2 weeks? Recent viral illness, HIV/HCV risk, vaccination, autoimmune history? Alcohol use disorder, cirrhosis, splenomegaly, portal hypertension? Prior platelet counts chronically low? Mucosal bleeding or wet purpura? ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion — a pattern, not a single lab result.

Likely immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)
Isolated thrombocytopenia with otherwise reassuring smear and no alternative cause. No or minor bleeding and platelets generally >=30k: often no emergent treatment, arrange close follow-up. Platelets <30k, significant mucosal bleeding, high-risk patient, or unreliable follow-up: hematology discussion for corticosteroids. Severe mucosal bleeding, wet purpura, critical bleeding, or need for rapid platelet rise: IVIG plus corticosteroids; platelet transfusion only for life-threatening bleeding or urgent procedure, usually with IVIG/steroids because transfused platelets are rapidly destroyed. Avoid routine platelet transfusion for nonbleeding ITP and reflex admission for every isolated low platelet count without risk stratification.
Drug-induced thrombocytopenia
Medication review is a core diagnostic test. Common ED culprits: heparin/LMWH (HIT if timing and thrombosis fit); quinine/tonic water, TMP-SMX, vancomycin, beta-lactams, linezolid, rifampin, valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin, thiazides, GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors, chemotherapy, alcohol. Abrupt severe thrombocytopenia can occur with immune drug-induced thrombocytopenia; marrow-suppressive drugs often create additional cytopenias. Stop the suspected nonessential culprit, do not re-challenge, treat bleeding with platelet support when indicated, and discuss severe/uncertain/recurrent cases with hematology.
Liver disease, alcohol, or splenic sequestration
Common chronic bucket: cirrhosis/portal hypertension with splenic sequestration, alcohol-related marrow suppression and nutritional deficiency, chronic viral hepatitis, hypersplenism. Compare with baseline — sudden large drops need another diagnosis. Check INR/PT and fibrinogen when bleeding or ill; INR does not fully predict bleeding risk in cirrhosis. Evaluate for infection, GI bleeding, DIC, medication exposure, and alcohol withdrawal when clinically present. Transfusion decisions should be bleeding/procedure-driven, not count alone, except very low prophylactic thresholds in selected marrow-suppression settings.
Infection and systemic illness
Infection can cause thrombocytopenia by marrow suppression, immune destruction, consumption, splenic sequestration, or DIC. High-yield checks: sepsis screen/cultures/lactate/source evaluation when ill; HIV and hepatitis C testing when new isolated thrombocytopenia is unexplained or risk is present; tickborne disease, dengue, malaria, EBV/CMV, COVID/influenza, and travel-associated infections when exposure fits; medication exposures used to treat the infection can also cause thrombocytopenia. Disposition depends on illness severity and platelet trend — a well patient with mild viral-associated thrombocytopenia differs from a septic patient with falling platelets and coagulopathy.
  • Repeat CBC with differential; peripheral smear review by lab/pathology
  • PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer when bleeding, ill, pregnant/postpartum, or DIC/TMA concern
  • CMP including creatinine and liver tests
  • LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocyte count when hemolysis/TMA concern
  • HIV and HCV when isolated/unexplained; pregnancy test when applicable
  • Type and screen if bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, procedure, or possible admission
  • Add as indicated: B12/folate, TSH, ethanol/toxicology, tickborne/travel testing, autoimmune testing, urinalysis/protein, CT/US for splenomegaly or bleeding source

6 · Who to call & disposition

  • Suspected TTP/TMA or PLASMIC intermediate/high
  • Suspected HIT with thrombosis or need for alternative anticoagulation
  • DIC with bleeding/thrombosis, unclear trigger, or possible APL
  • Platelets <10k, severe mucosal bleeding, wet purpura, critical bleeding, or urgent procedure
  • Blasts, pancytopenia, suspected leukemia/marrow failure
  • Severe thrombocytopenia in pregnancy/postpartum with HELLP/TMA/DIC concern
  • Other calls by phenotype: OB/MFM, ICU, transfusion medicine/blood bank, pharmacy, surgery/IR/GI/ENT/neurosurgery, oncology

Admit or transfer when the diagnosis can deteriorate faster than outpatient follow-up can protect the patient.

Admit / transfer / observe
Telemetry/ICU if bleeding, TTP/TMA, DIC, severe sepsis, neurologic symptoms, severe renal injury, or transfusion need. Hematology-capable center for TTP/TMA/plasma exchange, suspected acute leukemia/APL, severe refractory ITP, or complex HIT. OB-capable setting for pregnancy/postpartum HELLP/TMA/DIC. Handoff must include: platelet count and trend, smear findings, coags/fibrinogen/D-dimer, hemolysis markers, heparin exposure/4Ts, medications stopped, transfusions given, and pending ADAMTS13/PF4 or other send-outs.
Discharge safety net
Discharge only if all are true: no active bleeding, no wet purpura, no concerning neuro/renal/systemic symptoms; smear has no schistocytes or blasts; coagulation profile does not suggest DIC when clinically relevant; platelets are not in a critical range and are stable or have a clear benign/chronic explanation; no intermediate/high probability HIT, no pregnancy danger pattern, no acute leukemia concern; medication review done and culprit drugs stopped if suspected; patient can return quickly and follow-up is realistic.

Return now for: headache, confusion, focal neuro deficit, syncope, melena/hematemesis, hematuria, heavy vaginal bleeding, mucosal bleeding, new petechiae/purpura, fever, chest pain/dyspnea, new limb swelling/pain, or worsening weakness.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls6 items
Do NOT label isolated thrombocytopenia as ITP before excluding TTP/TMA, DIC/sepsis, HIT, pregnancy emergencies, acute leukemia/marrow failure, and pseudothrombocytopenia.
Do NOT delay plasma exchange for ADAMTS13 results in a sick patient with high suspicion for TTP/TMA — send the test before plasma exposure, but escalate on clinical grounds.
Do NOT reflexively transfuse platelets in suspected TTP or HIT, or in nonbleeding ITP — reserve transfusion for life-threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure.
Do NOT start warfarin during active HIT before platelet recovery — discuss vitamin K reversal with hematology if the patient is already on warfarin early in HIT.
Do NOT assume gestational thrombocytopenia in a pregnant/postpartum patient who is sick or has a very low count — screen for HELLP/preeclampsia, DIC, and TTP/HUS first.
Do NOT anchor on ITP when blasts, pancytopenia, or other abnormal cell lines are present — these need urgent heme/onc evaluation, and APL is a hemorrhagic emergency.

  • Suspected TTP/TMA or PLASMIC intermediate/high
  • Suspected HIT with thrombosis or need for alternative anticoagulation
  • DIC with bleeding/thrombosis, unclear trigger, or possible APL
  • Platelets <10k, severe mucosal bleeding, wet purpura, critical bleeding, or urgent procedure
  • Blasts, pancytopenia, suspected leukemia/marrow failure
  • Severe thrombocytopenia in pregnancy/postpartum with HELLP/TMA/DIC concern
  • Other calls by phenotype: OB/MFM, ICU, transfusion medicine/blood bank, pharmacy, surgery/IR/GI/ENT/neurosurgery, oncology

Admit or transfer when the diagnosis can deteriorate faster than outpatient follow-up can protect the patient.

Telemetry/ICU if bleeding, TTP/TMA, DIC, severe sepsis, neurologic symptoms, severe renal injury, or transfusion need. Hematology-capable center for TTP/TMA/plasma exchange, suspected acute leukemia/APL, severe refractory ITP, or complex HIT. OB-capable setting for pregnancy/postpartum HELLP/TMA/DIC. Handoff must include: platelet count and trend, smear findings, coags/fibrinogen/D-dimer, hemolysis markers, heparin exposure/4Ts, medications stopped, transfusions given, and pending ADAMTS13/PF4 or other send-outs.

Discharge only if all are true: no active bleeding, no wet purpura, no concerning neuro/renal/systemic symptoms; smear has no schistocytes or blasts; coagulation profile does not suggest DIC when clinically relevant; platelets are not in a critical range and are stable or have a clear benign/chronic explanation; no intermediate/high probability HIT, no pregnancy danger pattern, no acute leukemia concern; medication review done and culprit drugs stopped if suspected; patient can return quickly and follow-up is realistic.

Return now for: headache, confusion, focal neuro deficit, syncope, melena/hematemesis, hematuria, heavy vaginal bleeding, mucosal bleeding, new petechiae/purpura, fever, chest pain/dyspnea, new limb swelling/pain, or worsening weakness.

7 · One-glance algorithm

Thrombocytopenia — first look — Platelet count <150,000/microL. Assess bleeding, acuity, trajectory, smear, coags, pregnancy, heparin exposure, thrombosis, infection, marrow failure clues before anchoring on ITP.

Active bleeding, critical count, or urgent procedure? — Critical bleeding = intracranial, airway, massive GI, hemoptysis with compromise, postpartum/obstetric hemorrhage, uncontrolled surgical/procedural bleeding, shock, or bleeding needing emergent intervention.

Confirm true thrombocytopenia — Repeat CBC/smear, citrate/heparin-tube recheck if clumping suspected, compare to prior counts.

Stabilize: resuscitate + transfuse + correct coagulopathy — ABCs, 2 large-bore IVs, type & cross, activate MTP if shock. Transfuse platelets for major/procedural/critical-site bleeding. Correct fibrinogen with cryoprecipitate. Reverse anticoagulants. Control source. Keep the differential open.

Pseudothrombocytopenia — no treatment — Clumping artifact confirmed on smear + repeat in citrate/heparin tube. Document clearly.

Dangerous syndrome screen positive? — TTP/TMA, DIC/sepsis, HIT, pregnancy danger pattern, or marrow failure — screen before diagnosing ITP.

Platelet transfusion decision — Threshold-based: <10k prophylaxis, <20k fever/sepsis/risk, <50k active bleeding/procedure, <100k CNS/critical site. Avoid reflex transfusion in TTP/HIT/nonbleeding ITP.

Danger pattern identified — urgent action + hematology call — TTP/TMA: ADAMTS13 before plasma, urgent plasma exchange. DIC: trigger control + platelet/fibrinogen targets. Pregnancy: OB/MFM for HELLP/TMA/DIC. Marrow: heme/onc for blasts/pancytopenia, APL is a hemorrhagic emergency.

HIT probability (4Ts)? — 4Ts: magnitude, timing, thrombosis/reaction, other causes. Low (0-3) HIT unlikely; intermediate/high (>=4) stop heparin.

Isolated/chronic mechanism? — Danger screen negative — classify by ITP, drug/toxin, liver/spleen, infection, or unclear cause.

Stop heparin + start non-heparin anticoagulation — Stop all heparin products, send PF4/functional assay, start argatroban/bivalirudin/fondaparinux/DOAC per protocol, avoid early warfarin, document allergy.

Classify mechanism: ITP / drug / liver-spleen / infection — ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion — treat by bleeding/count/follow-up reliability. Drug-induced: stop culprit, no re-challenge. Liver/spleen: baseline-driven, sudden drops need new workup. Infection: ranges benign viral to DIC/sepsis.

Core ED workup — Repeat CBC/smear, coags when indicated, CMP, hemolysis labs, HIV/HCV, pregnancy test, type/screen; add-as-indicated tests by exposure.

Who to call now — Hematology for TTP/TMA, HIT with thrombosis, DIC/APL, platelets <10k or critical bleeding, blasts/pancytopenia, pregnancy danger pattern. Plus OB/MFM, ICU, transfusion medicine, pharmacy, surgery/IR/GI/ENT/neurosurgery, oncology by phenotype.

Disposition? — Admit or transfer when the diagnosis can deteriorate faster than outpatient follow-up can protect the patient.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT label isolated thrombocytopenia as ITP before excluding TTP/TMA, DIC/sepsis, HIT, pregnancy emergencies, acute leukemia/marrow failure, and pseudothrombocytopenia.

Do NOT delay plasma exchange for ADAMTS13 results in a sick patient with high suspicion for TTP/TMA — send the test before plasma exposure, but escalate on clinical grounds.

Do NOT reflexively transfuse platelets in suspected TTP or HIT, or in nonbleeding ITP — reserve transfusion for life-threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure.

Do NOT start warfarin during active HIT before platelet recovery — discuss vitamin K reversal with hematology if the patient is already on warfarin early in HIT.

Do NOT assume gestational thrombocytopenia in a pregnant/postpartum patient who is sick or has a very low count — screen for HELLP/preeclampsia, DIC, and TTP/HUS first.

Do NOT anchor on ITP when blasts, pancytopenia, or other abnormal cell lines are present — these need urgent heme/onc evaluation, and APL is a hemorrhagic emergency.
