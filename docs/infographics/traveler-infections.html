algorithm

Returned International Traveler: Fever, GI, Rash, Respiratory & Neuro Triage

Adults presenting to the ED after international travel within the preceding several months, across five presenting syndromes: fever, GI, rash, respiratory, and neurologic. Excludes pediatric weight-based dosing, pregnancy-specific substitutions, and the full depth of malaria / meningitis / measles / rabies / tuberculosis / typhoid management — each has its own dedicated myMedKitt consult for deeper management beyond this ED-triage layer.

⚠ Isolate immediately on arrival if respiratory symptoms, hemorrhage, or rash + fever accompany the travel history — before the full workup begins.
⚠ STAT malaria testing (thick/thin smears + RDT) for every fever in a traveler from a malaria-endemic area — repeat q12-24h ×3 before ruling out. P. falciparum malaria is a medical emergency; clinical deterioration can occur within 24-36 hours.
⚠ Eschar present: start doxycycline immediately for suspected rickettsial infection — do NOT wait for serology. Case fatality is 20-40% if treatment is delayed, excellent outcomes if started within the first week.
⚠ Suspected typhoid/enteric fever: draw blood cultures BEFORE giving antibiotics. Untreated case fatality is 10-30%; early antibiotics reduce it to <1%.
⚠ Confirmed or suspected P. falciparum malaria: admit to ICU and call the CDC Malaria Hotline (770-488-7788 business hours / 770-488-7100 after hours) to arrange IV artesunate.
⚠ Petechiae/purpura + fever: treat as meningococcal disease until proven otherwise — blood cultures + LP, empiric ceftriaxone or cefotaxime, do NOT delay antibiotics if LP is delayed. Immediate admission for all petechiae/purpura + fever.
⚠ Suspected measles or active TB: airborne precautions (AIIR room, N95) immediately, before confirmatory testing returns.
⚠ Rabies PEP: do NOT wait for animal observation or testing if the exposure is high-risk — start wound care, HRIG, and vaccine in the ED. Rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic.
⚠ Life-threatening neurologic causes (cerebral malaria, meningococcal meningitis, rabies, Japanese encephalitis) must be actively excluded first in any returned traveler with neurologic symptoms.

1 · Recognize & triage

Returned international traveler: isolate immediately if respiratory symptoms, hemorrhage, or rash accompany the fever. Take a travel history (countries, dates, return date) and calculate the incubation period. Determine the presenting syndrome — fever, GI, rash, respiratory, or neurologic — to pick the branch below.

Immediate concerns (don't miss): malaria, typhoid/enteric fever, rickettsial infection, meningococcal disease, severe dengue, and (rarely) viral hemorrhagic fever. The 2026 high-risk ED bundle is malaria, dengue, enteric fever, and leptospirosis.

Incubation periodLikely diagnoses
<10 daysDengue, chikungunya, plague, COVID-19
10-14 daysMalaria, typhoid, leptospirosis
>2 weeksMalaria, hepatitis, parasites, TB

Physical exam red flags: eschar → rickettsial infection (start doxycycline immediately); hepatosplenomegaly → malaria, typhoid, hepatitis; jaundice → malaria, hepatitis, leptospirosis; petechiae/purpura → hemorrhagic fever, meningococcal disease.

2 · Fever module

4 · Rash module

Maculopapular: dengue, chikungunya, Zika, measles, rickettsial. Petechiae/purpura: meningococcal, hemorrhagic fever, thrombocytopenia. Vesicular: varicella, monkeypox. Eschar: rickettsial (pathognomonic).

Dengue: severe myalgia, thrombocytopenia, no prominent arthritis. Chikungunya: severe symmetric arthritis, can persist months to years. Zika: generally mild, conjunctivitis, pregnancy concern (microcephaly). Measles: Koplik spots, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis. Cannot distinguish clinically — CBC, dengue/chikungunya/Zika PCR & serology, measles PCR if suspected.

No aspirin or NSAIDs until dengue is ruled out
Acetaminophen only for fever/pain. Monitor for dengue warning signs.
Petechiae / purpura (folded leaf: ti-petechiae)meningococcal emergency + VHF

Life-threatening causes: meningococcal disease, viral hemorrhagic fever (rare), severe dengue (hemorrhagic), severe *P. falciparum* malaria. Workup: CBC, coagulation studies (PT/PTT/fibrinogen/D-dimer for DIC), blood cultures ×2, malaria testing, LP if meningismus (after blood cultures).

Meningococcal disease — same emergency protocol as the meningitis flow node
Blood cultures immediately, LP for CSF, don't delay antibiotics if LP is delayed. Empiric ceftriaxone or cefotaxime 2 g IV q12h (identical dosing to the meningitis pathway — see that node). Droplet precautions, notify public health (contacts need prophylaxis).
Viral hemorrhagic fever (rare)
Immediate isolation (contact + droplet precautions). Notify infection control, public health, and CDC. Supportive care only — no specific treatment. High mortality.
Measles (folded leaf: ti-measles)2026 outbreak, airborne isolation, vitamin A

2026 alert: 1,671 confirmed US cases (highest since elimination in 2000), mostly unvaccinated US residents infected during international travel. 3 C's: cough, coryza, conjunctivitis. Koplik spots (pathognomonic, appear 2-3 days before rash). Morbilliform rash starting at the face/hairline.

Airborne precautions immediately
AIIR room (negative pressure), N95 respirator for all staff, duration 4 days after rash onset. Measles PCR + IgM serology. Notify public health immediately for contact tracing.

5 · Respiratory module

Respiratory distress + travel → airborne precautions immediately (AIIR room, N95). Rule out MERS, avian flu, TB, measles. Region-specific: Middle East (MERS-CoV, camel/healthcare contact), Asia (avian influenza H5N1/H7N9, TB), any region (COVID-19, influenza, pneumonia).

MERS-CoV: Arabian Peninsula travel, camel contact, healthcare exposure in the Middle East; rapidly progressive pneumonia, mortality 30-40%. Avian influenza (H5N1/H7N9): Asia travel, poultry/live-bird market exposure; rapidly progressive ARDS, mortality 40-60%. Testing: lower respiratory specimen + PCR for MERS; influenza PCR + subtyping for avian flu.

Oseltamivir
75 mg PO BID x 5 days
For avian influenza if started early. No specific antiviral for MERS-CoV — supportive care (oxygen, mechanical ventilation).
Tuberculosis (folded leaf: ti-tb)RIPE regimen, isolation criteria

High-risk travel: Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe, Latin America. Chronic cough >2-3 weeks, fever, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis.

Airborne isolation until 3 negative AFB sputum smears
Sputum AFB smear ×3 (early morning), NAAT (rapid), IGRA preferred over TST if BCG-vaccinated. Do NOT start empiric TB treatment in the ED unless critically ill, high suspicion, and ID consultation obtained. Standard regimen if treated: RIPE (rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol). See the full tuberculosis consult.

Most common cause of respiratory symptoms in travelers — don't assume an exotic diagnosis without evidence. Testing: chest X-ray, COVID-19 PCR, influenza PCR, sputum/blood cultures if severe. Standard CAP regimens (outpatient: amoxicillin-clavulanate + azithromycin; inpatient: ceftriaxone + azithromycin). PSI/CURB-65 for admission decision — same criteria as non-travelers.

Oseltamivir
75 mg PO BID x 5 days
If influenza PCR is positive.

6 · Neurologic module

Life-threatening causes: cerebral malaria (*P. falciparum*), meningococcal meningitis, rabies (exposure history critical), Japanese encephalitis, leptospirosis (aseptic meningitis). Clinical syndromes: meningitis/encephalitis, altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits.

High-risk travel: Africa meningitis belt (meningococcal), rural Asia (Japanese encephalitis). Emergency protocol: blood cultures ×2 immediately, LP for CSF (cell count, glucose, protein, Gram stain, culture, PCR); if LP delayed for imaging, start antibiotics after blood cultures — don't delay antibiotics waiting for LP.

Ceftriaxone
2 g IV q12h
Third-generation cephalosporin (cefotaxime is an equivalent alternative). Add vancomycin if resistant pneumococcus is a concern; add acyclovir if encephalitis features (altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits). Droplet precautions + notify public health for meningococcal disease.
Cerebral malaria — medical emergency
*P. falciparum* malaria with CNS involvement: altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits, high parasitemia (>5%). Diagnose with STAT thick/thin smears + RDT. Airway management (intubate if GCS <8), seizure control (benzodiazepines), monitor glucose (hypoglycemia common).
IV Artesunate
2.4 mg/kg IV at 0, 12, and 24 hours, then daily until PO tolerated
First-line — obtain via the CDC IND protocol, CDC Malaria Hotline 770-488-7788. Alternative if unavailable: IV quinidine 10 mg/kg load then 0.02 mg/kg/min infusion with cardiac monitoring, plus doxycycline or clindamycin.
—Artesunate dose per administration (2.4 mg/kg)

Critical point: rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic. Exposure: animal bite/scratch (dog, bat, monkey, cat), bat exposure even without a visible bite, mucous membrane contact with saliva. Wound care: thorough washing with soap & water x 15 minutes. Do NOT wait for animal observation or testing if the exposure is high-risk.

Human Rabies Immune Globulin (HRIG)
20 IU/kg
Infiltrate as much as anatomically feasible into and around the wound; inject remainder IM at a site distant from the vaccine. Give with the first vaccine dose only — previously unvaccinated patients.
—HRIG dose (20 IU/kg)
Rabies vaccine
1.0 mL IM on days 0, 3, 7, and 14
4-dose series, immunocompetent, deltoid only (never gluteal). Immunocompromised: add day 28 (5-dose series) and check rabies titer. Previously vaccinated: 2-dose series (days 0, 3) only, no HRIG.

8 · Disposition & consultation

Mandatory admission
Suspected/confirmed P. falciparum malaria
Meningococcal disease
Severe dengue (hemorrhage, shock, organ dysfunction)
Viral hemorrhagic fever (isolation unit)
Acute respiratory distress or hemodynamic instability
Altered mental status
Severe leptospirosis (renal/hepatic failure)
Enteric fever with complications
Severe dehydration requiring IV fluids
Low threshold for admission
Undiagnosed undifferentiated systemic febrile illness, high fevers with abnormal labs, hemorrhagic symptoms, thrombocytopenia, or any patient requiring observation during an expedited workup.
  • Hemodynamically stable, no evidence of severe disease
  • Malaria ruled out (or non-falciparum species, well-appearing)
  • Tolerating PO fluids/medications, reliable for follow-up
  • Infectious disease referral arranged, with follow-up within 24-48 hours, before discharge
  • Return precautions given: fever, bleeding, confusion, persistent vomiting, shortness of breath, inability to tolerate PO

Consultation requirements: infectious disease for all admitted patients; CDC Malaria Hotline if confirmed/suspected malaria; public health notification for measles, meningococcal disease, viral hemorrhagic fever, and cholera.

Resources: CDC Yellow Book (cdc.gov/yellow-book), CDC Malaria Hotline 770-488-7788 (business) / 770-488-7100 (after hours), CDC Travel Notices (wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/notices).

9 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls8 items
Do NOT delay isolation for respiratory symptoms, hemorrhage, or rash + fever while the workup is pending.
Do NOT wait for serology to start doxycycline when an eschar is present — clinical diagnosis, treat immediately.
Do NOT give antibiotics before blood cultures in suspected typhoid/enteric fever.
Do NOT give aspirin or NSAIDs for any dengue-suspicious maculopapular rash until dengue is excluded — hemorrhage risk.
Do NOT use fluoroquinolones empirically for typhoid — widespread resistance.
Do NOT give antimotility agents (loperamide) in dysentery or suspected STEC — increases HUS risk.
Do NOT delay antibiotics for a delayed LP in suspected meningitis — give antibiotics after blood cultures if imaging delays the LP.
Do NOT wait for animal observation or testing to start rabies PEP when the exposure is high-risk.

Petechiae / purpura (folded leaf: ti-petechiae)

Life-threatening causes: meningococcal disease, viral hemorrhagic fever (rare), severe dengue (hemorrhagic), severe *P. falciparum* malaria. Workup: CBC, coagulation studies (PT/PTT/fibrinogen/D-dimer for DIC), blood cultures ×2, malaria testing, LP if meningismus (after blood cultures).

Blood cultures immediately, LP for CSF, don't delay antibiotics if LP is delayed. Empiric ceftriaxone or cefotaxime 2 g IV q12h (identical dosing to the meningitis pathway — see that node). Droplet precautions, notify public health (contacts need prophylaxis).

Immediate isolation (contact + droplet precautions). Notify infection control, public health, and CDC. Supportive care only — no specific treatment. High mortality.

Measles (folded leaf: ti-measles)

2026 alert: 1,671 confirmed US cases (highest since elimination in 2000), mostly unvaccinated US residents infected during international travel. 3 C's: cough, coryza, conjunctivitis. Koplik spots (pathognomonic, appear 2-3 days before rash). Morbilliform rash starting at the face/hairline.

AIIR room (negative pressure), N95 respirator for all staff, duration 4 days after rash onset. Measles PCR + IgM serology. Notify public health immediately for contact tracing.

Age | Vitamin A dose

6-11 months | 100,000 IU × 2 doses (days 1 and 2)

≥12 months | 200,000 IU × 2 doses (days 1 and 2)

Unvaccinated contact PEP: MMR vaccine within 72 hours of exposure, OR immune globulin within 6 days if vaccine is contraindicated. See the full measles consult for complications and disposition detail.

5 · Respiratory module

Respiratory distress + travel → airborne precautions immediately (AIIR room, N95). Rule out MERS, avian flu, TB, measles. Region-specific: Middle East (MERS-CoV, camel/healthcare contact), Asia (avian influenza H5N1/H7N9, TB), any region (COVID-19, influenza, pneumonia).

MERS-CoV: Arabian Peninsula travel, camel contact, healthcare exposure in the Middle East; rapidly progressive pneumonia, mortality 30-40%. Avian influenza (H5N1/H7N9): Asia travel, poultry/live-bird market exposure; rapidly progressive ARDS, mortality 40-60%. Testing: lower respiratory specimen + PCR for MERS; influenza PCR + subtyping for avian flu.

Oseltamivir: 75 mg PO BID x 5 days — For avian influenza if started early. No specific antiviral for MERS-CoV — supportive care (oxygen, mechanical ventilation).

Tuberculosis (folded leaf: ti-tb)

High-risk travel: Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Eastern Europe, Latin America. Chronic cough >2-3 weeks, fever, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis.

Sputum AFB smear ×3 (early morning), NAAT (rapid), IGRA preferred over TST if BCG-vaccinated. Do NOT start empiric TB treatment in the ED unless critically ill, high suspicion, and ID consultation obtained. Standard regimen if treated: RIPE (rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol). See the full tuberculosis consult.

Most common cause of respiratory symptoms in travelers — don't assume an exotic diagnosis without evidence. Testing: chest X-ray, COVID-19 PCR, influenza PCR, sputum/blood cultures if severe. Standard CAP regimens (outpatient: amoxicillin-clavulanate + azithromycin; inpatient: ceftriaxone + azithromycin). PSI/CURB-65 for admission decision — same criteria as non-travelers.

Oseltamivir: 75 mg PO BID x 5 days — If influenza PCR is positive.

6 · Neurologic module

Life-threatening causes: cerebral malaria (*P. falciparum*), meningococcal meningitis, rabies (exposure history critical), Japanese encephalitis, leptospirosis (aseptic meningitis). Clinical syndromes: meningitis/encephalitis, altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits.

High-risk travel: Africa meningitis belt (meningococcal), rural Asia (Japanese encephalitis). Emergency protocol: blood cultures ×2 immediately, LP for CSF (cell count, glucose, protein, Gram stain, culture, PCR); if LP delayed for imaging, start antibiotics after blood cultures — don't delay antibiotics waiting for LP.

Ceftriaxone: 2 g IV q12h — Third-generation cephalosporin (cefotaxime is an equivalent alternative). Add vancomycin if resistant pneumococcus is a concern; add acyclovir if encephalitis features (altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits). Droplet precautions + notify public health for meningococcal disease.

*P. falciparum* malaria with CNS involvement: altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits, high parasitemia (>5%). Diagnose with STAT thick/thin smears + RDT. Airway management (intubate if GCS <8), seizure control (benzodiazepines), monitor glucose (hypoglycemia common).

IV Artesunate: 2.4 mg/kg IV at 0, 12, and 24 hours, then daily until PO tolerated — First-line — obtain via the CDC IND protocol, CDC Malaria Hotline 770-488-7788. Alternative if unavailable: IV quinidine 10 mg/kg load then 0.02 mg/kg/min infusion with cardiac monitoring, plus doxycycline or clindamycin.

Critical point: rabies is 100% fatal once symptomatic. Exposure: animal bite/scratch (dog, bat, monkey, cat), bat exposure even without a visible bite, mucous membrane contact with saliva. Wound care: thorough washing with soap & water x 15 minutes. Do NOT wait for animal observation or testing if the exposure is high-risk.

Human Rabies Immune Globulin (HRIG): 20 IU/kg — Infiltrate as much as anatomically feasible into and around the wound; inject remainder IM at a site distant from the vaccine. Give with the first vaccine dose only — previously unvaccinated patients.

Rabies vaccine: 1.0 mL IM on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 — 4-dose series, immunocompetent, deltoid only (never gluteal). Immunocompromised: add day 28 (5-dose series) and check rabies titer. Previously vaccinated: 2-dose series (days 0, 3) only, no HRIG.

7 · One-glance algorithm

Returned International Traveler — Isolate if resp/hemorrhage/rash. Travel history + incubation period. Determine presenting syndrome.

Fever: Malaria-Endemic Area? — Isolation precautions, incubation-period DDx, exam red flags (eschar, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, petechiae).

STAT Malaria Testing — Thick/thin smears + RDT, repeat q12-24h x3. CDC Malaria Hotline 770-488-7788. P. falciparum = medical emergency.

Classify Fever Syndrome — Thrombocytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, eschar, relative bradycardia, arthralgia patterns.

Dengue / Arboviral Syndrome — Breakbone fever, thrombocytopenia. No ASA/NSAIDs. Admit for severe thrombocytopenia, hemorrhage, shock.

Typhoid / Enteric Fever — Blood cultures before antibiotics. Ceftriaxone 2g IV daily first-line. No fluoroquinolones.

Rickettsial Infection — Eschar pathognomonic. Doxycycline 100mg PO/IV BID now — don't wait for serology. CFR 20-40% if delayed.

Leptospirosis — Freshwater/flood exposure. Biphasic illness. Doxycycline (mild) or penicillin G (severe/Weil's).

GI: Classify Diarrhea — Acute <2wk vs persistent >2wk vs dysentery vs cholera. Red flags: fever+blood, severe dehydration.

Acute Traveler's Diarrhea — Loperamide if no fever/blood. Azithromycin for moderate-severe. Metronidazole for Giardia.

Cholera / Severe Dehydration — Rice-water stools. 30 mL/kg bolus over 30min cornerstone. Doxycycline/azithromycin adjunctive.

Rash: Classify Pattern — Maculopapular, petechiae/purpura, eschar, vesicular. Measles needs airborne precautions.

Maculopapular Rash + Fever — Dengue/chikungunya/Zika/measles/rickettsial — cannot distinguish clinically. No ASA/NSAIDs.

Respiratory: Classify — Airborne precautions immediately if resp distress+travel. Rule out MERS, avian flu, TB, measles.

MERS / Avian Influenza — Arabian Peninsula/camel (MERS) or Asia/poultry (avian flu). Mortality 30-60%. Oseltamivir for avian flu.

Community-Acquired Pneumonia — Most common non-exotic cause. Standard CAP regimens. PSI/CURB-65 for disposition.

Neuro: Classify — Cerebral malaria, meningococcal meningitis, rabies, Japanese encephalitis — life-threatening causes first.

Meningitis / Encephalitis — Blood cx x2, LP, don't delay antibiotics. Ceftriaxone/cefotaxime 2g IV q12h ± vancomycin/acyclovir.

Cerebral Malaria — P. falciparum + CNS involvement. IV artesunate 2.4mg/kg 0/12/24h then daily. Medical emergency.

Rabies Exposure — 100% fatal if symptomatic. Wound care + HRIG 20 IU/kg + vaccine. Don't wait for animal observation.

8 · Disposition & consultation

Mandatory admission

Suspected/confirmed P. falciparum malaria

Meningococcal disease

Severe dengue (hemorrhage, shock, organ dysfunction)

Viral hemorrhagic fever (isolation unit)

Acute respiratory distress or hemodynamic instability

Altered mental status

Severe leptospirosis (renal/hepatic failure)

Enteric fever with complications

Severe dehydration requiring IV fluids

Undiagnosed undifferentiated systemic febrile illness, high fevers with abnormal labs, hemorrhagic symptoms, thrombocytopenia, or any patient requiring observation during an expedited workup.

  • Hemodynamically stable, no evidence of severe disease
  • Malaria ruled out (or non-falciparum species, well-appearing)
  • Tolerating PO fluids/medications, reliable for follow-up
  • Infectious disease referral arranged, with follow-up within 24-48 hours, before discharge
  • Return precautions given: fever, bleeding, confusion, persistent vomiting, shortness of breath, inability to tolerate PO

Consultation requirements: infectious disease for all admitted patients; CDC Malaria Hotline if confirmed/suspected malaria; public health notification for measles, meningococcal disease, viral hemorrhagic fever, and cholera.

Resources: CDC Yellow Book (cdc.gov/yellow-book), CDC Malaria Hotline 770-488-7788 (business) / 770-488-7100 (after hours), CDC Travel Notices (wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/notices).

9 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT delay isolation for respiratory symptoms, hemorrhage, or rash + fever while the workup is pending.

Do NOT wait for serology to start doxycycline when an eschar is present — clinical diagnosis, treat immediately.

Do NOT give antibiotics before blood cultures in suspected typhoid/enteric fever.

Do NOT give aspirin or NSAIDs for any dengue-suspicious maculopapular rash until dengue is excluded — hemorrhage risk.

Do NOT use fluoroquinolones empirically for typhoid — widespread resistance.

Do NOT give antimotility agents (loperamide) in dysentery or suspected STEC — increases HUS risk.

Do NOT delay antibiotics for a delayed LP in suspected meningitis — give antibiotics after blood cultures if imaging delays the LP.

Do NOT wait for animal observation or testing to start rabies PEP when the exposure is high-risk.
