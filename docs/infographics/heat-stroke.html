Heat Stroke: Cool First, Then Workup

Adults in the ED with suspected heat stroke — core temperature >40°C (104°F) plus CNS dysfunction. Excludes isolated heat exhaustion (normal mentation), pediatric weight-based nuances beyond the calculator, and patients with known contraindications to cold-water immersion.

⚠ Cool first, workup second. Core temp >40°C + altered mental status is a true emergency — mortality 10–50% without rapid cooling. Do NOT delay cooling for IV access, labs, imaging, or transport.[1]
⚠ Use rectal or esophageal thermometry. Oral, axillary, and temporal temperatures are UNRELIABLE in heat illness and underestimate core temperature.[1]
⚠ Antipyretics are useless and can harm. Acetaminophen and NSAIDs do not lower temperature here — the hypothalamic setpoint is normal — and may worsen hepatic injury and coagulopathy.[1]
⚠ Stop active cooling at 39°C (102.2°F) to prevent overshoot hypothermia (afterdrop); resume cooling if temperature rebounds >39°C.[1]
⚠ Avoid succinylcholine when rhabdomyolysis is suspected — hyperkalemia risk. Use rocuronium for RSI paralysis.[2]
⚠ Every heat stroke patient is admitted. Multi-organ dysfunction — hepatic failure, DIC, AKI — can be delayed 24–72 hours.[2]

Definition

Heat exhaustion (temp <40°C, normal mentation) is not heat stroke but can progress: move to a cool environment, remove excess clothing, oral rehydration (IV NS if not tolerating PO). Any new mental-status change → reclassify as heat stroke and cool.[2][3]

2 · Cool first — the only intervention that changes mortality

Cool first, transport second
Target: core temp <39°C (102.2°F) within 30 minutes. Mortality is near-zero if cooled within 30 min and >50% if cooling is delayed beyond 2 hours. Remove all clothing and start cooling while IV access and monitoring are established in parallel.[1][2]

Stop active cooling at 39°C (102.2°F): temperature drifts down further after cooling stops (afterdrop), and overcooling triggers shivering, rebound hyperthermia, and coagulopathy. Dry the patient, cover lightly, and recheck rectal temp every 5–15 min. Resume cooling if it rebounds >39°C.[1]

4 · Complications — multi-organ dysfunction

Heat stroke is a systemic inflammatory response — assume multi-organ involvement. Order CBC, CMP, LFTs, coags, lactate, CK, and UA with myoglobin. Repeat CK q6–12h if elevated.[1][2][3]

Rhabdomyolysis — common in exertional
CK thresholds: >1000 U/L = rhabdomyolysis, >10,000 U/L = high AKI risk, >40,000 U/L = very high risk for AKI/dialysis. Treat with aggressive IV fluids targeting urine output 200–300 mL/hr; urine alkalinization is controversial. Watch for compartment syndrome → emergent fasciotomy.

Check CK on every heat-stroke patient — rhabdomyolysis occurs in classic heat stroke too, just less often than exertional. Target urine output 200–300 mL/hr whenever CK >5000.[2]

DIC, hepatic failure & other organ injury4 systems
DIC — thermal endothelial injury + inflammation. Transfuse only for active bleeding: FFP if INR >2, platelets if <50,000, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen <100, TXA 1 g IV for significant hemorrhage. Do NOT transfuse prophylactically.[2]
Hepatic failure — can be delayed 24–72 hours; trend LFTs even if initially normal.[2]
AKI — from hypovolemia, myoglobin, and direct thermal injury; monitor Cr and potassium.[2]
ARDS & cardiac dysfunction — capillary leak, arrhythmias, myocardial injury.[2]
