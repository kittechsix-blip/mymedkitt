Dementia in the ED: Rule Out Delirium, Prove Function, Route Safely

Adults presenting to the ED with a new or worsening cognitive concern. Excludes pediatrics and known static/congenital cognitive disability without new decline. Does NOT model the medication/dosing/legal detail of acute agitation management — that content lives in the separate myMedKitt Dementia & Agitation consult and is out of scope here.

⚠ Acute, fluctuating, inattentive, or off-baseline cognitive change is delirium or medical illness until proven otherwise — do not diagnose dementia from one ED screen during an acute syndrome.
⚠ Exclude time-sensitive mimics before labeling dementia progression: Neurologic: stroke/TIA, ICH/subdural, seizure or nonconvulsive seizure, CNS infection, normal pressure hydrocephalus, rapidly progressive dementia/prion pattern, tumor. Metabolic/toxic: hypoglycemia, hyponatremia/hypernatremia, hypercalcemia, renal/hepatic failure, hypercarbia/hypoxia, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency, medication toxicity, alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal. Psychiatric/sensory/sleep: depression, grief, psychosis, severe anxiety, sleep apnea, hearing/vision loss, isolation. Rapidly progressive or atypical course (weeks to months, myoclonus, seizures, focal deficits, gait disorder, early parkinsonism, prominent hallucinations, personality/language change, young onset, cancer/immunosuppression, or systemic inflammatory features) needs urgent neurology and advanced evaluation.
⚠ Suspected Lewy body or Parkinson disease dementia carries marked antipsychotic sensitivity — avoid or use extreme caution with high-potency dopamine blockers (haloperidol, risperidone); use nonpharmacologic de-escalation first.
⚠ Agitation requires a medical trigger screen (pain, urinary retention, constipation, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, infection, medication toxicity/withdrawal, fall/head injury) and a least-restrictive safety plan before antipsychotics or restraint, unless immediate danger blocks essential care — then open the dedicated Dementia & Agitation pathway.
⚠ Antipsychotics carry an FDA boxed warning for increased mortality in older adults with dementia-related behavioral disturbances.
⚠ Capacity is decision-specific — a dementia diagnosis alone does not remove capacity. Unsafe incapacity needs a surrogate/POA and social work; suspected neglect, abuse, or exploitation needs Adult Protective Services involvement.
⚠ Do not discharge the diagnosis — discharge the actual safety situation. Unsafe home, no reliable supervision, recurrent falls, medication mismanagement, wandering/elopement risk, or caregiver failure require observation, admission, or protected placement.

1 · Triage: delirium or dementia?

Dementia is usually not diagnosed from a single ED visit. The ED role is to detect dangerous mimics, document the cognitive syndrome, obtain collateral, identify functional impairment, and route to safe follow-up or protected disposition.

  1. Is this delirium, hypoglycemia, stroke, infection, medication toxicity, intoxication, withdrawal, or another acute illness?
  2. If chronic/progressive, is there objective cognitive decline plus loss of independence?
  3. Is discharge safe today?

2 · What to do now

Set the patient state above — the single matching instruction appears below. Precedence: immediate danger > acute/fluctuating change > Lewy body pattern > unsafe disposition.

Immediate danger or essential care blocked — act now
Skip further de-escalation attempts and open the dedicated Dementia & Agitation pathway now for medication choice, Lewy body/Parkinson cautions, capacity/hold distinctions, and restraint documentation.
Acute / fluctuating change — work up delirium first
Check POC glucose, oxygenation, temperature, and vitals; review medications/toxins/withdrawal; assess pain, urinary retention, constipation, and dehydration; look for a focal neurologic deficit, fall/head strike, or seizure. Treat infection only when symptoms or systemic signs support it — do not label this dementia progression yet.
Lewy body / Parkinson pattern — antipsychotic caution
Fluctuating cognition, recurrent visual hallucinations, REM sleep behavior disorder, and parkinsonism mark this pattern. Avoid or use extreme caution with high-potency dopamine blockers — favor nonpharmacologic de-escalation and, if medication is unavoidable, the lowest-risk, lowest-dose option with close monitoring.
Unsafe disposition — protect the discharge
Identify a surrogate/medical POA/guardian if capacity is lacking, involve social work/case management, use least-restrictive elopement precautions, and hold for observation/admission or protected placement until a safe plan exists.
Stable, progressive pattern — proceed with the diagnostic frame
No acute trigger, no Lewy body caution, no disposition red flag active — build the differential, obtain collateral, and complete the workup below.

3 · Differential & diagnosis comfort

Place the differential before the label — dementia is a syndrome, not a single disease.

How to be comfortable diagnosing
You can be clinically comfortable saying suspected dementia / major neurocognitive disorder when all 6 elements are present: (1) acquired decline from a previous baseline, (2) a persistent or progressive course — not just hours to days, (3) objective cognitive abnormality on history, exam, screen, or prior records, (4) functional impact on IADLs or ADLs, (5) collateral support for the time course and baseline change, and (6) delirium, intoxication, medication toxicity, depression, sensory impairment, and major metabolic mimics reasonably excluded or identified as contributors. Use cautious language: "This pattern is concerning for dementia, but outpatient longitudinal assessment is needed to confirm type and stage."

4 · Collateral, screening, and workup

Collateral is required. ED cognitive screening can be confounded by delirium, hearing, language, education, fatigue, pain, and stress — ask family/caregiver/facility about baseline cognition, time course, ADLs/IADLs, safety (falls, wandering, driving, firearms, scams, missed dialysis/meds), and caregiver capacity. Objective cognitive decline without functional loss is closer to mild cognitive impairment; decline with loss of independence supports major neurocognitive disorder/dementia.

Use a screen to document, not to diagnose alone: Mini-Cog, MoCA/SLUMS, 4AT/CAM if delirium is possible, clock drawing, 3-word recall, orientation, attention, naming. Document hearing aids/glasses, interpreter needs, and delirium features. A normal brief screen does not rule out early dementia; an abnormal screen during delirium does not diagnose dementia.

Usually reasonable baseline workup: CBC, CMP with calcium/renal/hepatic function, glucose, TSH, vitamin B12, depression/sleep/substance screen when relevant, and a medication review for anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids, steroids, and dopamine blockers. Do not shotgun UA for chronic confusion alone — send UA/culture only with urinary symptoms, fever, sepsis physiology, flank/suprapubic pain, catheter complication, or systemic signs with no other source. Biomarker/amyloid-tau testing is specialty-directed and does not replace the clinical syndrome, function, collateral, and counseling.

ED CT/urgent imaging triggers: acute neurologic deficit, severe headache, seizure, head trauma/fall, anticoagulation, rapid decline, cancer/immunosuppression, fever/meningismus, papilledema, a new gait disorder or urinary symptoms concerning for NPH, or a young-onset/atypical/focal course. Outpatient MRI is generally preferred when stable — it better characterizes vascular burden, atrophy, mass/subdural, and structural mimics.

5 · Special patterns

Do not overcall subtype from one visit, but document clues that guide follow-up: Alzheimer (memory/learning predominant, gradual), vascular (stepwise, executive dysfunction, stroke history), Lewy body (fluctuation, hallucinations, RBD, parkinsonism, antipsychotic sensitivity), frontotemporal (early personality/behavior/language change), Parkinson disease dementia (motor disease established first), NPH (gait leads), rapidly progressive (weeks-to-months, myoclonus, ataxia, seizures, systemic inflammatory/cancer/immunosuppression context).

Lewy body / Parkinson dementia — high-risk medication note
Suspected Lewy body dementia or Parkinson disease dementia changes the agitation plan: fluctuating cognition and alertness, recurrent formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behavior disorder, parkinsonism, and marked sensitivity to antipsychotics. Avoid or use extreme caution with high-potency dopamine blockers such as haloperidol and risperidone — favor nonpharmacologic de-escalation first. All antipsychotics carry an FDA boxed warning for increased mortality in older adults with dementia-related behavioral disturbances (1.6-1.7×). Use the dedicated Dementia & Agitation pathway for the medication/legal detail.

Gait impairment that is early or dominant, especially with cognitive slowing and urinary urgency/incontinence, raises normal pressure hydrocephalus — the classic triad spans gait, cognition, urinary symptoms. Do not promise reversibility from the ED. Look for unsafe gait, falls, subdural/trauma, infection, and medication effects. Ventriculomegaly on CT or strong clinical suspicion routes to neurology/neurosurgery or outpatient NPH evaluation depending on acuity.

6 · Infection edge case

Confusion alone is not a UTI diagnosis
IDSA recommends observation and assessment for other causes rather than antibiotics for bacteriuria with delirium/confusion when there are no urinary symptoms or systemic infection signs. Treat infection when supported by fever, hypothermia, sepsis physiology, localizing symptoms/signs, respiratory findings, cellulitis/wound, abdominal source, meningismus, urinary symptoms, flank pain, catheter obstruction/trauma, or a convincing lab/imaging pattern. Avoid the automatic UA → positive culture → antibiotics reflex for chronic confusion, falls, foul-smelling urine, or baseline dementia behavior alone.

Treat the confirmed or strongly suspected source; correct dehydration, hypoxia, fever, pain, retention, and constipation; avoid deliriogenic medications when possible; reassess mental status after source control. Hold for admission when infection causes delirium, poor intake, falls, inability to take antibiotics, sepsis risk, or unsafe supervision.

7 · Agitation, capacity, and safety

Agitation is usually a symptom, not the diagnosis. First screen for immediate danger or blocked essential care, pain, urinary retention, constipation, hunger/thirst, sleep deprivation, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, infection, medication toxicity/withdrawal/intoxication, fall/head injury/subdural/seizure/stroke, and caregiver/environmental triggers. Use nonpharmacologic de-escalation first when safe: reduce stimulation, one calm speaker, glasses/hearing aids, a familiar person, food/water/toileting, pain control, a mobility aid, daylight/sleep protection.

Open the Dementia & Agitation consult
Open the dedicated agitation consult for medication choice, Lewy body/Parkinson cautions, capacity/hold distinctions, restraint documentation, and protected disposition when behavior creates immediate danger, blocks essential care, or raises legal/capacity questions. Dementia & Agitation

Look beyond the memory complaint — complications often drive ED disposition more than the cognitive score: falls, wandering, unsafe gait, getting lost; medication errors, missed dialysis/appointments, inability to manage insulin/anticoagulants; poor intake, dehydration, weight loss, dysphagia, aspiration; driving, stove/firearms, financial exploitation, scams; caregiver burnout, neglect, abuse, unsafe home, no supervision; agitation, hallucinations, sleep reversal, elopement risk; capacity, surrogate, goals of care, placement needs.

Capacity and safety
Capacity is decision-specific. A dementia diagnosis alone does not remove capacity. Assess the exact decision — refusal of workup, discharge, transfer, medications, placement, or procedure — against 4 elements: understand relevant information, appreciate personal consequences, reason about options, and communicate a stable choice. If capacity is lacking and the situation is unsafe, identify a surrogate/medical POA/guardian, involve social work/case management, use least-restrictive elopement precautions, and hold for admission/observation until a safe disposition exists. Suspected neglect, abuse, exploitation, or an unsafe dependent-adult situation needs social work and Adult Protective Services per local policy.

9 · Disposition

Do not discharge the diagnosis. Discharge the actual safety situation. ED discharge can be reasonable when there is no delirium or dangerous mimic requiring treatment, vitals/labs/exam do not suggest unstable medical illness, the patient can eat/ambulate/transfer/take meds safely with support, reliable supervision and follow-up exist, and there is no active wandering, elopement, abuse/neglect, or unsafe refusal.

Safe discharge plan
Name the working diagnosis (suspected dementia, mild cognitive impairment, delirium resolved, or cognitive concern needing evaluation); give a caregiver-facing safety plan (meds, meals, hydration, falls, wandering, stove/driving/firearms, sleep, follow-up); arrange PCP/geriatrics/neurology/neuropsychology; request outpatient MRI when indicated; review the medication list for anticholinergics/sedatives; give explicit return precautions (new confusion, fever, fall/head injury, focal deficit, inability to walk/eat/drink, unsafe behavior, caregiver inability).
Observation, admission, or protected placement
Use for delirium or medical illness superimposed on dementia; unsafe gait, recurrent falls, inability to transfer; medication mismanagement with high-risk drugs; poor intake, dehydration, aspiration risk; wandering/elopement risk; no reliable caregiver/supervision; suspected abuse, neglect, exploitation; or lack of capacity for safe discharge with no immediate safe surrogate plan. Consult case management, social work, PT/OT, pharmacy medication reconciliation, and geriatrics/neurology when available.

10 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls6 items
Do NOT diagnose dementia from one ED screen during an acute or fluctuating presentation — work up delirium and dangerous mimics first.
Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria as the cause of confusion without urinary symptoms, systemic infection signs, or a compelling source gap.
Do NOT use high-potency dopamine blockers (haloperidol, risperidone) casually in suspected Lewy body or Parkinson disease dementia — marked neuroleptic sensitivity, and antipsychotics carry an FDA boxed warning for mortality in this population.
Do NOT medicate or restrain for agitation before a medical trigger screen, unless immediate danger is blocking essential care.
Do NOT assume a dementia diagnosis removes capacity — capacity is decision-specific and must be assessed for the exact decision at hand.
Do NOT discharge the diagnosis — discharge the actual safety situation; unsafe home, no supervision, or caregiver failure need protected disposition.

Do NOT use high-potency dopamine blockers (haloperidol, risperidone) casually in suspected Lewy body or Parkinson disease dementia — marked neuroleptic sensitivity, and antipsychotics carry an FDA boxed warning for mortality in this population.

Do NOT medicate or restrain for agitation before a medical trigger screen, unless immediate danger is blocking essential care.

Do NOT assume a dementia diagnosis removes capacity — capacity is decision-specific and must be assessed for the exact decision at hand.

Do NOT discharge the diagnosis — discharge the actual safety situation; unsafe home, no supervision, or caregiver failure need protected disposition.
