Acute Agitation — ED Management

Adults in the ED with acute agitation of any etiology (delirium/medical, intoxication/withdrawal, primary psychiatric, undifferentiated). Elderly (>65) require reduced dosing throughout — toggle below. Immediate-danger, high-threat stimulant, ketamine-monitoring, refractory-rescue, oversedation, physical-restraint, and hyperactive-delirium content is safety-critical and always shown, never gated behind a toggle.

⚠ IMMEDIATE DANGER: activate security, prepare medications, brief verbal attempt only (10-15 seconds max) — if patient is actively attacking, proceed directly to chemical restraint, do not delay.
⚠ STIMULANT INTOXICATION is high-threat: aggressive cooling if hyperthermic + benzodiazepines when feasible; ketamine only for immediate violent threat, severe hyperthermia preventing care, or failed benzodiazepines. Avoid antipsychotics alone — can worsen hyperthermia.
⚠ KETAMINE (ACEP Level C, high-threat only) requires continuous cardiac monitoring, pulse oximetry + capnography, and airway equipment at bedside — laryngospasm 1-4%, respiratory depression 2-20%.
⚠ REFRACTORY AGITATION (>10 mg lorazepam/hr or >40 mg/4h) → consider phenobarbital loading or propofol infusion (requires intubation) → ICU admission.
⚠ OVERSEDATION (RASS -3 or lower, airway concern): airway first; flumazenil carries seizure risk with chronic benzodiazepine use; naloxone if opioid co-ingestion suspected; intubate if airway compromised.
⚠ NEVER PRONE RESTRAINT — highest mortality risk. Physical restraint is a bridge to chemical sedation only, never standalone treatment; supine with head elevated 30°, minimum 5 trained personnel, monitor q15 min minimum.
⚠ HYPERACTIVE DELIRIUM with hyperthermia (>40°C) = high mortality: aggressive cooling, rapid sedation, large-bore IV ×2, continuous monitoring, minimal physical restraint.

Assessment & safety

Assess whether the patient is an immediate danger to self, staff, or others: combative, threatening, attempting to leave AMA, or unable to cooperate with any care.

  1. Activate security — multiple trained personnel
  2. Prepare medications — draw up sedation agents
  3. Clear the area — remove other patients/visitors
  4. Brief verbal attempt (10-15 seconds max)
  5. If patient is actively attacking: proceed directly to chemical restraint — do not delay

Project BETA — 10 domains: respect personal space (2+ arm lengths); do not be provocative; establish verbal contact; be concise; identify wants and feelings; listen actively; agree or agree to disagree; set clear limits; offer choices; debrief. Time limit: 5-10 minutes. If not improving → chemical restraint.

  • Glucose — hypoglycemia mimics psych
  • O2 sat — hypoxia causes agitation
  • Temperature — fever = infection, hyperthermia = hyperactive delirium
  • Pupils — toxidrome clues
  • Diaphoresis — sympathomimetic, withdrawal
Cannot delay sedation
The organic screen CANNOT delay sedation if the patient is an immediate threat — obtain these during/after sedation if needed.

▶ What to do now

Age >65 — reduce all doses
Reduce ALL doses 30-50%. Preferred: haloperidol 2.5 mg IV/IM (less sedating, safer in delirium) — note drug-store's elderly regimen tops out at 0.5-2 mg IM or IV push (see conflict note below). Lorazepam max 1-2 mg (not 4 mg). Avoid benzodiazepines alone if possible (worsen delirium). Higher risk of falls, oversedation, respiratory depression, orthostatic hypotension.
Delirium / medical cause
Delirium is a medical emergency — find and treat the cause (infection, metabolic, hypoxia, medication toxicity, withdrawal, structural). Haloperidol preferred in elderly (less sedating). Avoid benzodiazepines alone — they worsen delirium (except in withdrawal).
Stimulant intoxication — high-threat
Aggressive cooling if hyperthermic (>38.5°C). Benzodiazepines when feasible — midazolam or lorazepam. Ketamine only if immediate violent threat, severe hyperthermia preventing care, or failed benzodiazepines. Avoid antipsychotics alone — can worsen hyperthermia. Monitor for rhabdomyolysis, arrhythmias, AKI.
Alcohol / benzo withdrawal
Benzodiazepines are first-line: lorazepam 2-4 mg IV/IM q5-15 min PRN, or diazepam 10-20 mg IV q5-10 min PRN (if no liver disease). Do NOT use antipsychotics alone — lowers seizure threshold. High-risk features: prior seizures/DTs, PAWSS ≥4 → consider phenobarbital loading. See the full Alcohol Withdrawal protocol.
Unknown intoxication
Safest single agent: lorazepam 2 mg IV/IM, repeat q5-10 min as needed; max 10-20 mg for severe agitation. Avoid antipsychotics if a stimulant is possible — can worsen hyperthermia.
Hallucinogen intoxication
Usually not violent — reassurance often works. Quiet, dark environment, calm verbal reassurance. Benzodiazepine if needed (lorazepam 1-2 mg). Avoid antipsychotics — can worsen a "bad trip." LSD effects last 8-12 hours.
Primary psychiatric
Preferred: droperidol + midazolam when rapid sedation is needed; ziprasidone + midazolam as an atypical combo option; haloperidol + lorazepam when local practice favors it; olanzapine IM alone for monotherapy. Do NOT combine IM olanzapine with parenteral benzodiazepines.
Undifferentiated agitation
ACEP Level B: droperidol + midazolam — fastest, most effective. Alternative: atypical antipsychotic + midazolam. Continue workup (labs, glucose, toxicology, imaging) in parallel.

Reassessment & rescue

Goal: calm but arousable, RASS 0 to -1. Reassess 15-20 min after sedation.

If antipsychotic alone was given → add a benzodiazepine (midazolam 5 mg or lorazepam 2 mg). If benzodiazepine alone was given → consider an antipsychotic (olanzapine 10 mg or ziprasidone 20 mg). If both were given → reassess for organic cause; consider ketamine if safety risk escalates.

Refractory agitation
Refractory agitation (>10 mg lorazepam/hr or >40 mg/4h) → consider phenobarbital loading, consider propofol infusion (requires intubation), ICU admission.
  1. Head positioning — chin lift, jaw thrust
  2. Suction if secretions present
  3. Supplemental O2 — nasal cannula or BVM
  4. Capnography — monitor for hypoventilation
  5. Flumazenil 0.2 mg IV, repeat q1 min to max 1 mg — ⚠️ seizure risk if chronic benzodiazepine use
  6. Naloxone if opioid co-ingestion suspected
  7. Intubation if airway compromise

Physical restraint & rhabdomyolysis

Bridge to chemical restraint only
Use physical restraint as a BRIDGE to chemical restraint — never as standalone treatment. NEVER prone position — highest mortality risk. Supine with head elevated 30°. 5-point restraint minimum (wrists, ankles, chest). Minimum 5 trained personnel. No tight clothing around the neck.
  • q15 min minimum monitoring (Joint Commission)
  • Check distal pulses, skin color
  • Reposition every 15-30 min
  • Document time of application
  • Discontinue restraints as soon as chemical sedation is effective

High risk in prolonged struggling against restraints, stimulant intoxication, hyperthermia, or severe agitation. Monitor for dark (cola-colored) urine, muscle tenderness, weakness. Labs: CK, BMP (K+), creatinine. Treatment: aggressive IV hydration (target UOP 200-300 mL/hr), monitor K+ (hyperkalemia risk), avoid restraints if possible, rapid sedation to stop struggling.

Hyperactive delirium

High mortality — recognize early
Recognition: disoriented + combative + altered, adrenergic overdrive (tachycardia, hypertension), hyperthermia (>40°C), often stimulant use. Immediate management: aggressive cooling (ice packs, cold fluids, evaporative); rapid sedation (benzodiazepines when feasible; ketamine 5 mg/kg IM if immediate safety threat or failed benzodiazepines); large-bore IV ×2; continuous monitoring; minimal physical restraint — prioritize chemical control, avoid prone. Complications: sudden cardiac arrest, rhabdomyolysis → AKI, extreme hyperthermia.

Workup & disposition

Once sedation is achieved: labs (CBC, BMP, glucose, lactate, troponin if tachycardia/hypertension, UA, toxicology, CK if rhabdomyolysis concern); CT head if trauma, focal neuro findings, headache/seizure, anticoagulation/immunosuppression, delirium features, age >45 with first psychiatric symptoms, or atypical presentation; CXR if hypoxia/aspiration concern; ECG (QTc if antipsychotics given). Assess for underlying medical cause, psychiatric history, and suicide/homicide risk once able to participate.
