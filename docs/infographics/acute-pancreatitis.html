algorithm

Acute Pancreatitis: Classify, Resuscitate Moderately, Find the Cause, Delay Necrosis Intervention

Adults ≥18 with acute pancreatitis in the ED or inpatient setting. Excludes pediatric pancreatitis (different fluid/insulin protocols), pregnancy-specific management, and chronic pancreatitis exacerbations — each needs a modified pathway.

⚠ Avoid aggressive fluid resuscitation. Moderate approach (3-4 L in first 24 hours) is preferred over aggressive crystalloid — overresuscitation (net positive beyond 3-4 L) is harmful, not protective.
⚠ Severe AP (persistent organ failure >48h) requires ICU admission — mortality ~17-30%. Avoid excessive crystalloid; use early norepinephrine for fluid-refractory hypotension; monitor for abdominal compartment syndrome (bladder pressure >20 mmHg with organ dysfunction).
⚠ Cholangitis with gallstone pancreatitis needs ERCP within 24 hours — do not delay for pancreatitis severity. Without cholangitis, do NOT perform routine or diagnostic ERCP — unnecessary risk.
⚠ Sterile necrosis gets NO prophylactic antibiotics and NO routine FNA — clinical findings plus imaging (gas on CT, procalcitonin) are sufficient to diagnose infection.
⚠ Infected pancreatic necrosis is a life-threatening emergency — but delay is beneficial. Start antibiotics with pancreatic penetration first, allow the collection to wall off, and avoid early surgery (higher mortality). Ideal intervention timing is >4 weeks from onset.
⚠ Deteriorating/septic infected necrosis needs urgent drainage — percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) is preferred even emergently, over surgery. Surgical consultation is essential for all infected necrosis; IR should be on standby for pseudoaneurysm hemorrhage.
⚠ Insulin for hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis requires concurrent dextrose to prevent hypoglycemia, and NPO status — dietary fat worsens hypertriglyceridemia; do not feed until TG is controlled.

1 · Diagnose & risk-stratify

Diagnosis: 2 of 3 criteria
Diagnosis requires 2 of 3 criteria: characteristic abdominal pain (epigastric, radiating to back, sudden onset), serum lipase (or amylase) 3× upper limit of normal, or imaging evidence on CT/MRI/ultrasound. Most cases are mild and self-limiting.[1][2]

Revised Atlanta Classification (2012) assesses organ failure via the Modified Marshall Score (respiratory PaO₂/FiO₂, renal creatinine, cardiovascular — fluid-responsive vs vasopressor-dependent). BISAP (within 24h): BUN >25 mg/dL, GCS <15, SIRS ≥2 criteria, age >60, pleural effusion.[3][4]

BISAP ≥3
BISAP ≥ 3 carries a 12-30% mortality risk — treat as a severity red flag.[4]

Overresuscitation is harmful
Lactated Ringer's is preferred over normal saline (reduces inflammation, avoids hyperchloremic acidosis). Target 3-4 L in the first 24 hours — avoid net positive beyond 3-4 L. This is a moderate approach, not the older aggressive-fluids dogma.[5][6]

Mild AP: opioids are safe and effective for pain — no evidence morphine worsens AP. Early oral feeding within 24-48h as tolerated, starting with a low-fat solid diet (no stepwise progression needed); do not wait for pain resolution or normalized enzymes.[5][6]

Moderate AP: goal-directed fluids titrated to UOP/BUN/Hct; consider early norepinephrine for fluid-refractory hypotension; avoid excessive crystalloid (raises abdominal compartment pressure). Abdominal compartment syndrome: measure bladder pressure if tense abdomen — >20 mmHg with organ dysfunction = ACS, may require decompressive laparotomy. Enteral nutrition is critical, start within 48 hours.[7][9]

3 · Find the cause

Most common causes: gallstones (40-70%), alcohol (25-35%), hypertriglyceridemia (TG >1,000 mg/dL), post-ERCP, drugs, trauma, autoimmune. All patients need a RUQ ultrasound, serum triglycerides, and LFTs (ALT >3× ULN highly suggestive of biliary etiology). Age >40 with unknown etiology — consider pancreatic tumor (EUS or MRCP).[1][2]

Other etiologies (post-ERCP, drug-induced, autoimmune, trauma, idiopathic)5 items

Post-ERCP: most common ERCP complication (3-5%), usually mild/self-limiting. NSAIDs and pancreatic duct stents reduce risk.[14]

Drug-induced: azathioprine, 6-MP, valproic acid, ACE inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists (controversial) — discontinue the offending agent.

Autoimmune: Type 1 (IgG4-related, older males), Type 2 (associated with IBD) — elevated IgG4, pancreatic enlargement on imaging, responds dramatically to steroids.[15]

Trauma: blunt abdominal trauma → pancreatic duct injury — MRCP to evaluate duct integrity.

Idiopathic: 10-25% remain without identified cause — consider EUS for occult microlithiasis, genetic testing in recurrent cases.

4 · Complications

Early (<4 weeks): acute peripancreatic fluid collection (APFC), acute necrotic collection (ANC). Late (>4 weeks): pseudocyst (walled fluid, no solid debris), walled-off necrosis (WON). Image only if: no improvement at 48-72 hours, clinical deterioration, or suspected infected necrosis — do NOT routinely image early in mild AP.[3][16]

Pseudocyst — most resolve without drainage
Encapsulated fluid collection, no solid debris, develops >4 weeks after onset. Most acute fluid collections resolve spontaneously — size alone is NOT an indication for drainage. Intervention only if symptomatic (pain, early satiety, nausea), gastric outlet or biliary obstruction, infected, or expanding despite conservative management. EUS-guided endoscopic drainage is preferred; percutaneous for poor surgical candidates; surgical (cystgastrostomy) rarely needed.[3][17]
Sterile necrosis — conservative, no prophylactic antibiotics
Necrosis of pancreatic/peripancreatic tissue. Sterile necrosis: NO prophylactic antibiotics (ACG/AGA), supportive care (aggressive enteral nutrition, pain control, serial imaging — CT in 7-10 days or with clinical change). Delay intervention until WON matures (>4 weeks, ideally 6+ weeks) — mature WON is safer to drain/debride. ~30% will develop infected necrosis, usually weeks 2-4.[18]

5 · Infected necrosis — step-up approach

Life-threatening emergency — delay is beneficial
Diagnosis: gas on CT (pathognomonic), fever/sepsis/rising WBC-PCT, or failure to improve; FNA not routinely recommended. Start antibiotics with pancreatic penetration — piperacillin-tazobactam (first-line), carbapenems (meropenem, imipenem), or fluoroquinolone + metronidazole — for a 2-4 weeks minimum course. Key principle: delay is beneficial — antibiotics alone may resolve infection, allow the collection to wall off, and avoid early surgery (higher mortality). Ideal intervention timing is >4 weeks from onset.[18][19][20]

First-line for infected necrosis. Drug-store regimen matched from its 'Sepsis empiric coverage' dosing entry — no pancreatitis-specific dosing sub-entry exists.
  1. Delay + antibiotics if stable — goal: allow WON to mature (>4 weeks). 35% of patients improve without drainage.
  2. Percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) or endoscopic transluminal drainage (ETD) — 35% resolve with drainage alone.
  3. Minimally invasive necrosectomy — VARD, endoscopic necrosectomy, or sinus tract endoscopy through the PCD tract.
  4. Open necrosectomy — last resort when minimally invasive fails; higher morbidity, longer recovery.

Improving on antibiotics: continue current regimen — may transition to oral (ciprofloxacin + metronidazole, or amoxicillin-clavulanate) once tolerating PO. No antifungal prophylaxis unless documented fungal infection.[19]

Stable but not improving: optimal drainage timing is >4 weeks from onset — walled-off necrosis is easier to drain/debride with lower complications. PCD or EUS-guided endoscopic transluminal drainage (LAMS/plastic stents, preferred if the collection abuts stomach/duodenum). Consult GI and IR for multidisciplinary planning.[20][21]

Deteriorating/septic — urgent drainage
Indications: hemodynamic instability despite resuscitation, worsening multiorgan failure, abdominal compartment syndrome, massive hemorrhage from pseudoaneurysm. PCD preferred even urgently (less morbid than surgery); IR on standby for bleeding (angioembolization preferred over surgery for pseudoaneurysm — splenic or gastroduodenal arteries). If PCD not feasible or fails: VARD, then open necrosectomy as last resort. Surgical consultation is essential for all infected necrosis.[20][21]

6 · Disposition

Discharge criteria — mild AP
Pain controlled with oral medications, tolerating oral diet, hemodynamically stable, labs trending improved. Confirm etiology before discharge — gallstone: schedule cholecystectomy same admission or within 2 weeks; alcohol: cessation counseling + withdrawal precautions; HTG: lipid clinic referral + fibrate prescription. Follow-up PCP within 1-2 weeks; GI referral if recurrent or uncertain etiology.[1][2]
ICU vs step-down — complicated AP
ICU: persistent organ failure, infected necrosis requiring intervention, hemodynamic instability, respiratory failure. Step-down/floor: moderate AP with resolved transient organ failure, stable necrotizing pancreatitis on antibiotics, pseudocyst not requiring urgent intervention. Multidisciplinary team: GI (drainage, nutrition), IR (percutaneous drainage, embolization), surgery (necrosectomy if needed), nutrition. Long-term: exocrine/endocrine insufficiency, chronic pancreatitis if recurrent.[1][3]

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls7 items
Do NOT give aggressive fluid resuscitation (large-volume crystalloid) — target 3-4 L in the first 24 hours; overresuscitation is harmful, not protective.
Do NOT give prophylactic antibiotics for sterile necrotizing pancreatitis — ACG/AGA guidance is clear; it does not reduce mortality.
Do NOT perform early necrosectomy or drainage for infected necrosis if the patient is stable — delay >4 weeks for WON maturation lowers complications; early surgery raises mortality.
Do NOT perform routine diagnostic ERCP in gallstone pancreatitis without cholangitis — unnecessary risk.
Do NOT delay cholecystectomy in mild gallstone pancreatitis until a later admission — same-admission surgery reduces readmission for recurrent biliary events.
Do NOT routinely image (CT) early in mild acute pancreatitis — reserve imaging for no improvement at 48-72h, deterioration, or suspected infected necrosis.
Do NOT perform routine FNA to diagnose infected necrosis — clinical findings plus imaging (gas on CT, procalcitonin) are sufficient.

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT give aggressive fluid resuscitation** (large-volume crystalloid) — target 3-4 L in the first 24 hours; overresuscitation is harmful, not protective.

**Do NOT give prophylactic antibiotics** for sterile necrotizing pancreatitis — ACG/AGA guidance is clear; it does not reduce mortality.

**Do NOT perform early necrosectomy or drainage** for infected necrosis if the patient is stable — delay >4 weeks for WON maturation lowers complications; early surgery raises mortality.

**Do NOT perform routine diagnostic ERCP** in gallstone pancreatitis without cholangitis — unnecessary risk.

**Do NOT delay cholecystectomy** in mild gallstone pancreatitis until a later admission — same-admission surgery reduces readmission for recurrent biliary events.

**Do NOT routinely image (CT) early** in mild acute pancreatitis — reserve imaging for no improvement at 48-72h, deterioration, or suspected infected necrosis.

**Do NOT perform routine FNA** to diagnose infected necrosis — clinical findings plus imaging (gas on CT, procalcitonin) are sufficient.
