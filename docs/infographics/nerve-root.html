Nerve Root

Choose a root to compare its sensory territory, motor and reflex findings, and pain pattern. Adjacent roots overlap. Localize with the history and exam together.

Darker green marks more frequent patterns within the cervical or lumbosacral region. Gray indicates an unranked level. Color describes frequency only.

Cervical

C1Upper cervical motor rootUnranked
  • Dermatome. No consistent cutaneous territory.
  • Physical exam. There is no dependable isolated bedside motor or reflex test.
  • Pain syndrome. Upper neck discomfort has no distinctive C1 radiation pattern.

At the bedside

A patient localizes an ache to the skull base but has normal scalp sensation and limb strength. Those findings alone cannot distinguish C1 involvement from joint or muscular pain.

C2OcciputUnranked
  • Dermatome. Posterior scalp up toward the vertex.
  • Physical exam. Compare scalp sensation side to side. Limb reflexes can be normal.
  • Pain syndrome. Upper neck pain may shoot up the back of the head.

At the bedside

A patient traces the pain from one side of the upper neck into the occiput. Altered sensation over that scalp supports upper cervical involvement. Occipital neuralgia can feel similar. Sudden maximal headache warrants urgent assessment.

C3Upper lateral neckUnranked
  • Dermatome. Upper lateral neck, sometimes extending behind the ear.
  • Physical exam. Compare sensation below each ear and along the neck. There is no isolated limb reflex.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral upper neck or retroauricular pain.

At the bedside

Turning the head may reproduce pain behind the ear and along the side of the neck. A patch of altered neck sensation helps localize the complaint, although C2 and C3 territories overlap.

C4Shoulder capeUnranked
  • Dermatome. Lower neck and top of the shoulder near the acromioclavicular joint.
  • Physical exam. Compare sensation across the shoulder tops. Arm power and tendon reflexes may be preserved.
  • Pain syndrome. Pain from the lower neck into the trapezius or clavicular region.

At the bedside

The patient points to the ridge of the shoulder. Neck movement may reproduce the pain while glenohumeral motion remains comfortable. A sensory difference over the shoulder cape supports C4 involvement.

C5Lateral upper armLess common
  • Dermatome. Lateral shoulder and proximal upper arm over the deltoid.
  • Physical exam. Test shoulder abduction and elbow flexion. The biceps reflex may decrease.
  • Pain syndrome. Neck or scapular pain spreading into the shoulder and lateral upper arm.

At the bedside

Lifting an object onto a shelf becomes difficult. Deltoid weakness accompanied by weak elbow flexion or an asymmetric biceps reflex favors a root lesion over an isolated rotator cuff injury.

C6Radial forearm and thumbCommon
  • Dermatome. Radial forearm and thumb, sometimes the index finger.
  • Physical exam. Compare wrist extension, elbow flexion and the brachioradialis reflex. The biceps reflex may also decrease.
  • Pain syndrome. Neck or scapular pain tracking down the radial forearm toward the thumb.

At the bedside

Thumb tingling brings the patient in, but the pain began at the neck. Weak wrist extension and a quieter brachioradialis reflex support C6 involvement. A C5–6 foraminal disc lesion can compress this exiting root.

C7Triceps and middle fingerMost frequent
  • Dermatome. Middle finger, often with posterior forearm symptoms.
  • Physical exam. Test resisted elbow extension, wrist flexion and finger extension. Compare triceps reflexes.
  • Pain syndrome. Scapular pain extending down the posterior arm toward the middle finger.

At the bedside

Pushing a heavy door feels weaker on the painful side. Reduced triceps strength and reflex, together with middle-finger paresthesia, fit a C7 pattern. The usual foraminal disc level is C6–7.

C8Finger flexors and little fingerLess common
  • Dermatome. Little finger and ulnar hand.
  • Physical exam. Test middle-finger tip flexion against resistance. No dependable routine tendon reflex.
  • Pain syndrome. Pain may descend the medial arm into the ulnar fingers.

At the bedside

A patient drops a bag as the fingers lose their grip. Little-finger numbness can suggest ulnar entrapment, but weakness of middle-finger tip flexion extends beyond the ulnar nerve. C8 exits at C7–T1.

Thoracic

T1Intrinsic hand musclesUnranked
  • Dermatome. Medial forearm toward the medial elbow.
  • Physical exam. Resist finger abduction and compare thumb abduction. There is no reliable routine tendon reflex.
  • Pain syndrome. Aching or paresthesia may track along the medial arm and forearm.

At the bedside

The patient struggles to spread the fingers or hold a key. Weakness across median- and ulnar-innervated hand muscles with medial forearm sensory loss raises a root or lower-trunk plexus localization.

T2AxillaUnranked
  • Dermatome. Axillary apex and adjacent upper medial arm.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the axilla.

At the bedside

A patient describes a burning track from the upper back into the axilla. Side-to-side sensory change at the axillary apex can support T2 involvement. Examine the axilla, skin and chest for local causes.

T3Upper chestUnranked
  • Dermatome. Third intercostal space near the midclavicular line.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the upper chest.

At the bedside

The patient traces a stripe from between the shoulder blades across one side of the upper chest. Clothing may feel unusually sharp along the same strip. Consider urgent chest causes before attributing pain to a root.

T4Fourth intercostal spaceUnranked
  • Dermatome. Anterior chest at approximately the fourth intercostal space.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the fourth intercostal space.

At the bedside

A patient can follow the pain around one rib level to the anterior chest. Patchy allodynia along that band supports a segmental process. Its distribution cannot exclude a cardiac or pulmonary cause.

T5Lower anterior chestUnranked
  • Dermatome. Fifth intercostal space near the midclavicular line.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the lower anterior chest.

At the bedside

A narrow painful area extends from the mid-back around the lower chest. Compare pin sensation one interspace above and below and inspect for an evolving zoster eruption.

T6Xiphoid levelUnranked
  • Dermatome. Chest wall near the level of the xiphoid.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the xiphoid level.

At the bedside

Burning pain wraps from the back toward the low sternum. Light touch over the symptomatic skin may reproduce discomfort. Assess chest and upper abdominal causes according to the presentation.

T7Upper epigastric bandUnranked
  • Dermatome. Upper abdomen just below the xiphoid level.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the upper epigastric band.

At the bedside

The patient describes superficial burning across one side of the epigastrium. A sensory difference extending around to the back supports a thoracic source after evaluation for visceral disease.

T8Mid-epigastric bandUnranked
  • Dermatome. About halfway between xiphoid and umbilicus.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the mid-epigastric band.

At the bedside

A patient traces flank discomfort forward across the upper abdomen. Gentle touch may feel different across the same strip. Compare neighboring segments because symptoms can span adjacent roots.

T9Supraumbilical bandUnranked
  • Dermatome. Upper abdomen just above the umbilical level.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the supraumbilical band.

At the bedside

Pain arcs from one flank toward the area above the navel. The patient may notice that a shirt brushing that band stings. Examine the skin and abdomen before assigning a spinal cause.

T10Umbilical levelUnranked
  • Dermatome. Abdominal wall at the level of the umbilicus.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the umbilical level.

At the bedside

The patient traces a burning line from the back to one side of the navel. A side-to-side sensory difference supports segmental involvement. Deep or diffuse abdominal tenderness calls for a broader assessment.

T11Infraumbilical bandUnranked
  • Dermatome. Lower abdomen between the umbilical and inguinal levels.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the infraumbilical band.

At the bedside

A patient reports sensitivity over a belt-like strip below the navel. Compare both sides and examine the abdominal wall for focal weakness. Limb weakness or a sensory level raises concern for cord disease.

T12Suprapubic and inguinal regionUnranked
  • Dermatome. Lowest abdominal wall near the midpoint of the inguinal ligament.
  • Physical exam. Compare touch or pin sensation along the band and across the midline. No routine limb myotome or tendon reflex isolates this root.
  • Pain syndrome. Unilateral burning or shooting pain wrapping from the back toward the suprapubic and inguinal region.

At the bedside

Pain curves from the low flank toward the groin. A matching strip of hypersensitive skin supports a segmental source, but the hip, abdominal wall and pelvic organs can produce overlapping symptoms.

Lumbar

L1Groin creaseUnranked
  • Dermatome. Inguinal crease and adjacent proximal anterior thigh.
  • Physical exam. Compare groin sensation and hip motion. No reliable isolated limb strength or reflex test.
  • Pain syndrome. Low back or flank pain radiating into the groin.

At the bedside

The patient describes groin pain with a small area of numbness near the crease. Reproduction by passive hip rotation favors a hip source. Consider abdominal and pelvic causes as well.

L2Proximal anterior thighUnranked
  • Dermatome. Anterior proximal to mid-thigh.
  • Physical exam. Compare resisted hip flexion. L1–L3 share this movement. No reliable isolated tendon reflex.
  • Pain syndrome. Pain from the back or groin into the anterior thigh.

At the bedside

Lifting the thigh into a car feels difficult. Reproducible hip-flexion weakness with anterior thigh sensory change supports an upper lumbar process. Pain-limited effort can mimic weakness.

L3Anterior thigh and medial kneeUnranked
  • Dermatome. Distal anterior thigh toward the medial knee.
  • Physical exam. Test knee extension and hip adduction. The patellar reflex may be reduced.
  • Pain syndrome. Anterior thigh pain radiating toward the medial knee.

At the bedside

The knee buckles on stairs despite a relatively quiet knee exam. Quadriceps weakness plus weak hip adduction suggests a more proximal lesion than isolated femoral neuropathy.

L4Medial shin and ankleUnranked
  • Dermatome. Medial lower leg toward the medial malleolus.
  • Physical exam. Compare knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion and patellar reflexes.
  • Pain syndrome. Pain may cross the anterior knee and descend the medial shin.

At the bedside

A patient struggles to stand from a low chair and reports altered sensation inside the ankle. An asymmetric knee jerk and weak quadriceps support L4 involvement. Check dorsiflexion for weakness beyond the femoral distribution.

L5Great toe and dorsum of footMost frequent
  • Dermatome. Dorsum of foot toward the great toe.
  • Physical exam. Test great-toe extension, ankle dorsiflexion, inversion and hip abduction. No reliable routine tendon reflex.
  • Pain syndrome. Buttock or lateral leg pain tracking onto the dorsum of the foot.

At the bedside

The foot slaps the floor and the great toe gives way against resistance. Weak inversion or hip abduction supports L5 involvement over an isolated common fibular neuropathy. A paracentral L4–5 disc can compress L5.

Sacral

S1Posterior calf and lateral footMost frequent
  • Dermatome. Lateral border of the foot, sometimes extending onto the sole.
  • Physical exam. Compare Achilles reflexes and repeated supported single-leg heel raises.
  • Pain syndrome. Buttock pain radiating down the posterior calf into the lateral foot.

At the bedside

The patient loses push-off while walking. On the affected side the heel rises less with repetition and the ankle jerk may be quieter. A paracentral L5–S1 disc can affect this root.

S2Posterior thigh and kneeUnranked
  • Dermatome. Posterior thigh, especially the popliteal fossa.
  • Physical exam. Compare sensation behind the knees and resisted knee flexion, which shares L5–S2 input.
  • Pain syndrome. Pain may extend from the buttock into the posterior thigh.

At the bedside

A patient describes burning behind the thigh with altered sensation at the knee crease. Hamstring weakness alone gives limited localization. Ask about saddle sensation and bladder or bowel change.

S3Ischial and saddle regionUnranked
  • Dermatome. Lower buttock around the ischial tuberosity and adjacent saddle region.
  • Physical exam. With consent, compare saddle sensation. No routine limb strength or reflex test isolates S3.
  • Pain syndrome. Deep buttock or perineal discomfort may accompany sensory change.

At the bedside

A patient notices a numb patch where the buttock meets the seat. Clarify whether wiping or voiding feels different. New saddle sensory loss or bladder or bowel dysfunction warrants emergency assessment for cauda equina or conus compression.

S4Perianal sensationUnranked
  • Dermatome. Perianal skin, shared with S5.
  • Physical exam. With consent, assess perianal sensation. Pelvic floor and sphincter findings reflect several sacral roots.
  • Pain syndrome. Perineal burning or numbness can occur with little leg pain.

At the bedside

A patient says that wiping feels different despite preserved leg strength. This finding cannot reliably distinguish S4 from S5. New saddle sensory loss or bladder or bowel dysfunction warrants emergency assessment for cauda equina or conus compression.

S5Perianal sensory changeUnranked
  • Dermatome. Skin immediately adjacent to the anus, assessed clinically with S4.
  • Physical exam. Compare perianal sensation when indicated and with consent. Preserved rectal tone cannot exclude compression.
  • Pain syndrome. Perianal dysesthesia or loss of sensation may dominate the presentation.

At the bedside

A patient first notices numbness during toileting. Ask directly about urinary sensation, emptying and bowel control because the patient may not volunteer a change. New saddle sensory loss or bladder or bowel dysfunction warrants emergency assessment for cauda equina or conus compression.

Coccygeal

Co1Coccygeal regionUnranked
  • Dermatome. Small variable area near the coccyx supplied through the coccygeal plexus.
  • Physical exam. Inspect and palpate locally. No isolated limb myotome, tendon reflex or reliable cutaneous test.
  • Pain syndrome. Focal tailbone pain is nonspecific. An isolated Co1 pain syndrome is poorly defined.

At the bedside

Sitting on a hard chair brings on focal coccygeal pain. Point tenderness alone cannot establish a root lesion. Examine for local causes and ask about new saddle or sphincter symptoms.

Sources and frequency guide

C7 ranked first and C6 second in a population-based cervical cohort. C5 and C8 are less frequent. L5 and S1 are the predominant lumbosacral patterns. The remaining roots are unranked here. Individual probabilities depend on the population and cause.
