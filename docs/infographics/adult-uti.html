Adult UTI: Classify, Treat, Dispose

Adults ≥18 in the ED with suspected urinary tract infection — cystitis, pyelonephritis, and complicated UTI (male, catheter, structural, diabetic, immunocompromised, recurrent/resistant). Includes pregnancy and elderly-specific guidance. Excludes pediatric UTI (see myMedKitt pediatric UTI pathway) and prostatitis dosing beyond duration.

⚠ Pregnancy: TREAT asymptomatic bacteriuria. Untreated ASB in pregnancy progresses to pyelonephritis in 25-40% of cases, risking preterm labor and low birth weight — screen and treat at the first prenatal visit. Avoid TMP-SMX (1st-trimester neural tube defects; 3rd-trimester kernicterus) and fluoroquinolones (cartilage damage) in pregnancy. ADMIT ALL pregnant patients with pyelonephritis for IV antibiotics and OB consultation.
⚠ Urosepsis with a suspected resistant organism: empiric vancomycin 15-20 mg/kg IV q8-12h PLUS meropenem 1 g IV q8h (2 g IV q8h extended infusion for critical illness); add tobramycin or switch to cefepime + vancomycin if Pseudomonas/catheter risk. Run the full sepsis bundle: lactate, blood cultures ×2, antibiotics within 1 hour, 30 mL/kg crystalloid, vasopressors if hypotensive despite fluids.
⚠ Do NOT use nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis — it does not achieve adequate renal tissue levels.
⚠ Fluoroquinolones carry an FDA black-box warning (tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, CNS effects) — reserve for complicated UTI/pyelonephritis, not uncomplicated cystitis.
⚠ Elderly: asymptomatic bacteriuria is common (15-50% in community-dwelling adults, up to 100% in catheterized patients) and should NOT be treated — foul-smelling or cloudy urine alone, and confusion alone, are NOT UTI.
⚠ Catheter-associated UTI: a positive culture alone is NOT CAUTI — it is always positive in catheterized patients. Remove or change the catheter before treating.
⚠ ESBL-producing organisms: do NOT use piperacillin-tazobactam — associated with 12% vs 4% 30-day mortality compared to meropenem in bloodstream infections.
⚠ Pyuria absence (negative leukocyte esterase AND negative nitrite) has >95% negative predictive value against UTI — reconsider the diagnosis before treating empirically.

1 · Recognize & classify

Classic symptoms: dysuria, frequency, urgency ± suprapubic pain/hematuria (cystitis); fever ≥38°C, flank pain, CVA tenderness ± nausea/vomiting (pyelonephritis).

In women, dysuria + frequency without vaginal discharge carries a 90% probability of UTI; any single symptom alone is closer to 50%, and vaginal discharge markedly lowers it.

Negative LE AND negative nitrite → UTI unlikely (negative predictive value >95%). Pyuria is defined as ≥10 WBC/hpf on microscopy and is the single most sensitive UA finding — its absence argues strongly against UTI.

E. coli causes 75-95% of uncomplicated UTI; Staph saprophyticus 5-10%; Klebsiella/Proteus 5-10%; Enterococcus/Pseudomonas more common in complicated UTI.

5 · Pyelonephritis management

Outpatient criteria (ALL required): tolerating PO, no significant comorbidity, no sepsis, not pregnant, reliable follow-up, home support.

Regimens: ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO BID × 7 days (preferred), or levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily × 5 days, or TMP-SMX 1 DS tab BID × 14 days with a ceftriaxone 1 g IM ×1 bridge dose while awaiting susceptibilities. Anti-emetic as needed; ensure culture sent. Follow-up 24-48h.

Admission criteria: unable to tolerate PO, sepsis/hemodynamic instability, pregnancy, complicated features (obstruction, abscess), significant comorbidity, no reliable follow-up, failed outpatient therapy.

IV options: ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h or levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily; ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily; gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV/IM ×1 + ampicillin 2 g IV q4h for Enterococcus coverage/FQ allergy; ertapenem or meropenem for severe/MDR. Step-down to PO once afebrile 24-48h, tolerating PO, and improving — complete 10-14 days total.

6 · Special populations (pregnancy, elderly, ASB)

Pregnancy makes ALL UTIs complicated. TREAT asymptomatic bacteriuria — untreated ASB progresses to pyelonephritis in 25-40% of cases (preterm labor, low birth weight risk). Screen and treat at the first prenatal visit. AVOID TMP-SMX (1st-trimester neural tube defects; 3rd-trimester kernicterus), fluoroquinolones (cartilage damage), and tetracyclines. Duration: cystitis 7 days, pyelonephritis 10-14 days.

Elderly — most over-diagnosed condition in the ED. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common: 15-50% prevalence in community-dwelling adults, 25-50% in nursing-home residents, up to 100% in catheterized patients. Foul-smelling or cloudy urine alone is NOT UTI; confusion alone is NOT UTI — UTI-driven delirium is over-diagnosed and should be a diagnosis of exclusion. True symptomatic UTI requires dysuria, new frequency/urgency, fever without other source, suprapubic pain, gross hematuria, or new/worsening incontinence.
Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB): significant bacterial growth without urinary symptoms. Treat ONLY in pregnancy or before mucosa-disrupting urologic procedures. Do NOT treat in elderly, diabetics, catheterized patients, or spinal cord injury. "Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in this patient. No reduction in outcomes, increases resistance and adverse effects."
After treatment (pregnancy): obtain a test-of-cure culture and screen for recurrence. For recurrent UTI in pregnancy, consider suppressive therapy — nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO daily or cephalexin 250 mg PO daily. Red flags requiring immediate OB involvement: preterm contractions, fever unresponsive to antibiotics, sepsis, or renal function changes.

4 · First-line antibiotic selection (uncomplicated cystitis)

Nitrofurantoin (Macrobid): 100 mg PO BID × 5 days PO — Avoid if CrCl <30 mL/min or pyelonephritis (does not achieve adequate renal tissue levels). <5% E. coli resistance locally.

TMP-SMX (Bactrim) DS: 1 DS tab (160/800 mg) PO BID × 3 days PO — Use only if local E. coli resistance <20%. Avoid in pregnancy (1st trimester), sulfa allergy, G6PD deficiency.

Fosfomycin (Monurol): 3 g PO single dose PO — Slightly lower cure rate than 5-day nitrofurantoin (75-80% vs 85-90%); good for adherence concerns.

Drug | Uncomplicated cystitis | Pyelonephritis / complicated

Ciprofloxacin | 250 mg BID × 3 days | 500 mg BID × 7 days

Levofloxacin | 250 mg daily × 3 days | 750 mg daily × 5 days

Reserve fluoroquinolones for complicated UTI/pyelonephritis. FDA black-box: tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, CNS effects. Avoid for uncomplicated cystitis unless no alternative exists.

5 · Pyelonephritis management

Outpatient criteria (ALL required): tolerating PO, no significant comorbidity, no sepsis, not pregnant, reliable follow-up, home support.

Regimens: ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO BID × 7 days (preferred), or levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily × 5 days, or TMP-SMX 1 DS tab BID × 14 days with a ceftriaxone 1 g IM ×1 bridge dose while awaiting susceptibilities. Anti-emetic as needed; ensure culture sent. Follow-up 24-48h.

Admission criteria: unable to tolerate PO, sepsis/hemodynamic instability, pregnancy, complicated features (obstruction, abscess), significant comorbidity, no reliable follow-up, failed outpatient therapy.

IV options: ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV q12h or levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily; ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily; gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV/IM ×1 + ampicillin 2 g IV q4h for Enterococcus coverage/FQ allergy; ertapenem or meropenem for severe/MDR. Step-down to PO once afebrile 24-48h, tolerating PO, and improving — complete 10-14 days total.

Imaging red flags & step-down criteria

  • No clinical improvement in 48-72 hours
  • Diabetic patient (risk of emphysematous pyelonephritis)
  • Known kidney stones / suspected obstruction
  • History of urosepsis or suspected abscess
  • Afebrile 24-48 hours
  • Tolerating PO intake
  • Clinically improving
  • Then convert IV → PO and complete total course

Do NOT use nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis — it does not achieve adequate renal tissue levels.

6 · Special populations (pregnancy, elderly, ASB)

Pregnancy makes ALL UTIs complicated. TREAT asymptomatic bacteriuria — untreated ASB progresses to pyelonephritis in 25-40% of cases (preterm labor, low birth weight risk). Screen and treat at the first prenatal visit. AVOID TMP-SMX (1st-trimester neural tube defects; 3rd-trimester kernicterus), fluoroquinolones (cartilage damage), and tetracyclines. Duration: cystitis 7 days, pyelonephritis 10-14 days.

Nitrofurantoin (Macrobid) — pregnancy: 100 mg PO BID × 7 days PO — ACOG: appropriate in 1st trimester only when no suitable alternative is available; safe in 2nd/3rd trimester. Avoid at term (theoretical hemolysis risk).

Cephalexin — pregnancy: 500 mg PO QID × 7 days PO — Pregnancy-safe across all trimesters (IDSA 2011). First-line PO step-down after IV pyelonephritis.

Amoxicillin-Clavulanate — pregnancy: 500/125 mg PO BID × 5-7 days PO — Alternative when first-line agents (nitrofurantoin, cephalexin) contraindicated or resistance suspected. Pregnancy-safe. (The source consult text states TID; drug-store's canonical dosing record specifies BID — BID is used here; see brief.md source-conflict log.)

Pyelonephritis in pregnancy: ADMIT ALL cases for IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily), monitor for preterm labor, fetal monitoring, OB consultation.

Elderly — most over-diagnosed condition in the ED. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common: 15-50% prevalence in community-dwelling adults, 25-50% in nursing-home residents, up to 100% in catheterized patients. Foul-smelling or cloudy urine alone is NOT UTI; confusion alone is NOT UTI — UTI-driven delirium is over-diagnosed and should be a diagnosis of exclusion. True symptomatic UTI requires dysuria, new frequency/urgency, fever without other source, suprapubic pain, gross hematuria, or new/worsening incontinence.

Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB): significant bacterial growth without urinary symptoms. Treat ONLY in pregnancy or before mucosa-disrupting urologic procedures. Do NOT treat in elderly, diabetics, catheterized patients, or spinal cord injury. "Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in this patient. No reduction in outcomes, increases resistance and adverse effects."

After treatment (pregnancy): obtain a test-of-cure culture and screen for recurrence. For recurrent UTI in pregnancy, consider suppressive therapy — nitrofurantoin 100 mg PO daily or cephalexin 250 mg PO daily. Red flags requiring immediate OB involvement: preterm contractions, fever unresponsive to antibiotics, sepsis, or renal function changes.

7 · Resistant / MDR UTI

Suspect a resistant organism when: healthcare-facility residence, recent hospitalization (<90 days), prior antibiotic exposure (strongest predictor), indwelling catheter/stent, history of resistant organism (ESBL, MDR), multiple prior UTIs, recent urologic procedure, or immunosuppression.

Key organisms: ESBL-E (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase E. coli/Klebsiella), DTR-Pseudomonas, VRE, CRE. Select the suspected organism above to see targeted therapy.

Alternative: tobramycin (preferred over gentamicin for Pseudomonas — concentrates in renal tissue) or amikacin if tobramycin-resistant. Duration 7-10 days for pyelonephritis, longer if bacteremic.
