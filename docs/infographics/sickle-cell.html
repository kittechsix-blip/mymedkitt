Sickle Cell Disease in the ED: Seven Ways It Presents

Children and adults with known sickle cell disease (SCD) or sickle cell trait (SCT) presenting to the ED. A triage-and-treat map across the seven acute presentations. Excludes chronic outpatient management, bone marrow transplant candidacy, and definitive priapism/stroke procedures — each links to its own protocol.

⚠ Fever ≥ 38.5 °C (101.3 °F) in SCD is a medical emergency. Functional asplenia develops by 3 months of age — give ceftriaxone within 1 hour of arrival, after blood cultures. *S. pneumoniae* is the most common pathogen.
⚠ Acute chest syndrome (ACS) accounts for up to 25% of SCD deaths. A new infiltrate plus fever or respiratory symptoms defines it; the CXR may lag behind the clinical picture, so treat on suspicion.
⚠ Do NOT give corticosteroids for SCD pain or ACS — they trigger rebound vaso-occlusive pain and are associated with stroke.
⚠ Splenic sequestration can progress to shock within hours. Tachycardia is often the first sign. Transfuse in 5 mL/kg aliquots and do NOT exceed Hgb 8 g/dL — re-entry of sequestered cells causes fatal hyperviscosity.
⚠ Give supplemental O₂ only for SpO₂ < 90%. Excess oxygen in a non-hypoxic patient blunts the marrow's reticulocyte response. Incentive spirometry q2h — not oxygen — is what prevents ACS.

4 · Acute chest syndrome — treat on suspicion

Definition and why it kills
A new radiodensity on chest imaging with fever and/or respiratory symptoms. ACS causes up to 25% of SCD deaths; ~20% of cases need ICU and ~10% need mechanical ventilation. Asthma is both a comorbidity and a trigger.[1][13]

Cover typical + atypical organisms — ceftriaxone plus a macrolide:

Simple transfusion
Indicated if Hgb drops > 1 g/dL below baseline. Targets: Hgb ~10 g/dL, HbS < 30%. Do NOT exceed Hgb 11 g/dL (hyperviscosity).[4]
Exchange transfusion
For worsening hypoxia/infiltrates despite O₂, multilobar disease, or rapid deterioration. Contact blood bank EARLY — extended antigen matching (anti-C, E, Kell) and sickle-negative units take time.[4][14]
Disposition
Admit ALL patients with ACS. ICU for exchange transfusion, FiO₂ > 40% to hold SpO₂ ≥ 92%, worsening respiratory status, or multilobar disease. ACS peaks on days 3–4 of admission.[4]

5 · The time-critical trio + trait

Stroke — exchange transfusion within 2 hours
A child with SCD is 33× more likely to have an ischemic stroke (peak age 2–9 yr). Emergent CT to exclude hemorrhage, then MRI/MRA. ASH recommends transfusion within 2 hours of symptom onset, exchange over simple, targeting HbS < 30%. Simple transfusion only if Hgb < 8.5 g/dL; tPA is NOT recommended in children < 18 yr. Activate stroke alert and notify hematology immediately.[1][6]
Splenic sequestration — the Hgb 8 ceiling
Sickling traps RBCs in the spleen → acute splenomegaly, severe anemia, thrombocytopenia, and shock within hours; tachycardia is often the first sign. Establish two large-bore IVs. Transfuse pRBC in 5 mL/kg aliquots and do NOT transfuse past Hgb 8 g/dL — sequestered cells re-enter the circulation and over-transfusion causes fatal hyperviscosity.[4][15]
Priapism > 4 hours
Affects ~40% of males with SCD; intervene within 4 hours to prevent permanent dysfunction. IV hydration, analgesia per VOC, supplemental O₂, oral pseudoephedrine 30–60 mg. Emergent urology for aspiration, irrigation, and phenylephrine injection.[1]
Sickle cell trait (HbAS) — benign until stressedECAST · rhabdo · renal
ECAST — exertional collapse
SCT is not SCD, but under extreme exertion, dehydration, acidosis, hypoxia, or altitude it can sickle. Kark (1987) found a 28× increased risk of exercise-related death in recruits with SCT. Stop the activity, cool the patient, and give aggressive IV fluids targeting urine output 200–300 mL/hr; admit to ICU with serial CK and BMP for rhabdomyolysis and AKI.[16][17]
Renal — hematuria
Renal papillary necrosis is the most common cause of hematuria in SCT. Flank pain + hematuria warrants CT urography to exclude renal medullary carcinoma — rare but aggressive, with median survival ~15 weeks.[1][18]
