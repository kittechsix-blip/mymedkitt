Monocular Vision Loss: Sick-Check First, Then Route the Lane

Adults presenting with acute monocular vision loss or vision change, ED/urgent-care setting. This hub triages the presenting complaint into vascular/GCA/eye-pressure/trauma/retina/optic-nerve/infection lanes and hands off to the specific consult (CRAO, amaurosis fugax, AACG, ocular trauma, retinal detachment/POCUS, optic neuritis, orbital cellulitis) for detailed management — it is a router, not a replacement for those full pathways.

⚠ Sudden painless monocular vision loss is retinal ischemia or retinal detachment until proven otherwise — separate vascular, GCA, globe/pressure, retinal, optic-nerve, infection, and trauma lanes before reassuring anyone.
⚠ Pick the first dangerous lane that fits before assuming a benign cause: CRAO/BRAO or retinal ischemic stroke, amaurosis fugax/TMVL, giant cell arteritis, acute angle-closure glaucoma, open globe/ocular trauma, retinal detachment/vitreous hemorrhage, optic neuritis/optic neuropathy, and orbital/intraocular/invasive infection are all first-pass, time-critical exclusions.
⚠ Treat sudden painless persistent monocular loss as retinal stroke (CRAO/BRAO) until ophthalmology/stroke evaluation proves otherwise — do not be falsely reassured by a normal-looking ED fundus exam.
⚠ Transient monocular vision loss (amaurosis fugax) needs urgent TIA/stroke workup, not routine outpatient eye follow-up — complete recovery does not make it benign; retinal TIA can precede brain stroke, especially early after the event.
⚠ Age ≥50 with compatible symptoms (jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, new temporal headache, PMR, diplopia, fellow-eye symptoms) is giant cell arteritis until proven otherwise — start high-dose corticosteroids immediately when suspicion or vision symptoms are present; do not wait for biopsy or ultrasound.
⚠ Painful red eye with halos, vomiting, and a mid-dilated poorly reactive pupil is acute angle-closure glaucoma — check IOP if open globe is not suspected, start IOP-lowering therapy, call ophthalmology emergently; do not dilate a suspicious angle-closure eye.
⚠ Trauma, an irregular pupil, hyphema, or possible open globe: protect the globe first — no patch, no pressure on the eye, avoid ultrasound/tonometry if rupture is possible.
⚠ Orbital pain/swelling with ophthalmoplegia, proptosis, fever, or immunocompromise is not simple conjunctivitis — treat orbital/intraocular infection as sight- and sometimes life-threatening.
⚠ When no single lane fits, run the full default eye bundle before reassuring the patient — acuity, pupils/RAPD, fields, EOM, external exam, slit lamp/fluorescein, IOP if safe, fundus/photo, and targeted GCA labs if age ≥50 or GCA is a concern.
⚠ Testing must never delay time-critical treatment for the CRAO pathway, GCA, acute angle-closure glaucoma, open globe, or orbital infection.
⚠ When the eye, retina, optic nerve, or brain is actively threatened, disposition is emergent admission/transfer/specialty consult — not discharge with routine follow-up.

1 · Sick check first

5 do-not-miss: central retinal artery occlusion (sudden painless loss, a stroke-equivalent), giant cell arteritis (age ≥50, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, high ESR/CRP), retinal detachment (flashes/floaters/curtain), acute angle-closure glaucoma (painful red eye, mid-dilated pupil, high IOP), and optic neuritis/pituitary apoplexy (pain on eye movement + RAPD, or sudden loss + headache + ophthalmoplegia).[1][2][3]

First 60 seconds
Confirm monocular vs binocular (test each eye separately). Document last-known-normal and onset (sudden seconds/minutes vs progressive hours/days). Check visual acuity each eye, pupils/RAPD, visual fields by confrontation, EOM, external eye, fluorescein/slit lamp if available, IOP when open globe is not suspected. Look for pain, red eye, trauma, headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, neuro deficits, anticoagulants, diabetes, immunosuppression. Do not wait for a perfect fundus exam if stroke-like retinal ischemia is possible.[1][2][3]

CRAO / BRAO / retinal ischemic stroke
Open Central Retinal Artery Occlusion and Acute Ischemic Stroke. Next 5 minutes: stroke-time history, visual acuity/RAPD, bedside fundus/photo if available, neuro exam, ECG, glucose, ophthalmology and stroke team. If within a local thrombolysis pathway/window, involve stroke/ophthalmology immediately. Do not be falsely reassured by poor fundoscopy — early CRAO may be subtle and ED fundoscopy can miss findings.[1][2][6]
Transient monocular vision loss / amaurosis fugax
Open Amaurosis Fugax. Treat as retinal TIA: confirm monocular history, screen GCA if age ≥50, ECG/telemetry, vascular imaging of head/neck or carotids per local stroke pathway, brain MRI-DWI when available, secondary prevention after hemorrhage/contraindications considered. Pitfall: complete recovery does not make this benign — retinal TIA can precede brain stroke, especially early after the event.[2][7]
Giant cell arteritis threat
Age ≥50 plus compatible symptoms is vision-threatening disease. Order ESR, CRP, CBC/platelets; ask specifically about jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, new temporal headache, PMR symptoms, diplopia, and fellow-eye symptoms. If suspicion is high or vision symptoms are present, start high-dose corticosteroids immediately and involve ophthalmology/rheumatology — do not wait for biopsy or ultrasound. Open Amaurosis Fugax or CRAO if the presentation is retinal ischemic.[2][8]
Acute angle-closure glaucoma
Open Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma. Next 5 minutes: confirm IOP if open globe is not suspected, analgesia/antiemetic, start local protocol IOP-lowering therapy, ophthalmology emergently. Classic findings: painful red eye, halos, headache, nausea/vomiting, cloudy cornea, mid-dilated poorly reactive pupil. Pitfall: do not dilate a suspicious angle-closure eye before ophthalmology guidance.[3][9]
Open globe / ocular trauma / compartment
Open Ocular Trauma or Globe Rupture. Next 5 minutes: rigid shield, NPO, antiemetic/analgesia, tetanus/antibiotics per open-globe pathway, avoid pressure on the eye, avoid ultrasound/tonometry if open globe possible, CT orbit when stable. Proptosis + tight lids + RAPD + high IOP + acute vision loss after trauma/surgery raises orbital compartment syndrome and the lateral canthotomy pathway. Pitfall: no patch and no eye pressure for suspected open globe.[3][4]
Retinal detachment / vitreous hemorrhage
Open Ocular POCUS when ultrasound is safe. Clues: flashes, floaters, curtain/shadow, peripheral field loss, high myopia, prior eye surgery, trauma, diabetic retinopathy, anticoagulation. Do not ultrasound suspected open globe. Ophthalmology timing depends on macula status, detachment suspicion, and vision threat. Pitfall: a normal ED ultrasound does not clear small retinal tears or subtle macula-threatening disease.[3][4]
Optic neuritis / optic neuropathy
Open Multiple Sclerosis for the optic neuritis pathway. Clues: subacute unilateral loss, pain with eye movement, reduced color saturation, RAPD, young adult, prior demyelinating symptoms. Consider atypical features: severe bilateral loss, older age, no pain, optic disc hemorrhage, systemic symptoms, infection risk, NMO/MOG concern. Pitfall: do not give oral prednisone alone for typical optic neuritis — follow neuro-ophthalmology/neurology pathway.[3][10]
Orbital / intraocular / invasive infection
Open Orbital vs Preseptal Cellulitis. Danger signs: ophthalmoplegia, pain with EOM, proptosis, decreased vision, RAPD, fever, immunocompromise, recent eye procedure/injection, contact lens ulcer, black eschar, DKA risk. Next steps: ophthalmology, CT orbit/face with contrast when stable, IV antibiotics when orbital cellulitis/endophthalmitis/deep infection is plausible.[3][4]

5 · Disposition

Disposition is driven by diagnosis, visual acuity, progression, and follow-up reliability.[1][2][3][4]

Disposition tierCriteria

Admit / transfer / emergent specialty
Admit/transfer/emergent consult for CRAO or retinal ischemia requiring stroke pathway, suspected GCA with vision symptoms, acute angle closure, open globe, orbital compartment syndrome, orbital cellulitis/endophthalmitis/invasive infection, severe persistent loss, or unreliable urgent follow-up. When the eye, retina, optic nerve, or brain is actively threatened, disposition is emergent specialty pathway.[1][2][3][4]
Urgent ophthalmology / neuro follow-up
Urgent same-day or next-day ophthalmology is typical for retinal detachment/tear concern, vitreous hemorrhage with decreased view, optic neuritis, unexplained persistent visual symptoms, hyphema, significant corneal ulcer, or diagnostic uncertainty. Neurology/stroke follow-up is added for vascular or optic neuritis pathways. Give a specific specialist destination and timeframe, not vague "eye follow-up."[3][4]
Discharge checklist
Discharge only when visual acuity is normal or baseline, pain controlled, diagnosis is low-risk and clear, IOP is safe when relevant, no RAPD/focal neuro/GCA/retinal detachment/open globe/orbital infection concern, and follow-up is reliable. Return now for: worse vision, recurrent curtain/shade, eye pain/redness, headache/jaw pain/scalp tenderness, diplopia, neuro symptoms, vomiting/halos, fever/swelling, new floaters/flashes, or inability to obtain follow-up.[3][4]

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT be falsely reassured by a normal-looking ED fundus exam in suspected CRAO/BRAO — early retinal ischemia can be subtle and ED fundoscopy can miss findings.

Do NOT treat a fully-recovered transient monocular vision loss as benign — amaurosis fugax gets urgent TIA/stroke workup; retinal TIA can precede brain stroke, especially early after the event.

Do NOT wait for biopsy or ultrasound to treat suspected GCA with visual symptoms — start high-dose corticosteroids immediately when suspicion is high.

Do NOT dilate a suspicious angle-closure eye before ophthalmology guidance.

Do NOT patch or place pressure on a suspected open globe — avoid tonometry and ultrasound if rupture is possible.

Do NOT rely on a normal ED ocular ultrasound to clear retinal disease — it does not rule out small retinal tears or subtle macula-threatening disease.

Do NOT give oral prednisone alone for typical optic neuritis — follow the neuro-ophthalmology/neurology pathway.
