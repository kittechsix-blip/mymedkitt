Human Trafficking — ED Recognition & Response

ED patients with suspected sex or labor trafficking, any age. For minors, ANY commercial sex is trafficking regardless of force/fraud/coercion, and CPS/law-enforcement reporting is mandatory regardless of patient consent. Universal screening is reasonable for high-yield presentations: sexual assault, STI evaluation (especially adolescents), pregnancy in minors, psychiatric or substance-use complaints in young patients, and injuries inconsistent with the stated mechanism.

⚠ Minors: ANY commercial sex act is trafficking regardless of force/fraud/coercion — mandatory CPS/law enforcement reporting, does not require patient consent.
⚠ Do NOT screen in front of the companion — separate the patient first; never confront the companion directly if they resist separation.
⚠ Do NOT force disclosure or pressure the patient to report — respect patient autonomy except when the patient is a minor.
⚠ Do NOT release a minor to a suspected trafficker — the hospital can refuse release if there is a safety concern.
⚠ National Human Trafficking Hotline: 1-888-373-7888 (24/7, confidential, 200+ languages); text "HELP" to 233733 (BeFree).

Recognition

Human trafficking occurs in every community — including yours. Definition: use of force, fraud, or coercion to exploit a person for labor or commercial sex. For minors, ANY commercial sex is trafficking regardless of force/fraud/coercion. 88% of trafficking victims access healthcare while being trafficked, often in EDs — you may be the only person who can help.

  • Intellectual/developmental disabilities

Suspicion Assessment

Trust your instincts
Consider: number of red flags present, patient demeanor, controlling companion behavior, inconsistencies in history, and your gut feeling. You don't need to be certain to screen — screening is low-risk and may be life-saving.

Safe Screening

Separating from companion
Safe separation is essential before screening — never screen in front of the companion. Strategies: "It's hospital policy to examine patients privately"; take the patient to X-ray/CT; have security present but unobtrusive. If the companion resists: document it, involve security/social work, and do NOT confront them — prioritize patient safety. If unable to separate, document and attempt again before discharge.

Consider routine screening for high-risk presentations. Normalize the questions: *"I ask everyone these questions to make sure they're safe..."* High-yield presentations: sexual assault, STI evaluation (esp. adolescents), pregnancy in minors, psychiatric complaints in young patients, substance use in young patients, injuries with inconsistent history. Universal screening removes stigma — the patient doesn't feel singled out.

  • Environment: "Where do you sleep at night?" / "Is there anyone who controls where you go?" / "Do you feel safe at home?"
  • Work: "Can you leave your job if you want to?" / "Has anyone ever threatened you to make you work?" / "Do you owe your employer money?"
  • Control: "Does anyone hold your ID or documents?" / "Are you free to come and go as you wish?" / "Has anyone ever made you do something you didn't want to do?"
  • Adolescents: "Has anyone ever asked you to have sex in exchange for something?" / "Has anyone ever taken pictures of you?"
Interpreting the screen
Positive: answers suggest exploitation, patient discloses trafficking, confirms red-flag behaviors, or expresses fear of companion. Uncertain: evasive or inconsistent answers, unable to adequately assess. Negative: no concerning responses, patient denies exploitation, plausible explanations for red flags.

Don't

Respect their autonomy
Leaving a trafficking situation is complex and dangerous — the patient may not be ready. If they want help: activate resources, safety-plan, consider admission for safety. If they don't want help: respect the decision, provide resources for future use, and let them know they can return. Do NOT force intervention — except for minors.

Minor involved — mandatory reporting
ANY commercial sex involving a minor is trafficking (no force/fraud/coercion required). Mandatory reporting to CPS/law enforcement — does not require patient consent. Process: notify social work immediately, contact CPS hotline, notify law enforcement (may be the same call), keep the child safe in hospital, do NOT release to a suspected trafficker. If the trafficker is a parent/guardian: CPS involvement is essential, may need security/LE to prevent removal, and the hospital can refuse to release if there is a safety concern.

Still provide resources: trafficking hotline info, domestic violence resources, return instructions. Document red flags observed, screening attempted, barriers encountered, resources provided. Consider admission for observation if medically indicated, social work consultation, or a repeat screening attempt before discharge. Trust your instincts — if something feels wrong, involve social work.

Still document that screening occurred, the patient's responses, and that no indicators were identified. Provide safety resources anyway — normalize offering them. Return precautions: if the situation changes, come back; the ED is always available. Patients may deny trafficking even if it's occurring — one negative screen doesn't mean never follow up.
