Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: Diagnose Fast, Prevent the Rebleed

Adults ≥18 in the ED with acute nontraumatic headache or suspected spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhage (sSAH). Excludes traumatic SAH, pediatrics, and patients with a known aneurysm under active surveillance. The Ottawa SAH Rule applies only to acute nontraumatic headache peaking within 1 hour with a normal neurologic exam.

⚠ Rebleeding is the highest-mortality SAH complication — cumulative incidence 8-23% in the first 72 hours (50-90% of episodes within the first 6 hours). Primary prevention: SBP <160 mmHg until the aneurysm is secured, plus early definitive repair.
⚠ Antifibrinolytics (TXA) are NOT recommended (2023 AHA/ASA) — the ULTRA trial showed no functional benefit (mRS 0-3: 60% TXA vs 64% control) and increased hydrocephalus and seizures.
⚠ Avoid nitroprusside for BP control — it increases intracranial pressure and carries toxicity risk with prolonged infusion. Use labetalol, nicardipine, or clevidipine instead.
⚠ Avoid phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis — associated with worse outcomes, functional decline, and cognitive disability. If prophylaxis is used at all, give levetiracetam 500 mg PO/IV BID, short course <3 days.
⚠ Avoid NSAIDs for SAH headache — platelet dysfunction increases rebleeding risk. Use acetaminophen; use opioids cautiously (they interfere with the neurologic exam).
⚠ Do NOT reverse antiplatelet agents in an anticoagulated patient unless a neurosurgical procedure is planned — transfusion without benefit increases mortality.
⚠ Nimodipine is oral/NG route only — severe hypotension and cardiac arrest have been reported with inadvertent IV administration of the oral capsule.
⚠ A negative CT does not rule out SAH — sensitivity falls from 98.7% within 6 hours to 58% by day 5. Lumbar puncture is still indicated per protocol when suspicion remains.

1 · Recognition & epidemiology

Why this is a rule-out disease
Spontaneous SAH is 85% aneurysmal; ~10% is nonaneurysmal perimesencephalic (better prognosis). Mortality is ~40% at 1 week (10-15% die prehospital). Up to 5% are missed on the first ED visit — delayed diagnosis worsens outcomes.[1][2][3]

Key pearl: No headache feature reliably distinguishes SAH from benign causes — pain responding to analgesics does NOT exclude SAH. Isolated neck/back pain occurs in 8% with no headache at all.[1][4]

2 · Ottawa Rule & CT

Ottawa SAH Rule
For acute nontraumatic headache peaking within 1 hour with a normal neurologic exam, further workup is required if ANY: age ≥40, neck pain/stiffness, witnessed LOC, exertional onset, thunderclap (peaks within 1 minute), limited neck flexion. Sensitivity 100%, specificity 27.5%.[15]

Highest sensitivity (≤6h) requires: 3rd-generation+ scanner, attending radiologist read, normal neuro exam, and normal hematocrit. A negative CT does not rule out SAH, especially at later presentations.[1]

ACEP 2019 Level B rule-out
Negative CT ≤6h + 3rd-gen+ scanner + attending radiologist read + normal neuro exam + no anemia (Hct not <30%) + low clinical suspicion → sSAH may be ruled out without LP. If any criterion is unmet or suspicion remains high, proceed to LP.[1][5][6]

3 · Lumbar puncture & CTA

  1. Lateral recumbent position.
  2. Measure opening pressure (elevated >20 cm H₂O in 60% of sSAH).
  3. Collect 4 serial tubes of CSF.
  4. Send the last tube for cell count AND xanthochromia — takes up to 12 hours to develop.
  5. Traumatic tap occurs in 10-15% of patients.
CSF findingInterpretation
Xanthochromia or persistent RBCs across all 4 tubesPositive — SAH
Partial RBC clearing (e.g., 3,000 → 400)Equivocal — obtain CTA
>10,000 RBCs in final tube6x more likely SAH
Zero RBCs in final tubeTraumatic tap
No RBCs, no xanthochromiaSAH excluded

Visual xanthochromia: sensitivity 93%, specificity 95%, NPV 99%.[1][6]

CTA for equivocal CSF
CTA sensitivity for aneurysm detection is 97-99% vs digital subtraction angiography. Bloody CSF with negative cerebrovascular imaging = low risk for subsequent SAH. Aneurysm detection decreases for aneurysms <4 mm. Consult neurosurgery for shared decision-making.[2][6]

Vasospasm timing
Peak vasospasm risk at 7-10 days (window: days to 2 weeks). All confirmed SAH patients receive nimodipine for vasospasm prevention regardless of Fisher grade; high-grade Fisher needs closer monitoring and earlier neurosurgical intervention.[8][10]

Historically, Hunt & Hess grades 4-5 carry 70-90% mortality, improved with modern neurocritical care.[7]

Early treatment within 72 hours is standard. Choice depends on aneurysm anatomy, clinician expertise, and patient factors — a multidisciplinary decision. Cerebral angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for planning; CTA is 97-99% sensitive; angiography may be negative in 10-20% (perimesencephalic hemorrhage, vasospasm, thrombosed aneurysm). Delaying neurosurgical consultation and CTA delays definitive repair — obtain both immediately once SAH is confirmed.[2][11]

Special populationspregnancy · anticoagulation · perimesencephalic

Pregnant/postpartum: hypercoagulable states — also consider ischemic stroke, cerebral venous thrombosis, RCVS, preeclampsia, PRES. Management principles are unchanged.[2]

Anticoagulated patients
Reverse therapeutic anticoagulation acutely. Do NOT reverse antiplatelet agents unless a neurosurgical procedure is planned — transfusion without benefit increases mortality.[2]

Perimesencephalic hemorrhage: 10% of sSAH; blood confined around the midbrain on CT with a normal angiogram (DSA is the test of choice). Excellent prognosis — 98% survive without deficits.[2]

Acute deterioration: repeat CT immediately — rebleeding, cerebral infarction, acute hydrocephalus, or subdural extension are potentially treatable causes.[2][3]
