Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: Diagnose Fast, Prevent Rebleed

Adults ≥18 in the ED with acute nontraumatic severe headache or suspected spontaneous SAH. Excludes traumatic SAH, pediatrics, and known intracranial mass — each follows a different pathway.

⚠ Keep SBP <160 mmHg until the aneurysm is secured to prevent rebleeding — titratable IV labetalol or nicardipine; avoid nitroprusside (raises ICP).
⚠ Rebleeding is the deadliest complication — 8-23% in the first 72h (50-90% of those within 6h). Early definitive repair is the only proven prevention; TXA is NOT recommended (2023 AHA/ASA; ULTRA trial negative).
⚠ Nimodipine 60 mg PO/NG q4h × 21 days, started within 96 hours, is the only proven intervention against vasospasm — oral route only; IV nimodipine, magnesium, and statins are not effective.
⚠ A negative CT does not exclude SAH beyond 6 hours — proceed to lumbar puncture (xanthochromia, RBC count) whenever suspicion persists.
⚠ Avoid phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis (worse outcomes) — if prophylaxis is used give levetiracetam, short course <3 days.

1 · Recognize — the headache that kills

What you are up against
Spontaneous SAH is blood in the CSF: 85% are aneurysmal, mortality is ~40% at 1 week, and up to 5% are missed on the first ED visit. Delayed diagnosis worsens outcomes — this is a rule-out, not a rule-in, disease.[2]

Classic: sudden severe thunderclap headache peaking within seconds — "worst headache of my life." Only 10-16% of thunderclap headaches are SAH, yet 70% of SAH present with headache alone and the neuro exam is normal in 75%. Pain that responds to analgesics does not exclude SAH.[1]

Atypical presentations that get missed5 traps
  • Isolated neck or back pain (8% have no headache)
  • Transient loss of consciousness (~25%) or a brief seizure at onset
  • Vomiting (~70%) or neck stiffness (~60%) as the loudest complaint
  • Sentinel / warning headache days to weeks earlier (10-43%)
  • Cranial nerve palsy (e.g., painful third nerve = posterior communicating aneurysm)

For an acute nontraumatic headache reaching maximum intensity within 1 hour and a normal neuro exam, apply the Ottawa SAH Rule — sensitivity 100%, specificity 27.5%. Any single criterion mandates workup.[4][15]

Within the 6-hour window
A negative CT on a third-generation scanner, read by an attending radiologist, with a normal neuro exam and no anemia, is 98.7% sensitive and may exclude SAH without LP (ACEP 2019 Level B). If any condition is unmet or suspicion is high, still do the LP.[1]
Beyond 6 hours
CT sensitivity falls (86% at day 1, 58% at day 5). A negative CT does not exclude SAH — proceed to lumbar puncture.[6]

Lumbar puncture: lateral recumbent, measure opening pressure, collect 4 serial tubes, send the last tube for cell count and xanthochromia. Xanthochromia (visual sens 93% / spec 95%) takes up to 12h to develop; before 12h accept either RBCs or xanthochromia. Persistent RBCs across all 4 tubes = SAH; clearing RBCs = possible traumatic tap → CTA.[13]

4 · SAH confirmed — first moves

The moment blood is seen
Get neurosurgery on the phone now and order CTA to localize the aneurysm. 85% are aneurysmal (70% anterior circulation); 10% are perimesencephalic with an excellent prognosis (98% recover without deficit). Everything below runs in parallel while the aneurysm is being secured.[2]

Modified Fisher predicts vasospasm from CT blood burden: grade 0-1 low risk, 2-3 moderate-high, 4 (thick SAH + IVH) highest risk. Vasospasm peaks days 7-10 — nimodipine for all, closer surveillance for high Fisher.[8]

6 · Prevent the complications & disposition

Nimodipine — vasospasm prevention
60 mg PO every 4 hours × 21 days
PO / NG
Start within 96 hours of ictus (earlier is better). Oral route ONLY — no IV nimodipine. Crush + NG if unable to swallow. Give to hemodynamically stable patients.

Nimodipine is the only proven pharmacologic intervention to reduce vasospasm-related ischemia — Cochrane risk ratio 0.67. It is a persistent order (start it within 96 hours and continue the full course); magnesium and statins are not effective.[9][10]

  1. Head of bed 30°, bed rest, NPO until the repair plan is set
  2. Continuous cardiac monitoring — ECG changes occur in 50-100%; troponin rises in 20-40% (neurogenic stunned myocardium)
  3. Serial neuro exams q1h; repeat CT immediately for any decline
  4. Prevent Valsalva: stool softeners, antiemetics, adequate analgesia

Seizures occur in up to 20% and can rupture an unsecured aneurysm. No RCT supports routine prophylaxis; if used, keep it a short course <3 days. Avoid phenytoin (worse outcomes).
Rebleeding — the deadliest complication
Cumulative incidence 8-23% in the first 72 hours, with 50-90% of episodes in the first 6 hours. Early definitive repair is the primary prevention; TXA is not recommended (2023 AHA/ASA, ULTRA trial negative).[14][16]
Definitive repair & special populationscoil vs clip · pregnancy · anticoagulation

Endovascular coiling (ISAT: better outcomes than clipping, slightly higher rebleed risk) vs microvascular clipping (higher seizure risk, needed for some anatomy). Early treatment within 72 hours is standard. DSA is the gold standard for planning; CTA is 97-99% sensitive.[11]

  • Pregnant/postpartum: same principles; also weigh CVT, RCVS, pre-eclampsia/PRES
  • Anticoagulated: reverse therapeutic anticoagulation acutely
  • Do NOT reverse antiplatelet agents unless a neurosurgical procedure is planned — transfusion without benefit increases mortality
  • Perimesencephalic pattern with a normal angiogram: excellent prognosis (98% recover without deficit)
Disposition
Every confirmed SAH goes to a neuro ICU. Outcomes are better at high-volume centers (mortality OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.60-0.97) — transfer early if you are not one. Air transport is safe; average 5.2h to the neurosurgical center and 90% arrive without GCS decline.[12]
