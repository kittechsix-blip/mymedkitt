CO Toxicity: Recognize, Treat, Dispo

Adults presenting to the ED with suspected or confirmed carbon monoxide exposure — fires, faulty heating systems, generators, enclosed-space car exhaust, or incidental elevated COHb. Includes pregnancy-specific and fire/cyanide co-exposure pathways. Excludes isolated cyanide poisoning without CO co-exposure and pediatric weight-based dosing (the source consult states only a fixed adult hydroxocobalamin dose).

⚠ Pulse oximetry (and a standard ABG) is FALSELY NORMAL in CO poisoning — it cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin. Obtain a venous or arterial COHb level by co-oximetry.
⚠ Severe CO toxicity (COHb >40%, seizures, coma, arrhythmias, MI, respiratory failure): give 100% O2 via NRB/BVM immediately — do not wait for COHb confirmation. Intubate for GCS ≤8 or airway compromise. Contact the hyperbaric center immediately. COHb may be misleadingly low if time has elapsed since exposure or O2 was given en route — treat the clinical presentation, not just the number.
⚠ Fire/smoke inhalation — suspect cyanide co-exposure if AMS out of proportion to COHb, hypotension, or lactate >10 mmol/L. Give hydroxocobalamin 5g IV over 15 minutes empirically (can be given concurrently with O2 therapy). Hydroxocobalamin interferes with co-oximetry for ~24 hours — draw COHb BEFORE administration if possible.
⚠ Pregnancy has a lower HBO threshold than non-pregnant patients — COHb >15% (vs >25%), any LOC, or any fetal distress. Fetal hemoglobin binds CO more tightly and eliminates ~5× longer than maternal Hb; continue 100% O2 until both mother and fetus are cleared.
⚠ Any loss of consciousness at any time — even transient — is an indication for HBO evaluation, regardless of the COHb level.

1 · Recognition & epidemiology

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colorless, odorless gas that binds hemoglobin with 240× greater affinity than oxygen. CO poisoning causes ~50,000 ED visits and ~400 deaths annually in the US, more often in winter (heating systems, generator use during power outages).

"Occult CO" cluster
Multiple patients from the same household presenting with headache, nausea, or "flu-like" symptoms — think CO poisoning.

4 · Diagnostic workup & COHb interpretation

COHb level (co-oximetry): venous or arterial — both are accurate. Standard ABG and pulse ox CANNOT detect CO — must use co-oximetry. Normal COHb in a non-smoker is <3%; a smoker may run up to 10%. Cardiac: ECG (ischemia, arrhythmias) and troponin (worse prognosis if elevated). Metabolic: BMP, lactate, VBG/ABG (PaO2 is NORMAL — dissolved O2 unaffected). Additional: pregnancy test in women of childbearing age, salicylate level if coingestant concern.

5 · Oxygen therapy

Oxygen
100%
Inhalation via non-rebreather mask (NRB) or BVM
Start immediately for ALL suspected CO poisoning — do not wait for lab confirmation. Continue until COHb <5% AND completely asymptomatic. NRB flow rate 15 L/min to maintain reservoir bag inflation.
TherapyCOHb half-life
Room air (21% O2)4-6 hours

Intubation indications
GCS ≤8, respiratory failure, airway compromise, or unable to tolerate NRB. Post-intubation: FiO2 100%, target normal PaCO2. Assess fire/smoke victims for thermal airway injury.

6 · HBO criteria & protocol

Weiss criterionThreshold
Loss of consciousnessAny time, even transient
COHb>25%
Metabolic acidosisBase deficit >2
Age>36 years

Also indicated for: neurologic symptoms (confusion, ataxia, seizure, focal deficits), cardiac ischemia or arrhythmia, pregnancy with COHb >15% or fetal distress, and persistent symptoms despite 4-6 hours of NBO.

Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO)
2.5-3 ATA
90-120 minutes per session, 1-3 treatments
Most effective within 6 hours of exposure; may still help up to 24 hours. Continue 100% O2 during transport. Relative contraindications: untreated pneumothorax (absolute), active bleomycin/doxorubicin chemo, severe COPD with CO2 retention, claustrophobia (can sedate).
Contact hyperbaric center early
Divers Alert Network (DAN): 1-919-684-9111 (24/7). Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222. Provide age/weight, exposure time and source, peak/current COHb, LOC history, neuro and cardiac status, pregnancy status, acidosis, airway status.

7 · Special populations

Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) binds CO more tightly than adult Hb. Fetal COHb runs 10-15% higher than maternal levels and eliminates ~5× more slowly, crossing the placenta freely. Risks: fetal hypoxia even with mild maternal symptoms, fetal demise, developmental abnormalities, neurologic injury. HBO is safe in pregnancy and may be fetal-protective — the risk of not treating outweighs HBO risk. OB consultation for fetal monitoring.

Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit)
5g IV over 15 minutes
IV
Empiric for smoke inhalation + (AMS OR hypotension OR lactate >10 mmol/L). May repeat 5g ×1 if persistent hemodynamic instability. Can give concurrently with O2 therapy — does not interfere with CO treatment. Interferes with co-oximetry for ~24 hours — draw COHb BEFORE administration if possible. This is a clinical diagnosis; do not wait for a cyanide level.

8 · Delayed neurologic sequelae (DNS)

DNS occurs in 10-30% of significant exposures
Onset 2-40 days after apparent recovery (most within 2-3 weeks). Cognitive impairment, personality changes, movement disorders, gait disturbances, incontinence. Risk factors: LOC during exposure, age >36, prolonged exposure, higher COHb, metabolic acidosis, delayed treatment. HBO may reduce DNS risk. Neuropsychological testing recommended for significant exposures.

9 · Disposition

Discharge if ALL met
Asymptomatic, COHb <5%, normal ECG, normal troponin, no history of LOC, no neurologic symptoms, CO source identified and eliminated, safe environment. Counsel: do NOT return to the source until inspected; install CO detectors on every level of the home; return for headache/confusion/memory changes. PCP follow-up within 1 week.
Admit if
Persistent symptoms, any cardiac involvement, pregnancy, LOC at any time (even resolved), social concerns (intentional exposure, unsafe home), or unable to confirm source eliminated. Continue 100% O2, telemetry, serial COHb q4-6h, neuro checks q4h.

Do NOT — pitfalls

Do NOT assume a lower COHb level means a better prognosis — COHb does not correlate well with symptoms or outcome; loss of consciousness and neurologic symptoms are more predictive.
