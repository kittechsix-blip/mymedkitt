Myocarditis — ED Recognition to Disposition

Adults in the ED with suspected or confirmed myocarditis (viral/idiopathic, autoimmune, cardiotoxin, immune checkpoint inhibitor, or post-vaccine). Covers ED recognition, Stage C vs Stage D (fulminant) risk stratification, fulminant stabilization, etiology framework, and disposition. Excludes pediatric-specific dosing and detailed inpatient/tertiary-center biopsy and immunosuppression protocols — those are cardiology/ICU decisions beyond the ED phase.

⚠ Stage D / fulminant myocarditis = hemodynamic instability (shock) or electrical instability (VT/VF, high-grade AV block, recurrent syncope) — pads on, continuous telemetry, POCUS, early advanced HF/ECMO-capable transfer. Do NOT wait for CMR in a crashing patient.
⚠ Norepinephrine is first-line for hypotension/MAP support in fulminant myocarditis shock — titrate to MAP ≥65 mmHg. Avoid large blind fluid boluses; most fulminant myocarditis shock is pump failure, not fluid deficit.
⚠ Sustained VT/VF, high-grade AV block, or syncope with an abnormal myocarditis workup is Stage D risk — pads on, correct K/Mg, cardiology/EP consult, ICU admission.
⚠ Do NOT discharge a symptomatic immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) myocarditis patient with elevated troponin as "viral myocarditis" — hold ICI therapy, urgent oncology/cardiology, admit to a monitored setting, ICU if arrhythmia/HF/shock.
⚠ Giant-cell, eosinophilic, or sarcoid myocarditis are "do not miss" patterns — urgent biopsy and immunosuppression may change outcome. Cardiology early; admit/transfer to a biopsy-capable center if unstable or progressive.
⚠ Do NOT discharge suspected myocarditis with VT, high-grade AV block, syncope, new LV dysfunction, shock, or ICI therapy.
⚠ Do NOT use NSAIDs casually in symptomatic HF or LV dysfunction.
⚠ Do NOT clear return to sports/exercise from the ED — avoid strenuous activity for 3-6 months in symptomatic myocarditis; return only after cardiology clearance and objective testing.
⚠ Stage D = hemodynamic or electrical instability; stable but abnormal ECG/troponin/echo still usually requires monitored telemetry admission — normal early tests do not fully exclude myocarditis when suspicion is high.
⚠ Do NOT forget ACS, aortic dissection, PE, and Takotsubo mimics before anchoring on myocarditis — if the ECG looks territorial or the story is ischemic, treat ACS until reasonably excluded.

ED recognition

Myocarditis is a syndrome, not one disease — it can look like viral chest pain, STEMI/NSTEMI, heart failure, dysrhythmia, syncope, or cardiogenic shock. Rule out immediate mimics, identify Stage D instability, and involve cardiology early.

  • Recent viral syndrome, COVID/influenza-like illness, GI illness
  • Chest pain with troponin elevation and a non-obstructive/atypical ACS pattern
  • New dyspnea, pulmonary edema, or cardiogenic shock without known CAD
  • Palpitations, VT, high-grade AV block, syncope
  • Autoimmune disease, sarcoid, eosinophilia, drug reaction
  • Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy
  • Young male within 7 days after mRNA/Novavax COVID vaccine dose 2 — rare, usually improving by discharge, but still evaluate carefully
Do not anchor — active mimics to exclude
Myocarditis is a diagnosis of suspicion plus exclusion. Actively rule out ACS/STEMI/NSTEMI (territorial ECG, dynamic troponin), aortic dissection (tearing pain, pulse/BP differential), PE (pleuritic pain, hypoxia, RV strain), Takotsubo (stress trigger, apical ballooning), sepsis/toxic shock, pericarditis/myopericarditis (positional pain, diffuse ST/PR changes, preserved LV function), and tachycardia-mediated/demand injury. If the ECG looks territorial or the story is ischemic, treat ACS until reasonably excluded.
  • 12-lead ECG + repeat ECG if symptoms evolve
  • High-sensitivity troponin, repeat if early or changing symptoms
  • BNP/NT-proBNP if dyspnea/HF features
  • CBC with differential — eosinophilia matters
  • CMP/Mg/Phos, lactate if ill, TSH when indicated
  • CRP/ESR — supportive inflammation markers
  • Viral testing when clinically useful (SARS-CoV-2, influenza, respiratory panel)
  • Pregnancy test when applicable
  • CXR if dyspnea/HF/alternative diagnosis
  • Bedside echo/POCUS early if abnormal vitals, dyspnea, syncope, or elevated troponin
Normal early tests do not rule out myocarditis
ECG, troponin, and echo are useful for triage, but normal tests do not fully rule out myocarditis when pretest suspicion is high.
POCUS / echo red flagsLV/RV dysfunction, effusion, IVC plethora, tamponade mimics

Echo cannot prove myocarditis, but it detects danger: new LV systolic dysfunction (global or regional), RV dysfunction or biventricular failure, pericardial effusion/tamponade physiology, wall thickening from edema, IVC plethora, pulmonary edema, or low forward flow. High-risk pattern: biventricular dysfunction, reduced EF, pericardial effusion with instability, or low-output physiology. Regional wall-motion abnormality can still be ACS — if an ischemic pattern is plausible, ACS evaluation continues.

Etiology framework

Ask and look for: viral/COVID/influenza-like illness, recent vaccination with compatible timing, immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, autoimmune disease/sarcoid/lupus/vasculitis, drug reaction/eosinophilia/DRESS, tick/travel/infectious exposure (Lyme, Chagas, HIV, TB, diphtheria depending on context), giant-cell clues (rapidly progressive HF, VT, AV block), or toxin/cardiotoxin (cocaine/amphetamine, anthracyclines, clozapine). Do not shotgun labs in every low-risk patient — target the workup to the phenotype.

Common ED phenotype: chest pain after viral syndrome, elevated troponin, nonischemic ECG pattern, stable vitals. Admit or observe with telemetry if troponin/ECG abnormal or myocarditis is likely; echo for LV/RV function and effusion; cardiology consult for CMR timing; supportive care; avoid strenuous activity; HF guideline-directed therapy if LV dysfunction; avoid routine antibiotics or antivirals unless a treatable infection is suspected. Vaccine-associated myocarditis is rare, most often in adolescent/young adult males within 7 days after dose 2; most reported cases improve by discharge but still warrant evaluation for alternative causes and cardiology involvement.

ICI myocarditis is uncommon but high mortality — treat as high-risk until proven otherwise. Clues: on PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA-4 therapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, atezolizumab), often early after starting therapy but can occur later, chest pain/dyspnea/weakness/myositis, troponin elevation, conduction disease, VT, LV dysfunction. Hold ICI therapy and contact oncology/cardiology urgently; admit to a monitored setting, ICU if arrhythmia/HF/shock; check CK, LFTs, troponin trend, ECG, echo; high-dose corticosteroids are often started early by oncology/cardiology protocols when suspected.

Giant-cell myocarditis clues: new HF over days-weeks, VT or high-grade AV block, failure to improve with usual care, autoimmune history. Eosinophilic myocarditis clues: eosinophilia, rash, fever, DRESS/drug exposure, asthma/EGPA phenotype. Sarcoid myocarditis clues: AV block, VT, unexplained cardiomyopathy, known pulmonary/systemic sarcoid. ED action: cardiology early, admit/transfer to a biopsy-capable center if unstable or progressive — do not start casual outpatient management.

Target testing to exposure and phenotype. Infectious workup when indicated: SARS-CoV-2/influenza/respiratory viruses, HIV if risk/unknown status, Lyme in endemic exposure, Chagas in compatible geography, blood cultures if febrile/toxic, TB/fungal/parasitic studies only when history supports it. Autoimmune workup when indicated: ANA/ENA/complement/ESR/CRP for systemic features, sarcoid evaluation for conduction disease/VT/unexplained cardiomyopathy, rheumatology input for lupus/vasculitis/EGPA. Avoid broad low-yield panels in routine stable viral-like cases.

Stable myocarditis treatment

No magic ED antidote for routine viral myocarditis — treat the syndrome and prevent deterioration. Telemetry/observation or admission when abnormal ECG, elevated troponin, syncope, arrhythmia, or LV dysfunction. Treat HF with guideline-directed therapy when LV dysfunction exists. Give explicit exercise restriction. Avoid alcohol, cocaine/amphetamines, and cardiotoxins. Avoid routine antibiotics/antivirals unless a specific treatable infection is suspected. Cardiology follow-up and CMR planning when appropriate.

Congested but perfusing: diuretics for pulmonary edema/volume overload, oxygen/NIV if needed (watch hemodynamics), start/continue GDMT with cardiology input when stable. Hypoperfused/shock: no longer routine HF — use the fulminant pathway with pressor/inotrope support and early advanced HF/ICU involvement. Avoid exercise clearance from the ED, avoid NSAIDs in symptomatic HF or LV dysfunction, do not discharge new LV dysfunction without a monitored plan. Follow-up: repeat echo 2-4 weeks for Stage C/D, then further imaging/rhythm testing before exercise return.

Pericarditic chest pain and myocarditis overlap; treatment changes when HF/LV dysfunction is present. Reasonable when pericarditic pain predominates AND no symptomatic HF/LV dysfunction: NSAID such as ibuprofen per pericarditis protocol, colchicine if myopericarditis/pericarditis phenotype, PPI if GI-risk. Avoid or discuss with cardiology if new LV dysfunction, symptomatic HF/pulmonary edema/shock, renal failure, anticoagulation, or GI bleeding risk. Analgesia alternative: acetaminophen, opioid-sparing approach when possible. Troponin elevation alone does not forbid NSAIDs, but symptomatic HF/LV dysfunction changes the risk-benefit.

Disposition

Most confirmed/suspected myocarditis with abnormal objective findings needs monitored care. Admit/observe with telemetry for: elevated troponin with suspected myocarditis, abnormal ECG beyond nonspecific changes, arrhythmia/palpitations with concerning ECG/syncope, new LV/RV dysfunction or HF symptoms, pericardial effusion with concern, ICI therapy/eosinophilia/autoimmune disease, or pediatric/adolescent concern (lower threshold). Transfer/ICU if shock, VT/VF, high-grade AV block, rising lactate, severe EF reduction, biventricular failure, or a suspected biopsy-changing etiology. Discharge is only for low suspicion/normal objective testing with reliable return precautions and follow-up.

Inpatient plan: telemetry; troponin/ECG trends as clinically indicated; formal echocardiogram if not done; cardiology consultation; CMR planning when stable/available; HF therapy if LV dysfunction; etiology-directed testing for atypical/severe/high-risk cases; clear exercise restriction on discharge.

Low suspicion is not "myocarditis ruled out forever" — it means no current objective evidence and no danger phenotype. Possible discharge only if: normal vitals and well appearing, no syncope/sustained palpitations/HF symptoms/exertional chest pain, reassuring ECG, normal troponin with appropriate timing/repeat strategy, no concerning echo/POCUS findings if performed, no ICI therapy or high-risk immune/drug trigger, and reliable follow-up/return precautions. Return now for: worse or exertional chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, fever/toxic appearance, new edema, exercise intolerance.

Do not let active myocarditis exercise. Symptomatic Stage C/D myocarditis should avoid strenuous physical activity for 3-6 months. Before return, reassess with cardiology and objective testing: symptom resolution, normalizing troponin/inflammatory markers, LV function recovered on echo/CMR, no active inflammation/high-risk LGE pattern on CMR when obtained, 24-hour rhythm monitoring without concerning arrhythmia, exercise stress testing when appropriate.

High-risk rhythms: sustained VT/VF, recurrent nonsustained VT with symptoms or LV dysfunction, high-grade AV block/complete heart block, syncope with abnormal ECG/troponin/echo, wide-complex tachycardia. ED actions: pads on, defib/cardioversion per ACLS, correct K/Mg, avoid QT-prolonging meds when possible, amiodarone for stable VT per local ACLS protocol, temporary pacing for unstable high-grade AV block, cardiology/EP + ICU admission.

Transfer / MCS trigger | Why it matters

Persistent hypotension or rising lactate despite pressor/inotrope support | Ongoing hypoperfusion despite maximal medical support

Biventricular dysfunction or rapidly falling EF | Marker of progressive pump failure

VT/VF storm, recurrent malignant arrhythmias, high-grade AV block | Electrical instability needing EP-capable care

Need for temporary circulatory support (IABP, Impella, VA-ECMO, VAD evaluation) | Requires an ECMO/MCS-capable center

Etiology framework

Ask and look for: viral/COVID/influenza-like illness, recent vaccination with compatible timing, immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, autoimmune disease/sarcoid/lupus/vasculitis, drug reaction/eosinophilia/DRESS, tick/travel/infectious exposure (Lyme, Chagas, HIV, TB, diphtheria depending on context), giant-cell clues (rapidly progressive HF, VT, AV block), or toxin/cardiotoxin (cocaine/amphetamine, anthracyclines, clozapine). Do not shotgun labs in every low-risk patient — target the workup to the phenotype.

Common ED phenotype: chest pain after viral syndrome, elevated troponin, nonischemic ECG pattern, stable vitals. Admit or observe with telemetry if troponin/ECG abnormal or myocarditis is likely; echo for LV/RV function and effusion; cardiology consult for CMR timing; supportive care; avoid strenuous activity; HF guideline-directed therapy if LV dysfunction; avoid routine antibiotics or antivirals unless a treatable infection is suspected. Vaccine-associated myocarditis is rare, most often in adolescent/young adult males within 7 days after dose 2; most reported cases improve by discharge but still warrant evaluation for alternative causes and cardiology involvement.

ICI myocarditis is uncommon but high mortality — treat as high-risk until proven otherwise. Clues: on PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA-4 therapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, atezolizumab), often early after starting therapy but can occur later, chest pain/dyspnea/weakness/myositis, troponin elevation, conduction disease, VT, LV dysfunction. Hold ICI therapy and contact oncology/cardiology urgently; admit to a monitored setting, ICU if arrhythmia/HF/shock; check CK, LFTs, troponin trend, ECG, echo; high-dose corticosteroids are often started early by oncology/cardiology protocols when suspected.

Giant-cell myocarditis clues: new HF over days-weeks, VT or high-grade AV block, failure to improve with usual care, autoimmune history. Eosinophilic myocarditis clues: eosinophilia, rash, fever, DRESS/drug exposure, asthma/EGPA phenotype. Sarcoid myocarditis clues: AV block, VT, unexplained cardiomyopathy, known pulmonary/systemic sarcoid. ED action: cardiology early, admit/transfer to a biopsy-capable center if unstable or progressive — do not start casual outpatient management.

Target testing to exposure and phenotype. Infectious workup when indicated: SARS-CoV-2/influenza/respiratory viruses, HIV if risk/unknown status, Lyme in endemic exposure, Chagas in compatible geography, blood cultures if febrile/toxic, TB/fungal/parasitic studies only when history supports it. Autoimmune workup when indicated: ANA/ENA/complement/ESR/CRP for systemic features, sarcoid evaluation for conduction disease/VT/unexplained cardiomyopathy, rheumatology input for lupus/vasculitis/EGPA. Avoid broad low-yield panels in routine stable viral-like cases.

Stable myocarditis treatment

No magic ED antidote for routine viral myocarditis — treat the syndrome and prevent deterioration. Telemetry/observation or admission when abnormal ECG, elevated troponin, syncope, arrhythmia, or LV dysfunction. Treat HF with guideline-directed therapy when LV dysfunction exists. Give explicit exercise restriction. Avoid alcohol, cocaine/amphetamines, and cardiotoxins. Avoid routine antibiotics/antivirals unless a specific treatable infection is suspected. Cardiology follow-up and CMR planning when appropriate.

Congested but perfusing: diuretics for pulmonary edema/volume overload, oxygen/NIV if needed (watch hemodynamics), start/continue GDMT with cardiology input when stable. Hypoperfused/shock: no longer routine HF — use the fulminant pathway with pressor/inotrope support and early advanced HF/ICU involvement. Avoid exercise clearance from the ED, avoid NSAIDs in symptomatic HF or LV dysfunction, do not discharge new LV dysfunction without a monitored plan. Follow-up: repeat echo 2-4 weeks for Stage C/D, then further imaging/rhythm testing before exercise return.

Pericarditic chest pain and myocarditis overlap; treatment changes when HF/LV dysfunction is present. Reasonable when pericarditic pain predominates AND no symptomatic HF/LV dysfunction: NSAID such as [ibuprofen](#/drug/ibuprofen/pain) per pericarditis protocol, [colchicine](#/drug/colchicine/pericarditis) if myopericarditis/pericarditis phenotype, PPI if GI-risk. Avoid or discuss with cardiology if new LV dysfunction, symptomatic HF/pulmonary edema/shock, renal failure, anticoagulation, or GI bleeding risk. Analgesia alternative: [acetaminophen](#/drug/acetaminophen/headache), opioid-sparing approach when possible. Troponin elevation alone does not forbid NSAIDs, but symptomatic HF/LV dysfunction changes the risk-benefit.

Colchicine: 0.6 mg PO daily (<70 kg) or 0.6 mg PO BID (≥70 kg) × 3 months PO — Combine with an NSAID (ibuprofen, ASA, or indomethacin) and a PPI. Use only for the pericarditic phenotype without symptomatic HF/LV dysfunction.

Ibuprofen: 400-600 mg PO q6-8h PRN (max 3200 mg/day) PO — Generic adult analgesic dosing — drug-store has no pericarditis-specific ibuprofen indication (see source conflict note). Avoid if symptomatic HF/LV dysfunction, active GI bleed, or significant renal impairment.

Acetaminophen: 1000 mg PO or IV every 6 hours (max 4000 mg/24h; 2000 mg/24h if hepatic impairment or chronic alcohol use) PO / IV — Generic adult analgesic dosing from drug-store's 'Headache / Analgesia' indication (distinct from the pregnancy-specific entry). Opioid-sparing alternative or adjunct regardless of NSAID eligibility.

Disposition

Most confirmed/suspected myocarditis with abnormal objective findings needs monitored care. Admit/observe with telemetry for: elevated troponin with suspected myocarditis, abnormal ECG beyond nonspecific changes, arrhythmia/palpitations with concerning ECG/syncope, new LV/RV dysfunction or HF symptoms, pericardial effusion with concern, ICI therapy/eosinophilia/autoimmune disease, or pediatric/adolescent concern (lower threshold). Transfer/ICU if shock, VT/VF, high-grade AV block, rising lactate, severe EF reduction, biventricular failure, or a suspected biopsy-changing etiology. Discharge is only for low suspicion/normal objective testing with reliable return precautions and follow-up.

Inpatient plan: telemetry; troponin/ECG trends as clinically indicated; formal echocardiogram if not done; cardiology consultation; CMR planning when stable/available; HF therapy if LV dysfunction; etiology-directed testing for atypical/severe/high-risk cases; clear exercise restriction on discharge.

Low suspicion is not "myocarditis ruled out forever" — it means no current objective evidence and no danger phenotype. Possible discharge only if: normal vitals and well appearing, no syncope/sustained palpitations/HF symptoms/exertional chest pain, reassuring ECG, normal troponin with appropriate timing/repeat strategy, no concerning echo/POCUS findings if performed, no ICI therapy or high-risk immune/drug trigger, and reliable follow-up/return precautions. Return now for: worse or exertional chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, fever/toxic appearance, new edema, exercise intolerance.

Do not let active myocarditis exercise. Symptomatic Stage C/D myocarditis should avoid strenuous physical activity for 3-6 months. Before return, reassess with cardiology and objective testing: symptom resolution, normalizing troponin/inflammatory markers, LV function recovered on echo/CMR, no active inflammation/high-risk LGE pattern on CMR when obtained, 24-hour rhythm monitoring without concerning arrhythmia, exercise stress testing when appropriate.
