Hypoglycemia: Treat the Patient, Then Chase the Cause

Adults in the ED with a low point-of-care glucose. Excludes neonates and pediatric weight-based dextrose dosing, and pregnancy-specific management — each needs a modified protocol.

⚠ Treat first, confirm second. When symptomatic, altered, seizing, or unsafe to swallow, give glucose immediately — do not delay for confirmatory labs.
⚠ IV dextrose when unsafe to swallow; glucagon 1 mg IM/SC is the bridge when there is no IV access.
⚠ One normal glucose is not a disposition. Recheck glucose in 15 minutes and continue serial checks based on cause and drug half-life.
⚠ Sulfonylurea / meglitinide hypoglycemia recurs because the drug keeps the patient secreting insulin — treat with dextrose PLUS octreotide, then observe or admit.
⚠ Long-acting or concentrated insulin, or a pump error, can recur for many hours — it needs monitored glucose support, not a single bolus.
⚠ Critical-illness hypoglycemia is a severity marker and is rarely dischargeable from the ED after one normal glucose.

3 · Recheck, then find the cause

After any treatment
Recheck glucose in 15 minutes. Repeat therapy until glucose is >70 mg/dL and symptoms improve, then continue serial checks by cause and drug half-life. If it recurs, start a D10W infusion titrated to keep glucose generally >100 mg/dL while you address the cause.[1]

Draw the critical sample during hypoglycemia (ideally before dextrose if there is no delay): plasma glucose, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, beta-hydroxybutyrate, sulfonylurea/meglitinide screen, cortisol when adrenal insufficiency is plausible, plus ethanol and renal function.[2][3]

4 · Rebound risk & disposition

Insulin vs. insulin secretagogue
Insulin overdose is managed with carbohydrate, a dextrose infusion, time, and electrolyte monitoring. Sulfonylurea / meglitinide hypoglycemia recurs because the drug keeps driving endogenous insulin secretion — do not chase it with repeated D50 alone.[4][5]
—High-risk recurrence drivers flagged (≥1 → do not discharge)
Sulfonylurea / meglitinide pathway
Correct the initial low, then give octreotide for recurrent or high-risk exposure and add D10W only as needed. Call poison center for overdose, pediatric exposure, intentional ingestion, or unclear formulation. Observe or admit — this recurs.[4][5]
Insulin overdose / pump pathway
Oral carbohydrate if able, otherwise a D10W infusion titrated to the glucose trend; suspend or remove the pump if overdose or malfunction is suspected. Monitor potassium, magnesium, and phosphate; prolonged observation for long-acting or concentrated insulin; psychiatric safety evaluation if intentional.[4]
Critical illness / endocrine pathway
Treat the hypoglycemia while treating the underlying illness and start a dextrose infusion if recurrent or intake is poor. If adrenal crisis is plausible, give hydrocortisone — do not delay it for cortisol testing. Rarely dischargeable after one normal glucose.[2][3]
No high-risk driver flagged
Discharge is reasonable only when the cause is clear and reversible, the patient is eating reliably, serial glucose stays stable, there is no secretagogue or long-acting-insulin concern, no renal/hepatic failure, sepsis, adrenal crisis, hypothermia, frailty, or malnutrition, the medication plan is adjusted with supplies and follow-up, and the home setting is safe.
Discharge bundle & return precautionscorrected glucose + corrected reason
  • Document the cause and serial glucose stability — never write only "hypoglycemia resolved".
  • Give a meal before leaving if insulin or a diabetes medication remains active.
  • Reduce or hold the culprit medication when appropriate.
  • Ensure access to glucose testing supplies and fast carbohydrate.
  • Prescribe or confirm glucagon for insulin-treated patients at risk of severe events.
  • Arrange timely PCP / endocrine follow-up.
  • Return precautions: recurrent symptoms, poor intake, vomiting, medication confusion, fever, or inability to check glucose.
