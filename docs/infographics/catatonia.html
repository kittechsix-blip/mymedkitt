Catatonia: Screen, Challenge, Etiology, ECT

Adults in the ED with suspected catatonia (any etiology — medical, psychiatric, or drug-induced). Excludes pediatric catatonia dosing (not modeled in myMedKitt) and definitive inpatient ECT-course management beyond the referral decision.

⚠ Excited catatonia is a medical emergency — extreme motor agitation with autonomic instability risks exhaustion, hyperthermia, and death. Give lorazepam 2 mg IV, may repeat q5-10 min up to 8 mg cumulative; do NOT give antipsychotics.
⚠ AVOID antipsychotics in active catatonia — can worsen catatonia and precipitate malignant catatonia or neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), which shares features with catatonia.
⚠ Malignant catatonia (fever >38°C, autonomic instability) requires ICU admission — do not manage on a psychiatric floor.
⚠ All catatonia patients require admission — cannot be safely discharged from the ED pending etiology workup and benzodiazepine titration.
⚠ ECT is first-line for benzodiazepine-resistant catatonia (response rate >80-90%) — obtain an urgent psychiatry consult for ECT evaluation rather than prolonging ineffective medical trials.

6 · Antipsychotics & NMS overlap

AVOID antipsychotics in active catatonia
Can worsen catatonia and precipitate malignant catatonia or NMS (which shares features with catatonia). When it's safe to restart

  • Excited catatonia not responding to benzodiazepines
ECT course
Usually 6-12 sessions, 3x/week initially; maintenance ECT may be needed. Obtain an urgent psychiatry consult for ECT evaluation.

3 · Management algorithm

Catatonia Suspected — BFCSI Screen — Screen with Bush-Francis Catatonia Screening Instrument. ≥2 signs = positive screen; DSM-5-TR generally needs ≥3 signs or a strong clinical syndrome. Can coexist with delirium.

Catatonia Unlikely — 0-1 signs. Consider severe depression, Parkinson's, locked-in syndrome, akinetic mutism, conversion disorder, malingering. Re-screen if suspicion persists.

Excited Catatonia — Emergency — Extreme agitation + autonomic instability; risk exhaustion, hyperthermia, death. Lorazepam 2 mg IV, repeat q5-10min to 8 mg ceiling. Avoid antipsychotics.

Lorazepam Challenge (2 mg IV) — Diagnostic and therapeutic test. Observe 10-15 min for increased speech, decreased rigidity, improved eye contact, spontaneous movement (~50% sign reduction = positive).

Positive Response — Catatonia strongly supported. Schedule lorazepam 2 mg IV/PO q4-6h, titrate (10-20+ mg/day may be needed). Taper slowly once stable — rapid taper risks relapse.

No Response — Repeat lorazepam 4 mg IV. If still no response after cumulative 6-8 mg, proceed directly to ECT — also reconsider locked-in syndrome, akinetic mutism, severe Parkinson's, or malignant catatonia.

Partial Response — Increase to lorazepam 2-4 mg IV q2h; total daily doses of 20-30 mg may be needed. If plateaued at 48-72h, refer for ECT; consider adjunctive memantine or amantadine.

Investigate Etiology — Medical (encephalitis, metabolic, autoimmune, endocrine, drug withdrawal), psychiatric (mood disorder, schizophrenia), or drug-induced. Workup: CBC/CMP/TSH/UA, urine drug screen, LP if encephalitis suspected, MRI, EEG, anti-NMDA receptor antibodies.

Medical Cause — Treat It — Anti-NMDA encephalitis: neurology consult, steroids/IVIG/plasmapheresis, tumor screen. Metabolic: correct electrolytes/glucose. Infectious: acyclovir or antibiotics. Continue lorazepam — catatonia may persist after the cause resolves.

Psychiatric Catatonia — Most common: major depressive disorder, then bipolar disorder; schizophrenia less common than historically thought. Continue lorazepam; treat mood disorder once catatonia resolves.

Drug-Induced Catatonia — Antipsychotic (especially clozapine) or benzodiazepine withdrawal, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, PCP/ketamine, synthetic cannabinoids, stimulant withdrawal. Discontinue/reinstate offending agent; continue lorazepam.

Avoid Antipsychotics — Antipsychotics can worsen catatonia and precipitate malignant catatonia or NMS. Stop any antipsychotic on board (weigh risk/benefit for clozapine). Safe to restart only after catatonia fully resolved 48-72h, low dose, slow titration, avoid high-potency D2 blockers.

ECT Referral — Indicated for lorazepam-resistant catatonia, malignant catatonia, life-threatening complications, or excited catatonia not responding to benzodiazepines. Response rate >80-90%, often 1-3 sessions; course usually 6-12 sessions, 3x/week initially. Urgent psychiatry consult.

Disposition — Admit All — All catatonia patients require admission. ICU if malignant features (fever >38°C, autonomic instability), excited catatonia with exhaustion, respiratory compromise, or severe dehydration/rhabdomyolysis. Otherwise psychiatry admission. Orders: scheduled lorazepam, DVT prophylaxis, NPO/supervised feeding if swallowing impaired, PT/OT, Foley if unable to void.
