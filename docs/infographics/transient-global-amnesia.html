Transient Global Amnesia: Recognize, Exclude Mimics, Dispose

Adults presenting to the ED with sudden-onset anterograde amnesia. Excludes patients with focal neurologic deficits, episodes lasting >24 hours, a witnessed seizure/post-ictal state, or recent head trauma — these require a stroke, seizure, or trauma workup instead of a TGA diagnosis. TGA has no medication protocol; this infographic is diagnostic/disposition-only.

⚠ Focal neurologic deficits — even transient — mean this is NOT TGA. Pursue stroke workup (non-contrast CT, MRI-DWI, CTA head/neck, ECG/telemetry) instead of diagnosing TGA.
⚠ Episode duration >24 hours is NOT TGA by definition — reassess for stroke or another diagnosis; do not extend the observation window past 24 hours expecting late resolution.
⚠ Do NOT diagnose TGA if there is recent head trauma — this is post-traumatic amnesia; assess with CT per the Canadian CT Head Rule or PECARN instead.
⚠ TGA requires ALL Hodges & Warlow criteria to be met (witnessed attack, clear-cut anterograde amnesia, no clouding of consciousness or loss of personal identity, no focal signs, no epileptic features, no recent head injury, resolution within 24 hours) — missing any one criterion means TGA is not confirmed.
⚠ Frequent or recurrent episodes are atypical for TGA (which rarely recurs) — reconsider transient epileptic amnesia (TEA) and pursue EEG plus neurology referral.

1 · Initial assessment

Transient Global Amnesia (TGA) — sudden-onset anterograde amnesia with repetitive questioning ("What day is it?" "Why am I here?"), preserved personal identity, and no focal neurologic deficits, resolving within 24 hours.

From a witness, get: exact onset time, the precipitant (physical exertion, emotional stress, Valsalva, or water immersion are common triggers), whether a seizure was witnessed, and whether there was recent head trauma.

2 · Diagnostic criteria & pathophysiology

#Hodges & Warlow criterion (ALL required)
1Attack witnessed by a capable observer
2Clear-cut anterograde amnesia during the attack
3No clouding of consciousness or loss of personal identity
4No focal neurologic signs during or after the attack
5No epileptic features
6No recent head injury
7Resolution within 24 hours

Supportive features: age typically 50-80 years, a precipitant is often identified, repetitive questioning is the hallmark, and retrograde amnesia (hours to days before the event) is variable.

Epidemiology: incidence 3-8 per 100,000/year, peak age 50-70 years, rare under 40 (consider epilepsy), slight female predominance.

Proposed mechanisms: transient hippocampal dysfunction, venous congestion (Valsalva, jugular reflux), cortical spreading depression — NOT ischemic stroke, despite DWI lesions.

Common triggers: physical exertion, emotional stress, pain, sexual intercourse, water immersion, medical procedures.

DWI-MRI: may show punctate CA1 hippocampal lesions, best seen 24-72 hours after onset — not required for diagnosis.

3 · Mimics & red flags

Stroke mimic — red flags
Red flags for posterior circulation stroke: focal neurologic deficits (even transient), vertigo, diplopia, dysarthria, visual field cut, ataxia, duration >24 hours, or prominent vascular risk factors. Thalamic/PCA stroke can cause anterograde amnesia similar to TGA but usually has other posterior-circulation signs.

Workup: non-contrast CT head, MRI with DWI (preferred), CTA head/neck, ECG plus telemetry, labs (glucose, CBC, BMP, lipids, A1c). If stroke is confirmed, follow the stroke protocol; if the workup is negative and symptoms are resolving, TGA remains possible.
Transient epileptic amnesia (TEA)
Suspect TEA if episodes are <1 hour (TGA usually 4-8 hours), recurrent (TGA rarely recurs), occur on waking, show subtle automatisms/lip-smacking, or there is a seizure history in a younger patient (<50). Workup: EEG (may need prolonged/sleep-deprived) and MRI with an epilepsy protocol; refer to neurology. Treatment: antiepileptics (levetiracetam, lamotrigine) and driving restrictions per state law.
Post-traumatic amnesia
Recent head injury is the key differentiator. Assess with CT head per the Canadian CT Head Rule or PECARN, GCS monitoring, and repeated neuro exams; consider MRI if deficits persist. Treat the underlying TBI, cognitive rest, return precautions.

Do NOT diagnose TGA if there is recent head trauma.
Broader differential (other TGA mimics)6 categories

Vascular: posterior circulation TIA/stroke, thalamic infarct, subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Epileptic: transient epileptic amnesia, post-ictal confusion, complex partial status.

Toxic/metabolic: hypoglycemia, Wernicke encephalopathy, drug intoxication (benzodiazepines, anticholinergics), carbon monoxide.

Psychiatric: dissociative (psychogenic) amnesia, malingering.

Infectious: herpes encephalitis (usually more confused, febrile).

Structural: tumor (gradual onset, not transient), hydrocephalus.

TGA is a diagnosis of exclusion — pursue this broader workup when the presentation is atypical for classic TGA.

5 · Workup strategy

Minimal workup (classic presentation): fingerstick glucose, basic labs if indicated (CBC, BMP), ECG (screen for atrial fibrillation).

Extended workup (atypical features): CT head, MRI with DWI (posterior-circulation stroke, hippocampal lesions), EEG if seizure is suspected, CTA if vascular risk factors are prominent.

StudyFindings / role
CT headUsually normal in TGA; rules out hemorrhage or mass; reasonable first-line for atypical cases
MRI-DWIGold standard if obtained — punctate CA1 hippocampal lesions in 70-80%, best seen 24-72 hours after onset; lesions resolve without infarction; not required for diagnosis
CTA/MRAOnly if stroke is a concern — TGA itself is not a vascular event

6 · Prognosis

Natural history: complete resolution within 24 hours (usually 4-8 hours); dense amnesia for the event itself persists; retrograde amnesia for hours-to-days before the event; full cognitive recovery otherwise.

Recurrence: annual rate 2-3%, lifetime 6-10% — frequent recurrence should prompt reconsidering TEA.

Long-term outcomes: no increased stroke risk, no increased dementia risk, no increased mortality; quality of life unaffected.

Reassurance is key — patients often will not remember the episode, and that is normal and expected.

7 · Disposition

Admit if
Persistent deficits beyond 24 hours, focal neurologic signs, stroke identified on imaging, recurrent episodes needing EEG, or diagnostic uncertainty.
Discharge if
Classic TGA criteria met, symptoms resolving or resolved, no focal deficits, a safe home environment, and reliable follow-up.
  1. Serial neuro exams every 1-2 hours while symptoms are ongoing (most resolve in 4-8 hours)
  2. Reassess at 4-6 hours — improving: continue observation and plan discharge; worsening or focal signs: CT/MRI and neurology consult
  3. Reassess at 24 hours — resolved: discharge with follow-up; not resolved: no longer TGA, admit for workup (max duration by definition: 24 hours)
  4. Keep the patient calm — repetitive questioning is distressing; keep family at bedside; give simple, repeated orientation and written instructions
  5. Document time of onset, duration, and resolution time
Discharge — TGA resolved
Criteria: symptoms fully resolved, no focal deficits, normal mental status, able to form new memories, safe home environment with an observer.

Recommendation: Discharge home. Classic TGA, benign prognosis. No driving 24-48h. PCP follow-up 1-2 weeks. Return for new symptoms.

TGA is benign, NOT a stroke — the patient will not remember the episode, and that is normal. Low recurrence risk (2-3%/year). No driving for 24-48 hours; no legal/financial decisions for 24 hours. PCP follow-up in 1-2 weeks; neurology if recurrent or atypical. Return for a new episode, focal weakness/numbness, speech difficulty, or severe headache.
Admit — stroke identified
This is NOT TGA — follow standard stroke protocols; admit to the stroke service/neuro ICU. Workup: MRI-DWI if not done, CTA head/neck, echo (TTE/TEE), telemetry, lipids/A1c, hypercoagulability workup if young. Treatment: antiplatelet therapy, statin, risk-factor modification; thrombolysis if within window and a deficit is present.

Recommendation: Admit stroke service. This is NOT TGA. Follow stroke protocol.
Admit — diagnostic uncertainty
Indications: symptoms >24 hours, recurrent episodes, atypical features, cannot rule out stroke/seizure, or a young patient (<50). Workup: MRI-DWI, EEG (routine or prolonged), continuous telemetry, neurology consult. Most will still be TGA, but the workup is needed for atypical cases.

Recommendation: Admit for observation and workup. MRI, EEG, neuro consult. Monitor for resolution.
