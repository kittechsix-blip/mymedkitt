Lithium Toxicity: The Number Lies, the Patient Doesn't

Adults ≥18 in the ED with known or suspected lithium toxicity (acute, acute-on-chronic, or chronic). Excludes pediatrics and dosing of definitive psychiatric disposition — coordinate pregnancy, breastfeeding, and restart decisions with OB, psychiatry, toxicology, and nephrology.

⚠ Hold lithium immediately. There is no antidote, and activated charcoal does not bind lithium.
⚠ Seizure, coma / decreased LOC, or life-threatening dysrhythmia → hemodialysis is recommended regardless of the lithium level.
⚠ Impaired kidney function plus lithium >4.0 mEq/L → hemodialysis recommended. [1]
⚠ One level never clears a patient. Draw serial lithium levels q4-6 h; chronic toxicity is often sicker than the number looks.
⚠ After dialysis, watch for rebound for at least 12 hours and repeat dialysis if the level climbs back up. [2]

1 · First 60 seconds

Why lithium is dangerous
Narrow therapeutic index, no antidote, renal-only clearance, delayed CNS distribution, and poor correlation between a single serum number and how sick the patient is. The dangerous phenotype is chronic or acute-on-chronic toxicity with neurologic findings — very sick at levels that look only moderate.[5]
  1. Airway, mental-status, and seizure check; place on the monitor.
  2. Hold lithium and stop interacting meds (NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, thiazide/loop diuretics).
  3. Get an ECG — look for QT prolongation, bradycardia, AV block, T-wave changes.
  4. Classify the exposure: acute ingestion, acute-on-chronic, or chronic accumulation.
  5. Send serum lithium now, renal function/electrolytes, and start serial lithium q4-6 h.[4]

2 · Read the level as a risk anchor, not a disposition

Serum categories anchor risk, but exposure pattern, renal function, the neuro exam, and the serial trend drive decisions. Chronic patients can be worse than their number.[3]

—EXTRIP dialysis tier
Hemodialysis RECOMMENDED now
EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment when impaired kidney function AND lithium >4.0 mEq/L, OR when decreased LOC, seizure, or life-threatening dysrhythmia is present regardless of level. Call nephrology + toxicology now; prepare intermittent hemodialysis. Do not delay dialysis waiting for a repeat level in a clinically severe patient.
Hemodialysis SUGGESTED — discuss now
EXTRIP suggests extracorporeal treatment when lithium >5.0 mEq/L, OR confusion is present, OR expected time to lithium <1.0 mEq/L is >36 hours despite optimal management. Discuss with toxicology/nephrology early rather than waiting for deterioration; chronic/neuro patients earn a lower threshold.
No dialysis trigger now — monitor
No EXTRIP trigger at these values. Hold lithium and interacting meds, give isotonic crystalloid if volume-depleted, repeat lithium q4-6 h until clearly falling, and re-examine the neuro status frequently. Escalate to a dialysis discussion if the level rises/plateaus, renal function worsens, confusion appears, or a sustained-release ingestion produces delayed absorption.

Tier key: 0 = no trigger · 1 = HD suggested · 2 = HD recommended. The tier is computed live from your inputs — it is a prompt to call the consultants, not a substitute for them.

If dialysis happens: modality + rebound
Intermittent hemodialysis is preferred for rapid removal; CRRT is acceptable if HD is unavailable or not tolerated. Continue until lithium <1.0 mEq/L or clear clinical improvement. Rebound is expected — lithium redistributes from tissue back into serum, so check serial levels for at least 12 hours after stopping and repeat dialysis if the level rebounds or symptoms recur.

4 · Decontamination & fluids

Charcoal does NOT bind lithium
Activated charcoal is not lithium therapy. Use it only when a clinically important co-ingestant is suspected and the airway is safe or protected. Never let charcoal delay serial levels, IV fluids, the poison-center call, nephrology, or dialysis evaluation.
Whole-bowel irrigation (polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution)
High-volume PEG-ELS PO/NG until rectal effluent is clear
PO / NG
For large acute ingestions — especially sustained/extended-release lithium — presenting early enough that tablets may still be in the GI tract, with a protected airway. Avoid in ileus, obstruction, uncontrolled vomiting, shock, or airway risk.

Primary non-dialysis treatment: stop lithium and restore intravascular volume/sodium with isotonic crystalloid for dehydration, GI losses, AKI from volume depletion, or chronic toxicity with sodium depletion. Goal is euvolemia and eunatremia with adequate urine output — this improves renal lithium clearance. Avoid forced diuresis, routine diuretics, and kayexalate as removal strategies; be cautious in CKD, CHF, pulmonary edema, and hypernatremia/nephrogenic DI.[4]

6 · Disposition & differential

Most symptomatic lithium toxicity is not an ED discharge. Disposition depends on symptoms, exposure pattern, renal function, serial trend, dialysis need, and psychiatric safety.[3]

Discharge only when ALL are truechecklist
  • No symptoms; normal, reliable mental status and gait.
  • No intentional self-harm or need for a psychiatric hold.
  • Lithium level falling on serial checks and generally <1.5 mEq/L.
  • Renal function stable and the patient can maintain hydration.
  • No sustained-release delayed-absorption concern and no unfixable interacting-med problem.
  • Clear plan with the prescriber to hold/restart lithium, with a follow-up lithium/renal check arranged.

6 · Disposition & differential

Most symptomatic lithium toxicity is **not** an ED discharge. Disposition depends on symptoms, exposure pattern, renal function, serial trend, dialysis need, and psychiatric safety.{{ref3}}

Discharge only when ALL are true

  • No symptoms; normal, reliable mental status and gait.
  • No intentional self-harm or need for a psychiatric hold.
  • Lithium level falling on serial checks and generally **<1.5 mEq/L**.
  • Renal function stable and the patient can maintain hydration.
  • No sustained-release delayed-absorption concern and no unfixable interacting-med problem.
  • Clear plan with the prescriber to hold/restart lithium, with a follow-up lithium/renal check arranged.

Admit / ICU triggers

  • Any neurologic symptoms, even with a modest level.
  • Chronic or acute-on-chronic toxicity.
  • AKI/CKD, dehydration needing IV therapy, or sodium abnormality / nephrogenic DI.
  • Rising or plateauing levels, or a sustained-release ingestion.
  • ECG abnormality, bradycardia, QT prolongation, or AV block.
  • **ICU / dialysis-capable** for seizure, coma, life-threatening dysrhythmia, EXTRIP criteria, or airway risk.
