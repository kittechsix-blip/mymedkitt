Aortic Emergencies: Dissection, Ruptured AAA, IMH, PAU — Recognize, Resuscitate, Classify, Consult

Adults ≥18 in the ED with suspected acute aortic dissection, ruptured/symptomatic/incidental aortic aneurysm, intramural hematoma (IMH), or penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (PAU). Excludes pediatrics, traumatic aortic injury, and detailed operative/anesthetic management beyond ED disposition.

⚠ Anti-impulse order matters (dissection): beta-blocker FIRST, vasodilator second. Target HR 60-70 bpm, then SBP 100-120 mmHg. A vasodilator given before rate control causes reflex tachycardia that raises aortic wall stress and can propagate the dissection.
⚠ Hypotensive dissection is a grave sign — check BP in all four extremities (unilateral low pressure may be pseudohypotension from limb-artery occlusion, not true shock). Pericardiocentesis for tamponade is small-volume and temporizing ONLY — do not delay emergent surgery for it. Avoid pure alpha-agonists (phenylephrine) — reflex bradycardia further reduces cardiac output.
⚠ Type A dissection = immediate surgical indication regardless of symptoms. Untreated mortality 1-2% per hour, 50% at 48 hours.
⚠ Malperfusion syndromes change management: coronary malperfusion mimics STEMI but needs emergent surgery, not PCI. Mesenteric malperfusion carries >50% mortality. Cerebral malperfusion (stroke in 5-10% of Type A) does NOT preclude surgery unless the stroke is massive.
⚠ Ruptured AAA: permissive hypotension, not aggressive resuscitation. Target SBP 70-90 mmHg, minimize crystalloid, activate a 1:1:1 massive transfusion protocol, and avoid intubation if possible — positive-pressure ventilation can precipitate arrest.
⚠ Do NOT obtain a CT scan in a hemodynamically unstable patient with suspected ruptured AAA — confirm with bedside ultrasound only and go straight to the OR.

1 · Recognize & risk-stratify

Same presentation, different diseases
Aortic dissection, ruptured/symptomatic aneurysm, IMH, and PAU overlap on exam but need different management. Misdiagnosis rate is up to 30% for both AAA and dissection. Ruptured AAA mortality is >80% overall, 41-50% even with surgical repair; untreated Type A dissection mortality is 1-2% per hour, 50% at 48 hours.[1][2][3][4]

High-risk features: abrupt-onset severe chest/back/abdominal pain, tearing/ripping quality, hypotension with abdominal or flank pain, pulse deficit or BP differential >20 mmHg between arms, new aortic regurgitation murmur, neurologic deficit (stroke, syncope, paraplegia). Classic AAA triad (hypotension + back pain + pulsatile mass) is present in <50% of cases.[3]

D-dimer rules OUT the low/intermediate-risk only
Cutoff 500 ng/mL. ADD-RS 0 + D-dimer negative: 99.9% sensitivity. ADD-RS ≤1 + D-dimer <500 ng/mL: 98.9% sensitivity, avoiding ~50% of CTAs. Do NOT delay imaging for the D-dimer result if ADD-RS ≥2 — go straight to CTA.[7][8]

AAA is defined as infrarenal aortic diameter >3 cm. Physical-exam sensitivity rises with size: 3.0-3.9 cm 29%, 4.0-4.9 cm 50%, ≥5.0 cm 76%. Bedside ultrasound is 100% sensitive for detecting an AAA but cannot reliably detect rupture — retroperitoneal blood is not visible.[3][13]

2 · Imaging & classification

CTA is the diagnostic test of choice
CTA sensitivity/specificity is approaching 100%. Order type and screen (6-10 units pRBCs), BMP, CBC, coags, troponin, and lactate simultaneously — do not delay imaging for labs. Bedside POCUS (SPEED protocol: pericardial effusion, aortic outflow tract >35 mm, intimal flap) is 92% sensitive, 91% specific and useful when transport is not feasible.[1][5]

Intramural hematoma (IMH): crescent-shaped wall thickening >5 mm with no intimal tear or false lumen. Type A IMH carries surgical mortality similar to dissection; Type B IMH is managed medically with ~10% spontaneous resolution.[12]

3 · Anti-impulse therapy — dissection

Beta-blocker FIRST, then vasodilator. Target HR 60-70 bpm with a beta-blocker, THEN SBP 100-120 mmHg with a vasodilator once the rate goal is met. A vasodilator given before rate control causes reflex tachycardia that raises wall stress and can propagate the dissection. Add IV opioid (morphine or fentanyl) for pain — persistent pain suggests ongoing dissection.[1][9]

Beta-blocker available — esmolol first-line
Esmolol: 500 mcg/kg load over 1 min, then infusion 50-200 mcg/kg/min, titrated to HR 60-70. Ultra-short half-life makes it the preferred agent in unstable patients. Labetalol 20-40-80-80-80 mg IV push q10min (cumulative max 300 mg) or Metoprolol 5 mg IV q5min (max 15 mg) are valid alternatives.[1][9]
Beta-blocker contraindicated — diltiazem substitute
Use if asthma/COPD precludes a beta-blocker. Diltiazem: 0.25 mg/kg IV over 2 min, then infusion 5-20 mg/hr.[1][9]

Hypotensive dissection

4 · Ruptured AAA resuscitation

Permissive hypotension is the cornerstone
Target SBP 70-90 mmHg while maintaining consciousness — this maintains the tamponade effect of a contained hematoma and avoids dilutional coagulopathy from crystalloid. Two large-bore IVs or central access; type O-negative blood for profound shock; massive transfusion protocol 1:1:1 pRBC:FFP:platelets; order 6-10 units; minimize crystalloid; keep core temp >36°C; avoid intubation if possible — positive-pressure ventilation removes compensatory mechanisms and can precipitate arrest.[13][14]
Unstable — no CT, straight to OR
Bedside ultrasound confirms AAA; do NOT obtain a CT scan — it delays definitive care. Call vascular surgery immediately.[3][13]
Stable — CTA for surgical planning
CT angiography (noncontrast + arterial phase) delineates anatomy, identifies contained vs. free rupture, and may show a hyperattenuating crescent sign (impending rupture).[3][13]

EVAR is preferred when anatomy allows (local anesthesia, bifurcation ≥17 mm, neck ≥15 mm, suitable iliofemoral access). OSR is used for unsuitable anatomy or free intraperitoneal hemorrhage. Endovascular balloon control bridges an unstable patient to definitive repair. Operative mortality: elective repair 5-10%, ruptured repair 41-50%.[13]

5 · Type-specific disposition

Type A → emergent surgery
All Type A dissections = immediate surgical indication regardless of symptoms, including tamponade, severe AR, malperfusion, or impending rupture. Untreated mortality 1-2% per hour, 50% at 48 hours; with surgery, 20% at 14 days. Consult cardiothoracic surgery STAT, cardiac anesthesia, and a perfusionist.[1][2][10]
Type B — uncomplicated vs. complicated
Uncomplicated (no malperfusion/rupture/refractory symptoms): 14-day mortality <10%, manage medically with ICU admission and serial imaging at 24-48h. Complicated (malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain/HTN): 14-day mortality ~30%, needs urgent TEVAR (preferred) or open repair. High-risk-for-progression features: max diameter >40 mm, false-lumen diameter >22 mm, hemorrhagic effusion, entry tear on the inner curvature.[1][2][11]

PAU: rupture risk reported up to 40%. Managed like Type B medically unless persistent symptoms, rupture, pseudoaneurysm, or progression to dissection/large IMH — then TEVAR (preferred) or open repair.[5][12]

Symptomatic unruptured aneurysm = impending rupture until proven otherwise
Admit for urgent surgical evaluation (CTA, type and crossmatch, NPO, BP control). Repair thresholds: ≥5.5 cm in men, ≥5.0 cm in women, or growth >0.5 cm in 6 months, or saccular/eccentric morphology.[3][15]

7 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

**Do NOT give a vasodilator before a beta-blocker in dissection** — reflex tachycardia raises wall stress and can propagate the tear.

**Do NOT obtain a CT scan in a hemodynamically unstable patient with suspected ruptured AAA** — confirm with bedside ultrasound and go straight to the OR.

**Do NOT give large-volume crystalloid resuscitation in ruptured AAA** — permissive hypotension (SBP 70-90 mmHg) preserves the tamponade effect.

**Do NOT intubate a ruptured AAA patient unless absolutely necessary** — positive-pressure ventilation removes compensatory mechanisms and can precipitate arrest.

**Do NOT delay CTA for a D-dimer result** when ADD-RS ≥2 — high-risk patients need immediate imaging.

**Do NOT use a pure alpha-agonist (phenylephrine) for hypotensive dissection** — reflex bradycardia further reduces cardiac output.

**Avoid NG tube placement in suspected thoracic aneurysm/dissection** — risk of esophageal perforation.

**Do NOT treat coronary malperfusion (STEMI-mimic pattern) with primary PCI** — it needs emergent surgery, not the cath lab.
