Deep Neck Space Infections — ED Recognition & Management

ED patients with suspected or confirmed deep neck space infection — Ludwig angina, retropharyngeal abscess, parapharyngeal space infection, Lemierre syndrome, or peritonsillar abscess. Airway assessment for stridor, trismus, dysphagia, and drooling is the first step for every presentation. Safe ED discharge is considered ONLY for a simple, successfully drained peritonsillar abscess with reliable follow-up — every other deep neck infection requires inpatient admission.

⚠ Airway management is the absolute priority — secure the airway BEFORE detailed workup if any signs of compromise (stridor, respiratory distress, inability to lie flat, drooling).
⚠ Stridor and cyanosis are LATE, ominous signs of impending respiratory failure — do not wait for them before escalating airway management.
⚠ In Ludwig angina, avoid sedation/paralysis before airway control — it can cause complete airway collapse and make both intubation AND surgical airway extremely difficult.
⚠ CT neck with IV contrast has sensitivity ~100% but specificity only ~45% for abscess vs phlegmon — it may overcall abscess; clinical correlation is essential.
⚠ Anticoagulation in Lemierre syndrome is NOT routinely recommended for uncomplicated cases — no consensus guidelines exist.
⚠ Admit ALL deep neck infections for IV antibiotics and monitoring — discharge is considered ONLY for a simple, successfully drained peritonsillar abscess with reliable follow-up.

Recognition & Presentation

Deep neck infections (DNIs) are potentially life-threatening infections of the cervical fascial spaces that can progress rapidly to airway obstruction, sepsis, and mediastinitis. Key principle: airway management takes absolute priority — secure the airway BEFORE detailed workup if any signs of compromise.

40-60%Mortality — untreated (with mediastinitis)
8-16%Mortality — aggressive modern management
  • Odontogenic (dental infections) — 40-60% of cases
  • Tonsillar/pharyngeal — 20-30%
  • Salivary gland infections
  • Iatrogenic (post-instrumentation)
  • Trauma / foreign body

Common presenting symptoms: neck pain and swelling, odynophagia/dysphagia, fever and malaise, trismus, voice changes ("hot potato" voice), and drooling.

  • Stridor or respiratory distress
  • Tripod positioning
  • Crepitus (subcutaneous emphysema)
  • Bilateral neck swelling ("bull neck")
  • Floor of mouth elevation
  • Rapidly progressing symptoms
  • Neck erythema, induration, tenderness
  • Fluctuance (suggests abscess)
  • Dental caries or periapical abscess
  • Tonsillar asymmetry or bulging
  • Limited neck mobility (torticollis)

Neck Fascial Spaces (reference)

The neck contains interconnected fascial compartments that facilitate infection spread: submandibular (includes sublingual & submental), parapharyngeal (lateral to pharynx), retropharyngeal (posterior to pharynx), danger space (between alar and prevertebral fascia), and prevertebral. The "danger space" extends from skull base to diaphragm, permitting rapid spread into the mediastinum. The anatomic space involved determines clinical presentation, complications, and management approach.

Emergent Airway Management

AIRWAY IS THE ABSOLUTE PRIORITY. Deep neck infections create a "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate" risk.

  1. Maintain spontaneous ventilation as long as possible
  2. Avoid sedation/paralysis (can cause complete collapse)
  3. Have surgical airway immediately available
  4. Get expert help (ENT, Anesthesia) if time permits
  5. Mark cricothyroid membrane BEFORE any attempt
  6. Preferred: awake fiberoptic intubation (upright, topical anesthesia, nasal approach)
  7. Alternative: awake tracheostomy under local anesthesia
  8. Last resort: surgical airway (cricothyrotomy)
  1. Assess stability: can the patient maintain the airway currently?
  2. Prepare regardless of stability: ENT/Anesthesia early, surgical airway tray, mark landmarks, suction ready
  3. If time permits: awake fiberoptic intubation in OR with double setup (tracheostomy ready)
  4. If imminent collapse: awake tracheostomy under local, or cricothyrotomy if cannot delay
  5. Post-intubation: do NOT extubate until swelling resolves (may be several days)
Avoid unless absolutely necessary
RSI (risk of complete collapse), direct laryngoscopy (poor visualization), blind nasal intubation (risk of abscess rupture).

Imaging

CT neck with IV contrast is the gold standard. Findings: rim-enhancing hypodense collection (abscess), scalloping of the abscess wall (= purulence at surgery), soft tissue swelling and fat stranding, obliteration of fat planes, gas within soft tissues (necrotizing infection), airway narrowing/deviation. Lateral neck X-ray (prevertebral soft tissue >7 mm at C2 or >22 mm at C6) has limited utility — CT preferred. MRI: superior soft tissue detail but impractical acutely.

CT overcalls abscess
Sensitivity ~100%, specificity only ~45% for abscess vs phlegmon — may overestimate abscess; clinical correlation is essential.

  • Oral/Maxillofacial Surgery — odontogenic source, Ludwig angina
  • Cardiothoracic Surgery — mediastinal extension
  • Interventional Radiology — CT-guided drainage, vascular complications
  • Critical Care — severe sepsis, post-operative management

Disposition

Disposition | Criteria

ICU admission | Airway compromise, severe sepsis, necrotizing infection, post-operative

Surgical ward/floor | Stable airway, awaiting or post-drainage, IV antibiotics

Discharge (simple PTA only) | PTA successfully drained, tolerating PO, reliable follow-up

For any airway compromise or recent advanced airway, post-operative monitoring, severe sepsis/septic shock, necrotizing infection, mediastinal involvement, multi-organ dysfunction, or need for vasopressors. Do NOT extubate until swelling resolves and cuff leak test is positive.

Stable airway, post-drainage with drains in place, IV antibiotics, no necrotizing infection or mediastinitis. Step-down when afebrile 48-72h, improving exam, tolerating PO, drains removed. Follow-up: ENT, ID, and dental as indicated.

Only if abscess successfully drained, pain controlled with oral meds, tolerating PO, no extension to deeper spaces, reliable follow-up, no immunocompromise. ENT follow-up in 1-2 weeks; consider elective tonsillectomy for recurrent PTA. Return precautions: fever, worsening pain, difficulty breathing/swallowing, neck swelling.

Amoxicillin-clavulanate: 875/125 mg PO BID x 10 days PO — Discharge regimen for successfully drained simple PTA.

Clindamycin: 300 mg PO QID x 10 days PO — If penicillin allergy.

Management algorithm

Deep Neck Space Infections — Potentially life-threatening infections of cervical fascial spaces — can progress to airway obstruction, sepsis, mediastinitis. Airway management takes absolute priority.

Clinical Presentation — Neck pain/swelling, odynophagia, fever, trismus, voice change, drooling. Red flags: stridor, tripod position, crepitus, bull neck, floor of mouth elevation.

Airway compromise present? — Stridor and cyanosis are LATE signs. Earlier signs: drooling, muffled voice, tripod positioning, difficulty lying flat.

Emergent Airway Management — Maintain spontaneous ventilation, avoid sedation/paralysis, surgical airway immediately available. Preferred: awake fiberoptic intubation.

Neck Fascial Spaces — Submandibular, parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, danger space, prevertebral. The danger space permits rapid spread to mediastinum.

Airway Decision Algorithm — Assess stability, prepare regardless (ENT/Anesthesia, surgical tray, mark landmarks). Avoid RSI, direct laryngoscopy, blind nasal intubation.

Clinical Syndrome? — Based on presentation and suspected anatomic involvement, select the most likely clinical syndrome.

Ludwig Angina — Bilateral submandibular cellulitis (not abscess), floor of mouth elevation, bull neck. Odontogenic origin. Mortality ~8% with modern management.

Retropharyngeal Abscess — Between pharynx and prevertebral fascia. Peds 6mo-6yr most common. CT scalloping predicts drainable purulence; >2cm typically needs drainage.

Parapharyngeal Infection — Contains carotid artery, IJV, CN IX-XII. Unilateral swelling below mandible angle, trismus. CT with contrast essential.

Lemierre Syndrome — Septic thrombophlebitis of IJV following pharyngitis. >90% Fusobacterium necrophorum. Cord sign in 25-45%.

Peritonsillar Abscess — Most common deep neck infection. Unilateral swelling, uvular deviation, trismus. Needle aspiration 85-90% successful. Recurrence 10-15%.

Ludwig: Awake Fiberoptic Intubation — Direct laryngoscopy often fails. Awake fiberoptic preferred; avoid sedation/paralysis — can cause complete airway collapse.

RPA: Medical vs Surgical Drainage — Medical management may succeed for small (<2cm) collections in children. Surgical drainage if >2cm, no improvement 48-72h, airway compromise.

Lemierre: 4-6 Week Antibiotics — Pip-tazo or Amp-sulbactam 4-6 weeks. Anticoagulation controversial — not routine. Mortality 2-4% with modern treatment.

CT Neck with IV Contrast — Gold standard. Sensitivity ~100%, specificity ~45% for abscess vs phlegmon — may overcall abscess.

Empiric IV Antibiotics — Ampicillin-sulbactam 3g IV q6h OR Clindamycin 900mg IV q8h. Add vancomycin 15-20mg/kg IV q8-12h if MRSA risk.

Severe/Necrotizing: Broaden Coverage — Pip-tazo or Meropenem PLUS Vancomycin PLUS Clindamycin (toxin suppression). Duration 2-6 weeks based on extent.

Surgical Drainage — Required for most deep neck abscesses: frank abscess, fluctuance, failure at 48-72h, airway compromise, necrotizing infection, mediastinal extension.

Life-Threatening Complications — Descending mediastinitis, airway obstruction, sepsis/DIC, IJV thrombosis, carotid erosion, septic emboli, aspiration, osteomyelitis.

Descending Necrotizing Mediastinitis — Most feared complication. Predictors: age ≥55, NLR ≥13, CRP ≥30, diabetes. Mortality 16-41% with aggressive management.

Multidisciplinary Consultation — ENT, Anesthesia, Infectious Disease essential; Oral/Maxillofacial, Cardiothoracic, IR, Critical Care as needed.

Disposition? — Determine appropriate disposition based on clinical severity and airway status.

ICU Admission — Airway compromise, severe sepsis, necrotizing infection, post-operative. Do NOT extubate until cuff leak test positive.

Surgical Ward Admission — Stable airway, post-drainage, IV antibiotics. Step-down when afebrile 48-72h, tolerating PO, drains removed.

Discharge (Simple PTA only) — Successfully drained, tolerating PO, reliable follow-up, no immunocompromise. Oral antibiotics + ENT follow-up 1-2 weeks.
