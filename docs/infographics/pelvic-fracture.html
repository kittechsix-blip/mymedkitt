Pelvic Fracture: Stabilize, Localize, Classify, Fix

Adults ≥18 with a high-energy pelvic fracture in the ED/trauma bay. Excludes isolated low-energy fragility fractures (fall-from-standing in osteoporotic patients), pediatric pelvic trauma, and open pelvic wounds requiring immediate surgical control (binder contraindicated at wound site).

⚠ Apply the pelvic binder immediately at the level of the greater trochanters — NOT the iliac crests.
⚠ TXA 1 g IV load within 3 hours of injury, then 1 g over 8 hours — mortality benefit is time-dependent (CRASH-2). Given after 3 hours, TXA may increase mortality.
⚠ Do NOT rock the pelvis to assess stability — may dislodge clots and worsen hemorrhage. Assess stability ONCE only.
⚠ FAST positive + hemodynamically unstable → emergent laparotomy, NOT angiography — do not delay surgery for angio.
⚠ 90% of pelvic hemorrhage is venous (responds to binder + resuscitation); only 10% is arterial and needs angioembolization. A negative FAST does NOT rule out pelvic hemorrhage — it is typically retroperitoneal.
⚠ REBOA Zone 3: target <30 min, max 60 min total occlusion — a bridge to definitive control only, it does NOT stop hemorrhage.
⚠ Blood at the urethral meatus, a high-riding prostate, or scrotal/perineal hematoma → retrograde urethrogram BEFORE any Foley attempt. Do NOT attempt blind urethral catheterization.
⚠ Intraperitoneal bladder rupture requires surgical repair; extraperitoneal rupture is usually managed non-operatively with Foley drainage alone.

1 · Assessment & primary algorithm

The problem
High-energy pelvic fractures carry mortality of 5-16% (up to 50% if hemodynamically unstable).[1][2] Mechanism predicts pattern: lateral MVC impact → lateral compression (LC); frontal MVC impact → anterior-posterior compression (APC, "open book"); fall from height → vertical shear (VS); motorcycle/pedestrian → combined mechanism (CM).
Exam & imaging detailAP pelvis, FAST, CT indications, symphysis threshold

AP pelvis X-ray — part of the trauma series; identifies gross instability, pubic symphysis diastasis, sacral fractures. Pubic symphysis diastasis >2.5 cm = significant instability.[1][2][5]

FAST exam — evaluates for hemoperitoneum. Positive FAST in an unstable patient → OR for laparotomy. Negative FAST does NOT rule out pelvic hemorrhage (retroperitoneal).

CT pelvis with IV contrast — gold standard for fracture characterization; identifies arterial contrast blush (active hemorrhage); CT cystogram if bladder injury suspected. Indicated for all hemodynamically stable patients with a pelvic fracture on X-ray, and unstable patients only if they can be stabilized for transport.

  • Do NOT rock the pelvis to assess stability — assess ONCE by gentle compression.
  • Do NOT delay laparotomy for angiography in a FAST-positive unstable patient.
  • Do NOT attempt blind Foley catheterization if urethral-injury signs are present — RUG first.
  • Do NOT over-tighten the binder in pure LC (lateral compression) injuries — may worsen displacement.
  • Do NOT assume a negative FAST rules out pelvic hemorrhage — it is typically retroperitoneal.
  • Do NOT give TXA more than 3 hours after injury — may increase mortality rather than help.

1 · Assessment & primary algorithm

High-energy pelvic fractures carry mortality of 5-16% (up to 50% if hemodynamically unstable).[1][2] Mechanism predicts pattern: lateral MVC impact → lateral compression (LC); frontal MVC impact → anterior-posterior compression (APC, "open book"); fall from height → vertical shear (VS); motorcycle/pedestrian → combined mechanism (CM).

High-energy pelvic fracture suspected — Primary survey (ATLS): 2 large-bore IVs, type & crossmatch. Assess pelvic stability ONCE by gentle AP/lateral compression — do NOT rock the pelvis, do NOT repeat the exam (may dislodge clots). Screen for urethral-injury signs (blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, scrotal/perineal hematoma, inability to void) as part of the same initial exam, BEFORE any Foley attempt.

Hemodynamic status? — Stable: SBP >90, HR <100, responsive to crystalloid. Transient responder: initial response then recurrent hypotension. Unstable: SBP <90 despite resuscitation, HR >120, signs of shock.

Tighten binder, activate MTP, TXA if <3h, reassess q15-30min — May convert to stable or unstable category. Low threshold for intervention. If deteriorates, treat as unstable.

Complete imaging workup (CT pelvis + contrast, ± cystogram) — Assess for associated injuries: bladder (5-10%), urethral (5-25% in males), rectal (1-5%). Classification guides management.

Pelvic binder at greater trochanters + TXA (<3h) + activate MTP — Binder positioned at greater trochanters, NOT iliac crests. TXA 1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g IV over 8h if <3h from injury. MTP criteria: unstable + shock, predicted >10 units pRBC, or ABC Score ≥2.

FAST exam result? — Positive FAST = likely intra-abdominal hemorrhage source. Negative FAST = hemorrhage likely retroperitoneal/pelvic (does NOT rule out pelvic hemorrhage).

FAST+ → emergent laparotomy (NOT angiography) — Control intra-abdominal hemorrhage first (damage-control surgery). Consider preperitoneal packing intraoperatively if pelvic bleeding continues; external fixation can be applied in the OR. Do NOT delay laparotomy for angiography.

Responds to binder + MTP? — 90% of pelvic hemorrhage is venous — responds to binder + resuscitation. 10% is arterial (contrast blush on CT) and needs angioembolization.

Maintain binder + MTP; CT pelvis with contrast when stable — No contrast blush → continue supportive care. Contrast blush → angioembolization. Then proceed to fracture classification.

Refractory hemorrhage — which control method? — Choice depends on suspected source (venous vs arterial) and resource availability (OR, IR suite, trained REBOA personnel).

Preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) — Extraperitoneal approach, space of Retzius, 3 packs/side toward SI joints. 80-90% hemostasis for venous bleeding. Pack removal in 24-48h. If bleeding continues after PPP, arterial source likely — proceed to angioembolization.

Angioembolization — Selective internal iliac artery embolization, bilateral if diffuse. 80-100% success for arterial hemorrhage. Complications: gluteal necrosis (rare), impotence (bilateral), re-bleeding 5-10%.

REBOA Zone 3 (bridge, <30 min target, max 60 min) — Common femoral access, balloon to Zone 3 (infrarenal aorta). Increases central perfusion pressure, temporizes only — does NOT stop hemorrhage. Bridge to PPP, angioembolization, or OR for external fixation. Partial REBOA (pREBOA) allows longer inflation with lower ischemic burden.

Tile classification (stability)? — Type A: stable (LC-I, isolated rami fractures). Type B: rotationally unstable, vertically stable (APC-II, LC-II). Type C: rotationally AND vertically unstable (APC-III, VS, LC-III).

Tile A — conservative management — Weight-bearing as tolerated, multimodal analgesia, DVT prophylaxis, PT. Healing expected in 6-12 weeks. Orthopedic follow-up 1-2 weeks.

Tile B — external fixation / ORIF — Symphyseal plating, percutaneous SI screw, or external fixation. Surgery indicated if diastasis >2.5cm, displaced sacral fracture, associated acetabular fracture, or open fracture. Definitive ORIF typically day 5-14.

Tile C — emergent ortho, damage control, staged ORIF — Binder/traction/external fixation acutely, hemorrhage control (PPP/angio) next, definitive ORIF when physiology normalized (day 5-14). Associated injuries common: lumbosacral plexus, Morel-Lavallée degloving, open fractures.

Urethral injury signs present? — Blood at urethral meatus, high-riding/boggy prostate on DRE, scrotal or perineal hematoma, inability to void, or pelvic fracture pattern with symphysis diastasis / pubic rami fractures.

Do NOT attempt Foley — RUG first — Retrograde urethrogram before any catheterization attempt. Normal RUG → gentle single Foley attempt (16-18 Fr). Extravasation (partial tear) → single gentle attempt by experienced provider OR suprapubic catheter; urology consult; stricture risk 50-70%. Complete disruption → mandatory suprapubic catheter, do NOT attempt urethral catheterization, emergent urology, delayed urethroplasty 3-6 months.

Foley placement safe — No urethral-injury signs — proceed with Foley. Monitor for gross hematuria (bladder-injury indicator); if present, CT cystogram (bladder injury occurs in 5-10% of pelvic fractures).

3 · Classification & definitive management

Emergent orthopedic consultation. Damage-control stabilization (binder, traction, external fixation) acutely; definitive ORIF when physiology normalized.

Enoxaparin: 40 mg SC once daily SC — Start when hemostasis is achieved; continue until ambulatory or 4 weeks. Mechanical prophylaxis (not chemical) if active bleeding.

Multimodal Analgesia: Acetaminophen 1g q6h + Ibuprofen 400-600mg q6h + PRN oxycodone PO — Avoid the NSAID if renal injury. Add a bowel regimen — opioids + immobility cause constipation. Expected healing: 6-12 weeks.

Enoxaparin: 40 mg SC once daily SC — Pre-op framing: start when hemostasis is achieved; mechanical prophylaxis only if active bleeding — no twice-daily LMWH with an indwelling neuraxial catheter.

Enoxaparin: 40 mg SC once daily SC — Post-op framing (per the definitive-management pathway for unstable fractures requiring fixation): start 24-48h after fixation, once hemostasis is confirmed — later than the pre-op Tile B timing above.
