GI Foreign Body — Object Identification to Disposition

Adults and children in the ED with a confirmed or suspected ingested GI foreign body (esophagus, stomach, or beyond). Excludes airway foreign bodies and rectal/vaginal foreign bodies. Esophageal food-bolus impaction is described in myMedKitt but is not reachable from this consult's entry point — see build notes.

⚠ Esophageal button battery is a surgical emergency — target endoscopy <2 hours from ED arrival; severe mucosal injury occurs in <2 hours and perforation risk rises sharply after 2-4 hours.
⚠ Honey/sucralfate bridge dosing is ONLY for confirmed esophageal battery, <12 hours since ingestion — it must NEVER delay endoscopy.
⚠ Honey is CONTRAINDICATED under 12 months of age (infant botulism risk) — use sucralfate instead.
⚠ Sharp objects carry a 15-35% perforation risk if allowed to pass spontaneously — esophageal sharp objects need endoscopy <6 hours, gastric sharp objects <24 hours.
⚠ Multiple magnets (or a magnet plus metal) are a surgical emergency — pressure necrosis through bowel walls can cause fistula and perforation; do not manage expectantly.
⚠ Any sign of perforation (severe pain, subcutaneous emphysema, fever, peritonitis, free air) overrides conservative management at any point — obtain CT and surgical consultation immediately.

4 · Button battery

Esophageal button battery is a surgical emergency. Electric current generates liquefactive necrosis — severe mucosal injury occurs in <2 hours, and perforation risk increases after 2-4 hours. Can fistulize to trachea or aorta (fatal).

Honey
10 mL every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses
PO
Only if age ≥12 months, <12 hours since ingestion, esophageal location confirmed on imaging, and able to swallow safely. Bridge therapy only — do NOT delay endoscopy.
Sucralfate
10 mL (1 g) every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses
PO
Alternative if honey unavailable. Bridge therapy only — do NOT delay endoscopy.

5 · Sharp objects

Sharp objects have a 15-35% perforation risk if allowed to pass spontaneously — needles, pins, toothpicks, fish/chicken bones, glass fragments, razor blades, dental hardware.

Esophageal sharp — perforation signs
Severe chest/neck pain, subcutaneous emphysema, fever, tachycardia, pneumomediastinum on imaging. If suspected → CT neck/chest/abdomen, surgical consultation.
Distal sharp — surgical indications
Surgery indicated for signs of perforation (fever, peritonitis, free air), failure to progress (same location >3 days), obstruction, or persistent abdominal pain.

6 · Multiple magnets

Multiple magnets or a magnet plus a metal object attract through bowel walls, causing pressure necrosis, fistula, obstruction, and perforation. If all objects are in the stomach → urgent endoscopic removal. If beyond stomach or in different locations → surgical consultation, likely operative removal. A single magnet alone is low risk and managed like a blunt object — confirm no other magnets or metal were ingested.

7 · Blunt objects

Most blunt objects pass spontaneously: <2 cm width (adult pylorus ~2-2.5 cm) and <5-6 cm length (duodenal sweep) — coins, small toys, marbles. Objects at risk of retention: >2 cm width or >5 cm length, esophageal location, or prior GI surgery/strictures.

  • Coins are the most common pediatric esophageal foreign body, usually at the cricopharyngeus
  • Foley catheter technique: fluoroscopy guidance, airway protection equipment ready — only for objects <24h, coin-like, cooperative patient
  • Endoscopy preferred if irregular shape, ingestion >24h, prior esophageal pathology, or failed Foley attempt

Remove endoscopically if sharp (any size), button battery ≥20mm or symptomatic, multiple magnets, or object >2 cm width or >5 cm length, or fails to pass in 3-4 weeks. May observe if blunt <2cm width/<5cm length, single small magnet (no metal), and asymptomatic — weekly X-rays.

95% of objects that reach the small bowel pass spontaneously. Regular diet (high fiber may help), strain stools to confirm passage, weekly X-rays for concerning objects — most pass within 1 week. Return/surgical intervention if the object fails to progress >3 days (same location), or if abdominal pain, vomiting, distension, fever, peritoneal signs, or bloody stools develop.

8 · Disposition

Emergent intervention
Indications: esophageal button battery, airway compromise, signs of perforation, complete esophageal obstruction with aspiration risk. Actions: NPO, IV access, GI and/or surgical consultation STAT, transfer if endoscopy unavailable; honey/sucralfate while awaiting for battery. Emergent GI/surgical consultation. NPO. Endoscopy within 2-6 hours depending on object. Transfer if capability unavailable.
Urgent intervention
Indications: esophageal blunt object, esophageal food bolus (partial obstruction), gastric sharp object, gastric button battery (high-risk features), multiple magnets in stomach. Admission criteria: awaiting endoscopy, unable to tolerate PO, anticoagulation requiring reversal, high-risk comorbidities. GI consultation. Admit for endoscopy within 24 hours. NPO. IV fluids.
Discharge with observation
Criteria: object passed to small bowel/colon, blunt and small (<2cm wide, <5cm long), asymptomatic, no high-risk features. Discharge with stool straining instructions. Return precautions for obstruction/perforation signs. X-ray if not passed in 1 week. If sharp object, weekly X-rays until passed.
