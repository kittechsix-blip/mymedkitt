Tracheostomy Emergencies — ED Management

Patients with an existing tracheostomy or laryngectomy presenting in acute respiratory distress — obstruction, dislodgement, or bleeding — in the ED or inpatient setting. Based on the NTSP (National Tracheostomy Safety Project) Emergency Tracheostomy/Laryngectomy Algorithm.

⚠ Laryngectomy = stoma is the ONLY airway — CANNOT be intubated from above; do not attempt oral/nasal intubation
⚠ Fresh tracheostomy (<7 days): do NOT attempt blind reinsertion — high risk of false passage, pneumomediastinum, complete airway loss
⚠ Late tracheostomy bleeding (>72h, especially 7–21 days post-op) = treat as tracheo-innominate fistula sentinel bleed until proven otherwise — do NOT remove the tracheostomy tube
⚠ Capnography waveform is the gold standard for confirming placement — do NOT rely on misting or SpO2 alone

First actions

NTSP Emergency Algorithm: tracheostomy patient in distress. Call for expert airway help, then work the first actions simultaneously while you assess whether this is a tracheostomy (upper airway may still be patent) or a laryngectomy (stoma is the ONLY airway — no connection between mouth and lungs). Check the bedhead sign: red = laryngectomy (stoma only), green = tracheostomy (dual airway).

  1. Call for expert airway help
  2. Apply high-flow O2 to BOTH face AND stoma
  3. Assess: tracheostomy vs laryngectomy (check bedhead sign or ask patient/family)
Dual airway vs stoma-only airway
Tracheostomy = upper airway may be patent, can oxygenate from above. Laryngectomy = NO connection between oropharynx and airway — CANNOT be intubated from above.

Tracheostomy or laryngectomy?

If uncertain, attempt to feel airflow from the mouth during ventilation — no airflow suggests laryngectomy. Laryngectomy = total surgical removal of the larynx; cannot be intubated from above. Tracheostomy = tube through an intact larynx; upper airway may be patent.

Laryngectomy: stoma is the ONLY airway
Apply O2 directly to the stoma (pediatric face mask or trach collar). If not breathing: BVM over the stoma with a pediatric face mask. Remove devices/dressings, suction gently. If a tube is present: deflate cuff, remove inner cannula, suction. Do NOT attempt oral/nasal intubation. If tube replacement is needed, use a tracheostomy tube or a size 6.0 cuffed ETT through the stoma. Call ENT/surgeon immediately.

Initial assessment: problem type?

Look, listen, and feel at both the mouth AND the stoma. Apply waveform capnography to assess ventilation.

ProblemKey signs
ObstructionUnable to pass suction catheter; high resistance to BVM ventilation; visible secretions/blood in tube
DislodgementTube appears malpositioned or loose; subcutaneous emphysema; cannot ventilate despite clear lumen
BleedingBlood from stoma or tube; sentinel bleed; recent trach placement (<3 weeks)

Tracheostomy types & equipment

Bedside equipment for every trach patient. Immediate access: spare tracheostomy tube (same size + one size smaller), spare inner cannula, suction with appropriate catheters, 10 mL syringe for cuff deflation, stitch cutters/scissors, water-soluble lubricant, tracheal dilators. Nearby: BVM with pediatric face mask, flexible bronchoscope, size 6.0 cuffed ETT, difficult airway cart, capnography.

Cuffed tubes create a seal for mechanical ventilation; uncuffed allow speech with finger occlusion. Inner cannula: always remove first when troubleshooting obstruction. Fenestrated tubes allow airflow through the cords for speech/weaning — use a non-fenestrated inner cannula for suctioning; fenestrations can cause granulation tissue. Speaking valves (Passy-Muir): deflate the cuff before placing; remove immediately if respiratory distress occurs.

ID (inner diameter) corresponds roughly to ETT size; OD (outer diameter) is critical for stoma fit. Common adult sizes: female 6.0–7.0 ID, male 7.0–8.0 ID. Shiley, Portex, and Bivona are common brands and are NOT interchangeable. If the same brand/model is unavailable, use one size smaller trach or an appropriate ETT — ETT through stoma: size ~6.0 cuffed fits most adults. Do not advance too far (risk of right mainstem). Water-soluble lubricant only.

6.0 cuffed ETTETT size for stoma (if matching trach tube unavailable)

Obstruction management

  1. Remove external devices (speaking valve, cap, HME filter, trach collar/humidity device)
  2. Remove inner cannula — pull out, inspect, reassess ventilation immediately
  3. Pass suction catheter — if it passes easily, suction and reassess; if it cannot pass, obstruction is complete
  4. Deflate the cuff (if present) — may relieve a false-passage obstruction
  5. Remove the entire tracheostomy tube if all above fails — oxygenate via face or stoma

Reassess after working through the algorithm. Improved = suction catheter passes, capnography waveform present, patient oxygenating. Common causes: mucus plug (most common), blood clot, granulation tissue, false passage/displacement, tracheal stenosis.

Once cleared: replace the inner cannula (cleaned or new), confirm ventilation with capnography, humidify to prevent recurrence, and document. Recurrent obstructions warrant bronchoscopy, ENT/pulmonology consult, and consideration of tube downsizing or change.

Refractory obstruction: rescue airway
Tube removed but still cannot ventilate. Mature stoma (>7 days): tracheal dilators, replace with a smaller trach tube or ETT through the stoma (consider fiberoptic guidance); if that fails, oral intubation with the cuff advanced past the stoma. Fresh stoma (<7 days) — HIGH RISK: do NOT attempt blind reinsertion; oxygenate via face (BVM, oral intubation), occlude the stoma, call the surgeon immediately. Cannot intubate/cannot oxygenate: consider an LMA over the stoma or emergency front-of-neck access (new cricothyrotomy).

Dislodgement: fresh or mature trach?

The critical distinction is the age of the tracheostomy. Risk factors for difficult reinsertion: obesity, short/thick neck, high ventilatory requirements, agitation, percutaneous (vs surgical) technique.

7 daysFresh-trach threshold (blind reinsertion contraindicated below this)
Fresh trach (<7 days): do NOT replace
Blind reinsertion risks false passage into the pretracheal space, subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, complete airway loss, and death. Call for help (anesthesia, ENT, surgery); oxygenate via FACE (BVM or oral intubation); occlude the stoma with gauze/finger during face ventilation; advance the ETT cuff PAST the stoma level. Definitive management: surgeon replacement under direct visualization, or fiberoptic guidance through the stoma.

Generally a safe bedside procedure. Gather equipment (same size + one size smaller, lubricant, suction); oxygenate well; position with slight neck extension; deflate the cuff completely. Remove the old tube along its curve, inspect the stoma briefly, lubricate the new tube, insert along the natural tract angle (posterior then inferior), inflate the cuff, and confirm with capnography. If difficulty: tracheal dilators, one size smaller tube, bougie/fiberoptic guidance — do NOT force, it may create a false passage.

Capnography waveform is the gold standard. Also check: suction catheter passes easily past the tube tip, bilateral chest rise, bilateral breath sounds. Do NOT rely solely on misting, phonation, or SpO2 (delayed indicator). If placement is uncertain: do not inflate the cuff, oxygenate via face while reassessing, consider fiberoptic visualization of tracheal rings, and keep a low threshold for oral intubation. False-passage signs: increasing resistance to ventilation, subcutaneous emphysema, no capnography waveform.

Fresh vs mature: management path

Special cases: 7–14 days is "intermediate" — use caution, consider fiberoptic. Difficult anatomy → treat as fresh regardless of timing. Percutaneous tracheostomy may take longer to mature.

Bleeding: timing & severity

Assess timing and severity. Early bleeding (<48h post-op) is usually minor — skin edges, granulation, related to suctioning/manipulation, rarely life-threatening. Late bleeding (>72h, especially 7–14 days) is HIGH RISK for tracheo-innominate fistula (TIF) — innominate artery erosion approaches 50% mortality. A sentinel bleed (small, self-limited) precedes massive hemorrhage in up to 50% of TIF cases. Treat any late bleeding as potential TIF.

  • Suction to clear blood from the airway
  • Direct pressure to the stoma site; inspect for an obvious source (granulation, skin edge)
  • Topical epinephrine (1:1000 on gauze)
  • Hemostatic agents (Surgicel, gelfoam) or silver nitrate cautery for granulation tissue
  • Tranexamic acid-soaked gauze
  • Check coagulation (PT/INR, platelets); hold/reverse anticoagulation if safe
Tracheo-innominate fistula: any late bleeding is a sentinel bleed until proven otherwise
Risk factors: placement 7–21 days ago (peak incidence), low stoma site (below the 4th tracheal ring), high cuff pressure, excessive tube movement, prior radiation. Call for help (vascular surgery, ENT, OR team); type and crossmatch, prepare for massive transfusion; two large-bore IVs; prepare for cuff hyperinflation. Do NOT remove the tracheostomy tube.
  1. Hyperinflate the cuff — inject 50 mL of air (adult); creates tamponade against the innominate artery. If uncuffed, replace with a cuffed ETT.
  2. If cuff hyperinflation fails — Utley maneuver: insert a finger into the stoma, compress the innominate artery against the posterior sternum, maintain until OR.
  3. Resuscitate: activate the massive transfusion protocol, reverse anticoagulation, aggressive suctioning of blood.
  4. Definitive treatment: emergency OR for median sternotomy, innominate artery ligation/resection, cardiothoracic surgery consultation STAT.

Oral intubation with an existing tracheostomy (reference)

When to intubate orally: fresh trach dislodgement (<7 days), failed stoma replacement, cannot ventilate through the stoma, upper airway confirmed patent. Technique: standard oral approach with an uncut endotracheal tube; advance the cuff PAST the tracheostomy stoma level; confirm placement with capnography; occlude the stoma to prevent air leak. May need to deflate the trach cuff if still in place; apply an occlusive dressing to the stoma. Do NOT remove the trach tube unless it is causing obstruction. After stabilization, a surgeon should evaluate the stoma and replace the trach under controlled conditions.

Dual oxygenation strategy (reference — EMCrit "double-bagging")

Apply oxygen to both sites simultaneously: non-rebreather or BVM to the face, and a tracheostomy collar or pediatric face mask to the stoma. When ventilating via the face, occlude the stoma with gauze or a finger (may need to deflate the trach cuff). When ventilating via the stoma, occlude the nose and mouth, or use a tracheostomy BVM adapter — a pediatric face mask works well over the stoma. LMA options: standard LMA over the face, or an LMA over the stoma for rescue ventilation. Caution: if the trach is in a false passage, BVM through the tube may insufflate the mediastinum.

Late complications: granulation tissue & tracheal stenosis (reference)

Granulation tissue — caused by tube friction, fenestrated tubes, chronic irritation, infection, or high cuff pressure. Presents as recurrent obstruction, bleeding with suctioning, difficulty passing a suction catheter, or stridor after tube removal. Managed with bronchoscopy for diagnosis/treatment, silver nitrate cautery, laser ablation, tube change (non-fenestrated/different size), or surgical resection. Prevention: proper tube fit, low cuff pressure (<25 cmH2O), minimal manipulation.

Post-tracheostomy tracheal stenosis develops in 3–12% of patients. Causes: high cuff pressure (most common), infection, excessive tube movement, prolonged pre-trach intubation, low trach placement. Presents as failure to wean, high peak airway pressures, difficulty suctioning, stridor after decannulation, or exertional dyspnea. Bronchoscopy is the gold-standard diagnosis (CT neck/chest with 3D reconstruction also used). Management: dilation (balloon or rigid), stenting, surgical resection and anastomosis, or T-tube placement.

Do NOT

Do NOT — critical pitfalls8 things that kill trach patients

- Do NOT blind-reinsert a fresh tracheostomy (<7 days) — risk of false passage, pneumomediastinum, complete airway loss.
- Do NOT attempt oral/nasal intubation in a laryngectomy patient — the stoma is the ONLY airway.
- Do NOT remove the tracheostomy tube in suspected tracheo-innominate fistula bleeding — hyperinflate the cuff instead.
- Do NOT attempt interventional radiology for TIF hemorrhage — mortality is too high; go straight to the OR.
- Do NOT rely on misting, phonation, or SpO2 alone to confirm trach placement — capnography is the gold standard.
- Do NOT force a tube during mature-trach replacement — may create a false passage.
- Do NOT skip removing the inner cannula first when troubleshooting obstruction.
- Do NOT forget high-flow O2 to BOTH face AND stoma as a first action in any trach emergency.

Fresh trach (<7 days): DO NOT REPLACE — Blind reinsertion contraindicated — oxygenate via face, occlude stoma, oral intubate past stoma, call surgeon

Mature trach (>7 days): replacement — Deflate cuff, lubricate, insert along tract, inflate, confirm with capnography — do NOT force

Confirm trach placement — Capnography is gold standard — do NOT rely on misting or SpO2 alone, fiberoptic if uncertain

Stable after intervention — Stable after tracheostomy emergency. Ensure bedside equipment, appropriate monitoring, and document intervention thoroughly.

ENT/surgical consultation required — ENT/surgical consultation required for specialist management. Stabilize airway while awaiting consultation.

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for the complete tube-types/sizing module, granulation/stenosis detail, and dual-oxygenation technique.

Do NOT

Do NOT — critical pitfalls

- Do NOT blind-reinsert a fresh tracheostomy (<7 days) — risk of false passage, pneumomediastinum, complete airway loss.
- Do NOT attempt oral/nasal intubation in a laryngectomy patient — the stoma is the ONLY airway.
- Do NOT remove the tracheostomy tube in suspected tracheo-innominate fistula bleeding — hyperinflate the cuff instead.
- Do NOT attempt interventional radiology for TIF hemorrhage — mortality is too high; go straight to the OR.
- Do NOT rely on misting, phonation, or SpO2 alone to confirm trach placement — capnography is the gold standard.
- Do NOT force a tube during mature-trach replacement — may create a false passage.
- Do NOT skip removing the inner cannula first when troubleshooting obstruction.
- Do NOT forget high-flow O2 to BOTH face AND stoma as a first action in any trach emergency.
