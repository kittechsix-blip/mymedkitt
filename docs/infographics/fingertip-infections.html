Fingertip Infections — Photo Triage & Bedside Management

ED patients with a fingertip infection worked up by photo-pattern recognition: paronychia (acute or chronic), felon, herpetic whitlow, or subungual hematoma/abscess. Before branching into any pathway, screen for Kanavel signs — any positive sign is treated as flexor tenosynovitis, is out of scope for bedside management here, and is routed to the Hand Infections consult for OR-level care.

⚠ Do NOT incise herpetic whitlow — incision causes bacterial superinfection and viremia; this is the most common ED error in fingertip infections.
⚠ Felon I&D: avoid fishmouth (transverse through-and-through) and hockey-stick/J incisions — they cause pulp necrosis or digital nerve injury.
⚠ Subungual hematoma: trephinate based on pain, not the historical ">50% of nail" rule (abandoned) — tuft fracture is NOT a contraindication to trephination.
⚠ Kanavel signs positive (any one) → treat as flexor tenosynovitis: hand surgery consult immediately, IV antibiotics, NO bedside drainage.

Photo triage overview

Fingertip infections are diagnosed largely by pattern recognition. Before branching into a pathway, always screen for Kanavel signs — any positive sign means this is a flexor sheath infection, not a fingertip problem.

  • Nail fold swelling / pus → Paronychia
  • Tense, throbbing pulp → Felon
  • Grouped clear vesicles → Herpetic Whitlow (do NOT incise)
  • Dark blood under the nail after trauma → Subungual hematoma
  • Yellow/white pus under the nail without trauma → Subungual abscess
  • Fusiform swelling / Kanavel sign positive → Suspected flexor tenosynovitis, escalate
Screen for Kanavel signs before any bedside drainage
Four Kanavel signs of pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis: fusiform (sausage) swelling of the entire digit; finger held in slight flexion at rest; tenderness along the flexor tendon sheath; pain with passive extension (most sensitive, appears earliest). Any one sign raises concern; 3 of 4 is highly specific. A positive screen routes to suspected flexor tenosynovitis, not a fingertip drainage pathway.

Fingertip infection algorithm

This is the high-yield spine. See the full protocol for the photo atlas, Kanavel signs reference page, and steps summary.

Paronychia

Acute (<6 weeks): bacterial, usually Staph aureus, occasionally anaerobes/oral flora after nail-biting. Tender, erythematous nail fold, may be fluctuant.

Chronic (≥6 weeks): inflammatory, frequent water exposure, often Candida-colonized. Boggy, less tender, multiple digits possible. Cuticle absent.

  1. Simple lateral fold abscess (no subungual tracking): skin prep, digital block only if extensive. Slide a #11 blade or 18g needle parallel to the nail, bevel up, between cuticle and nail plate at the point of maximal fluctuance.
  2. Lift the eponychial fold off the nail to release pus. Do NOT incise the nail fold itself — scarring + nail dystrophy.
  3. Subungual extension (pus under nail edge): remove the lateral one-quarter of the nail with a small hemostat, keeping the rest as a biologic dressing. Total nail avulsion only if the entire subungual space is involved.
  4. Express pus, irrigate with saline, loose gauze. Warm soaks TID starting day 2. Dressing change in 24-48h.

Antibiotics usually NOT required after adequate drainage. Add antibiotics only if surrounding cellulitis or MRSA risk.

Early paronychia — conservative management
Warm soaks 15-20 min, 3-4x daily. Elevation, keep dry between soaks. Avoid manipulation, biting, picking. Antibiotics reserved for spreading erythema, immunocompromise, or failure at 48h. Reassess at 48h — if fluctuance develops, drain per the acute pathway.
Chronic paronychia — inflammatory, not bacterial
Candida is a colonizer, not the primary pathogen; repeated wet work disrupts the cuticle barrier. Stop wet exposure — cotton-lined gloves for housework/dishwashing. Topical high-potency steroid (triamcinolone 0.1% or clobetasol BID × 3 weeks) — more effective than antifungals head-to-head. Add topical antifungal (clotrimazole BID) if Candida present. Keep dry. Avoid drainage attempts (worsens inflammation) and systemic antibiotics (not bacterial). Refer to dermatology or hand surgery if no improvement at 6 weeks.

Felon

Distal pulp is partitioned by 15-20 fibrous septa into closed compartments — pus raises pressure rapidly, risking ischemia, septic DIP arthritis, and distal phalanx osteomyelitis. Classic exam: tense, glove-tight pulp; throbbing pain out of proportion; minor puncture/splinter often the trigger; Staph aureus most common.

Herpetic whitlow

Do NOT incise
Incision causes bacterial superinfection, viremia, and slower healing — the most common ED error in fingertip infections. Often misdiagnosed as felon or paronychia early; grouped clear vesicles on an erythematous base are the clue.

Burning/throbbing pain typically out of proportion to exam early on. Prodrome: tingling 24-48h before vesicles appear. High-risk groups: dental/respiratory therapy workers, thumb-sucking kids, healthcare workers; HSV-1 > HSV-2. Self-limited 2-4 weeks; recurrences possible.

Confirmatory testing (only if diagnosis uncertain): HSV PCR from de-roofed vesicle fluid (most sensitive). Tzanck smear: multinucleated giant cells (low sensitivity, fast). Viral culture (slow, less commonly available).

Crush, door-jam injury, sports mechanism → X-ray. A tuft fracture changes management to splint/antibiotic-for-open-fracture considerations.

Indication: painful subungual hematoma, regardless of size. Pain relief is dramatic and immediate. The old "drain only if >50% of nail" rule has been abandoned — trephinate based on pain, not percentage. Tuft fracture is NOT a contraindication; trephination of a hematoma overlying a closed tuft fracture does not increase infection risk and is preferred over nail removal in stable nail beds.

  • Electrocautery (preferred): hold the cautery tip perpendicular to the nail at the center of the hematoma. Touch lightly — the heated tip melts through in 1-2 seconds. Stop the moment blood escapes. Painless because the nail has no innervation.
  • Alternative — heated paperclip / 18g needle twirl: heat a paperclip end red-hot or rotate a sterile 18g needle bevel like a drill. Same endpoint: through-and-through with blood release.
  • After drainage: gentle pressure, dry dressing, keep dry 24h then normal hygiene. Splint the distal phalanx if associated tuft fracture (mallet-style splint, ortho follow-up 1-2 weeks). Antibiotics generally not needed even with tuft fracture in healthy patients.
  • Nail removal indications (rare): nail plate avulsed/floppy, nail bed laceration requiring repair, surrounding paronychia.

Often an extension of paronychia that has tracked under the nail.

  • Lateral edge only: digital block, lift the lateral nail fold, slide a hemostat under the nail edge to release pus. If pus persists, remove the lateral one-quarter of the nail.
  • Central or under most of the nail: trephinate the nail directly over the abscess (electrocautery) — often enough. Total nail avulsion only if the entire bed is involved or for poor response.
  • Follow-up: wound check 48-72h, hand surgery if no improvement.

Suspected flexor tenosynovitis

This is no longer a fingertip problem
Kanavel signs = flexor sheath infection until proven otherwise. Time to treatment determines outcome — delays >24h drive tendon necrosis and amputation risk.
  1. Hand surgery consult NOW — do not wait for imaging if classic.
  2. NPO; mark cellulitis border; serial exams.
  3. IV antibiotics: cover Staph (incl. MRSA), Strep, gram-negatives, and oral flora if bite/penetration history.
  4. Tetanus update if indicated.
  5. Splint in functional position; elevate.
  6. Do NOT drain at the bedside — flexor sheath drainage is an OR procedure.

IV antibiotics: Vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam (or Cefepime). Specific dosing and full FTS drainage technique are covered in the dedicated Hand Infections consult — this consult scopes to fingertip pathology only.

Disposition — outpatient

Discharge criteria
Source controlled (drained or not yet abscess). No Kanavel signs, no deep space involvement. Reliable patient, can return. No systemic signs (fever, leukocytosis, lymphangitis).
  • Oral antibiotic if indicated (see specific pathway).
  • Warm soaks TID, elevation, dry between soaks.
  • Wound check / dressing change at 24-48h.
  • Hand or PCP follow-up at 48-72h, sooner if worse.
  • Tetanus if indicated.
Strict return precautions
Worsening pain or swelling. Spreading redness, red streaking up the arm. Fever, chills. New finger stiffness or pain on passive extension (FTS). Numbness or color change of the fingertip.
