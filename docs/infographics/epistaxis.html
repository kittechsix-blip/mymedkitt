Epistaxis (Nosebleed) — ED Management

Adults presenting to the ED with epistaxis (nosebleed). Excludes pediatric-specific dosing, isolated facial/nasal trauma requiring primary surgical repair, and hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) as a standalone diagnosis — HHT is screened for as a posterior-bleed risk factor, not managed here.

⚠ Firm compression of the fleshy part of the nose (not the bony bridge) for 15-20 minutes is first-line before any other intervention.
⚠ Apply topical vasoconstrictor (oxymetazoline or phenylephrine) plus lidocaine before attempting visualization or cautery.
⚠ Never cauterize BOTH sides of the septum in the same session — risk of septal perforation.
⚠ All posterior nasal packs require hospital admission with continuous SpO2 monitoring.
⚠ ENT consultation is required for all posterior epistaxis — 18% require surgical intervention.
⚠ Do NOT routinely reverse anticoagulation for epistaxis — local hemostasis is effective in most cases; reversal increases stroke/VTE risk.
⚠ Topical TXA is particularly helpful in antiplatelet/anticoagulated patients — do not withhold it while considering more invasive measures.
⚠ BP usually normalizes once bleeding is controlled (anxiety response) — do NOT delay local hemostasis to manage BP, and do NOT use IV antihypertensives for epistaxis.
⚠ Hemodynamically unstable patients (tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status, active hemorrhage) require simultaneous resuscitation — two large-bore IVs, type and crossmatch, and early ENT notification — before/alongside local hemostatic measures.

Initial assessment

90% of epistaxis is anterior (Kiesselbach plexus) — the convergence of 4 arterial supplies at the anterior septum. Posterior bleeds are less common but more severe.

  • Hemodynamic stability (HR, BP, mental status)
  • Estimate blood loss (usually overestimated)
  • Establish IV access if significant bleeding
  • Type and screen if severe/recurrent
  • Anticoagulant/antiplatelet use
  • Bleeding diathesis (personal/family)
  • Recent trauma or nasal surgery
  • Hypertension history

Anterior bleed: cautery & packing

  1. Ensure adequate anesthesia (topical lidocaine)
  2. Dry the area with cotton-tipped applicator
  3. Apply silver nitrate stick to bleeding point for 5-10 seconds
  4. Work from periphery toward center
  5. Apply to limited area only (excessive cautery causes necrosis)

  • Expanding sponge (Merocel, Rhino Rocket) — coat with antibiotic ointment, hydrate with saline to expand

Posterior epistaxis

Usually the sphenopalatine artery (branch of internal maxillary). Risk factors: age >50, hypertension, anticoagulation, bleeding disorders, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT). 18% of posterior epistaxis patients require surgical intervention.

  • Dual-balloon device (preferred): inflate posterior balloon 5-10 mL air first, then anterior balloon 10-15 mL air
  • Foley catheter (10-14 Fr): inflate with 10-15 mL saline, seat in posterior choana, secure with padded umbilical clamp
  • Pack anterior nose with gauze/Merocel in front of the Foley
  • Continuous SpO2 monitoring required — 20 mmHg PaO2 drop reported

Anticoagulation management

50-60% of ED epistaxis patients are on antithrombotic therapy: warfarin, DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban), heparin/LMWH, aspirin, or P2Y12 inhibitors.

Check INR immediately. Do NOT routinely reverse anticoagulation for epistaxis — most can be managed with local measures. Reversal increases stroke/VTE risk. Higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc = more cautious about reversal.

Disposition

Assess disposition based on need for nasal packing, anticoagulation status, hemoglobin level, ability to follow up, and risk of recurrence.

  • Saline nasal spray 2-3x daily
  • Petroleum jelly to anterior septum BID
  • Humidifier at night
  • Avoid nose picking, forceful blowing, NSAIDs
  • If bleeding recurs: pinch fleshy part x15-20 min, lean forward, oxymetazoline spray, return if not controlled in 20 min
  • PCP follow-up within 1-2 weeks; ENT referral if recurrent (>2 episodes/month)

Keep pack in place — do not remove or adjust. Sleep with head elevated, soft diet, avoid strenuous activity. ENT follow-up within 24-48 hours for pack removal and reassessment. Return immediately for bleeding around/behind the pack, difficulty breathing, fever, worsening pain, or signs of infection.

  • Significant anemia (Hgb <8 g/dL) requiring transfusion
  • Unable to achieve hemostasis in ED
  • High-risk anticoagulated patient
  • Coagulopathy requiring correction
  • Unreliable patient / unable to follow up
  • Multiple ED visits for recurrent epistaxis

Clinical pearls

ENT consultation triggersEmergent vs urgent vs outpatient referral criteria

Emergent: posterior epistaxis (any), uncontrolled bleeding despite anterior packing, hemodynamic instability, need for surgical intervention (ligation, embolization).

Urgent: bilateral anterior packs, recurrent epistaxis (>2 episodes), suspected septal abnormality, suspected HHT.

Outpatient referral: recurrent anterior epistaxis, nasal mass or abnormality on exam, family history of bleeding disorders, chronic nasal obstruction.

Hypertension and epistaxisCommon misconception: HTN causes epistaxis

Epistaxis causes anxiety which raises BP; HTN rarely initiates bleeding. BP usually normalizes after bleeding is controlled. Control the bleeding first; recheck BP after hemorrhage controlled and patient calm. If BP remains severely elevated (>180/120) after bleeding controlled, consider an oral antihypertensive. Do NOT delay local treatment to manage BP. Do NOT use IV antihypertensives for epistaxis — may cause hypotension.

Topical TXA evidence baseZahed RCT vs the NoPAC trial

RCT of 124 patients on antiplatelets (Zahed): TXA vs anterior packing — faster hemostasis at 10 min, less rebleeding at 24h and 1 week, shorter ED stay, higher patient satisfaction. NoPAC trial (larger, unselected population): TXA vs placebo for epistaxis uncontrolled with first aid showed no significant difference. TXA may be most useful in antiplatelet/anticoagulated patients, as an adjunct to packing, or before more invasive interventions.
