ED Procedural Sedation: Readiness, Agent, Rescue

ED patients of any age undergoing a brief procedure (reduction, cardioversion, abscess drainage, imaging, wound care) needing analgesia, anxiolysis, amnesia, or dissociation, when the team can immediately rescue airway/ventilation. Excludes patients whose risk exceeds ED rescue resources — anticipated impossible ventilation, severe unstable cardiopulmonary disease, inability to monitor/recover, or a procedure too long/complex for ED sedation (route to anesthesia/OR/ICU).

⚠ Only sedate when the team can immediately rescue airway/ventilation — this is a precondition for ED sedation, not a step to complete after starting.
⚠ Complete rescue setup before any sedative: continuous pulse oximetry, cardiac monitoring, BP cycling; capnography when available (especially moderate/deep sedation); suction ready; BVM, oxygen, oral/nasal airways, and intubation backup immediately available; a dedicated monitor separate from the procedure operator; resuscitation meds and reversal agents accessible.
⚠ Airway/ventilation complication: reposition/jaw thrust → suction if needed → supplemental oxygen/BVM ventilation → oral or nasal airway if tolerated → treat laryngospasm with airway maneuvers, positive pressure, and escalation per local protocol → intubate or call for airway help if ventilation cannot be restored. Ventilation is the endpoint — escalate early if BVM ventilation is ineffective.
⚠ Do not proceed with routine ED sedation when risk exceeds ED rescue resources — anticipated impossible ventilation, severe unstable cardiopulmonary disease, inability to monitor, no recovery space, procedure too long/complex, or a complication requiring advanced airway/ICU/OR. Use anesthesia, OR, ICU, or a specialist pathway instead.

1 · Overview

ED procedural sedation supports short procedures needing analgesia, anxiolysis, amnesia, or dissociation — only when the team can immediately rescue airway and ventilation.

  1. Confirm ED sedation is appropriate
  2. Screen for risk factors
  3. Complete monitoring and rescue setup
  4. Choose and give a sedation agent
  5. Monitor continuously during the procedure
  6. Recognize and rescue any complication
  7. Recover to a safe baseline before disposition

Stop / escalate

5 · Agent selection

Match the agent to procedure pain, desired immobility, hemodynamics, airway risk, and clinician familiarity.

Strategy | Use when | Key reminder

Dissociation (ketamine) | Painful procedure, need preserved respiratory drive, pediatric or adult dissociative plan | Dose intentionally; avoid repeated small under-doses

Brief deep sedation (propofol/etomidate) | Reduction/cardioversion; rapid onset and rapid offset needed | Titrate to effect with immediate airway rescue readiness

Analgesia + anxiolysis only | Less painful procedure, frail patient, or lower depth target | Avoid stacking respiratory depressants without reassessing ventilation

Ketamine IV dissociative dose (1-2 mg/kg IV): 105 mg

Ketamine IM dissociative dose (4-5 mg/kg IM): 280 mg

Monitor for vomiting, hypersalivation, emergence reaction, and rare laryngospasm (0.3%). Emergence reactions occur in 10-20% of adults. Avoid when severe uncontrolled hypertension, active psychosis, or concerning airway secretions make another strategy safer.

Propofol: Titrate to effect per local protocol IV — No dedicated procedural-sedation weight-based dose exists in the drug reference (see sourceConflictsFound) — expect apnea/hypotension risk; avoid casual redosing after the procedure is complete.

Etomidate procedural sedation dose (0.1-0.15 mg/kg IV): 10.5 mg

Etomidate repeat dose (0.05 mg/kg PRN): 3.5 mg

Etomidate has NO analgesic properties — pair with fentanyl, given 2-3 minutes before etomidate. Myoclonus in 20-40% of patients — can be mistaken for seizure activity.

Fentanyl IV dose (closest drug-store match; capped 100 mcg): 100 mcg

Closest drug-store match: Burns — IV analgesia dosing, 1-1.5 mcg/kg IV over 1-2 minutes (max 100 mcg) — no dedicated procedural-sedation indication exists for fentanyl (see sourceConflictsFound).

Midazolam: 1-2 mg IV over 2 min IV — May repeat 1 mg q2 min to adequate sedation (typical total 2-5 mg). Use lower doses in elderly, hemodynamically unstable, or post-arrest patients. Have flumazenil available.

Avoid stacking respiratory depressants without reassessing ventilation after each dose.
