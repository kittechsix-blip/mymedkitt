Rapid Code Status: A Goals-of-Care Conversation

Clinicians in the ED (or any acute setting) leading an emergent or serious-illness goals-of-care / code-status conversation with adult patients or their surrogates. Excludes pediatric goals-of-care conversations and formal outpatient advance-care-planning visits, which follow separate protocols.

⚠ Use the Surprise Question — "Would I be surprised if this patient died?" — to identify when a goals-of-care conversation is needed, then have it early.
⚠ MAKE A RECOMMENDATION. Never ask "Do you want us to do everything?" — offering false equivalence between CPR and comfort care puts an impossible burden on families.
⚠ Explore patient values before recommending: "What's most important to you as you think about the future?" Listen more than you talk.
⚠ Share prognosis honestly, then pause. Allow silence — let the patient or family process before continuing.
⚠ Never say "withdraw care" or "there's nothing more we can do." Say "shift focus to comfort" — comfort-focused care is active treatment, not abandonment.
⚠ Explain CPR honestly: it is designed to restart a heart that stops suddenly in an otherwise healthy person, not to restore a critically ill patient to their prior health.
⚠ DNR does not mean "do not treat." Antibiotics, IV fluids, vasopressors, dialysis, surgery, and ICU admission can all continue under a DNR order unless specifically declined.
⚠ Document every conversation: who was present, capacity (or why a surrogate is speaking), prognosis communicated, values expressed, the specific recommendation made, and the decision reached.
⚠ Communicate code status at every handoff and update EMR orders immediately — code status is not a one-time form, it must travel with the patient.

What to do right now

The 30-second conversation
"I'm Dr. [Name]. Your [mother/father/loved one] is very sick. I need to talk with you about what happens if [their] heart stops."

"Based on what I'm seeing, if we do CPR, I don't think it will help [them] survive, and it may cause more suffering. What I'd recommend is that we focus on keeping [them] comfortable and let [them] die peacefully."

"Is that okay? Do you have questions?"

Don't ask: "Do you want us to do everything?" Instead make a recommendation based on medical judgment.
Uncertain prognosis — time-limited trial
"I wish I could tell you exactly what will happen. I can't. But I can tell you what I'm seeing and what I'm worried about." Propose a time-limited trial for 48-72 hours: clear goals, specific endpoints, who reassesses and when. Document the plan and revisit early and often.
Patient with capacity
Assess understanding of illness, share prognosis honestly, explore values ("What matters most?"), make a recommendation, document carefully. If the patient doesn't want to discuss it: "I want to respect your wishes. Can you tell me who should make decisions if you become too sick to speak for yourself?" Always identify a surrogate even when the patient has capacity.
Surrogate decision-maker
Frame it as substituted judgment: "Our goal is to figure out what [they] would want, not what you want for them." Ask: "Did [they] ever tell you what [they] would want?" "If [they] could see [themselves] right now, what would [they] say?" "What did [they] value most in life?" Relieve the burden: "You're not making this decision — you're helping us understand what [they] would want" and "Whatever happens, you didn't cause this illness." Identify the legal decision-maker (DPOA, next of kin by state law).
Family conflict / disagreement
Refocus on the patient ("Let's think about what [patient] would want"), identify the legally authorized decision-maker, hold a family meeting with chaplain/social work if available, acknowledge the difficulty ("You all love [them]"), and consider a time-limited trial — "Let's try [intervention] for 72 hours and reassess." You cannot please everyone; focus on the patient.
"We want everything done"
First understand why ("Tell me more about what 'everything' means to you") — usually: don't give up, don't let them suffer, don't miss anything. Reframe: "I hear you — you want us to do everything that will help. So do I. What I'm worried about is doing things that would hurt [them] without helping." Be honest that CPR would not bring them back to the life they had. "I will promise to do everything that will help. And I won't do anything that will only cause suffering."

1 · When to have the conversation

The "code status" conversation is really a goals-of-care conversation — not just a checklist of procedures to accept or refuse. Key principles: focus on what the patient VALUES, not procedures; make a recommendation when appropriate; avoid asking patients to make medical decisions; be honest about prognosis; a conversation, not a form to fill out. This is hard — you're doing something important.

Surprise Question: "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next year/month/week?" If NO — a goals-of-care conversation is needed.

2 · REMAP framework

REMAP framework (VitalTalk/REMAP):

R — Reframe: Set the stage. "I wish we were meeting under different circumstances..."

E — Expect emotion, explore values. "What's most important to you right now?" "What gives your life meaning?"

M — Map the future. "Given what you've told me about what's important..."

A — Align. "It sounds like what matters most is..."

P — Plan. "Based on that, I'd recommend..."

This takes 5-10 minutes when done well.

Reframe — setting the stage
Warning shot: "I'm worried about what I'm seeing today..." "I have some serious news to discuss..." Assess understanding: "What have the doctors told you about your condition?" "What's your understanding of where things are?" Share prognosis honestly: "I wish I had better news. The cancer has spread despite treatment." "I'm worried that [they] may not survive this hospitalization." Pause. Allow silence. Let them process.
Expect emotion, explore values
"What's most important to you as you think about the future?" "What are you hoping for?" "What abilities are so important that you can't imagine living without them?" "What would be a fate worse than death for you?" For surrogates: "If [they] could see [themselves] now, what would [they] say?" "What did [they] tell you about what [they'd] want?" "Knowing [them] as you do, what do you think [they] would choose?" Listen more than you talk.
Map, align, and make a recommendation
Do NOT say "Do you want us to do everything?" or "We can keep trying or we can stop" — false equivalence, impossible burden. DO say: "Based on what you've told me about what's important to [them], I'd recommend..." "Given the situation, I think the best thing we can do is..." "In my medical opinion, CPR would not help [them] and would cause suffering." A recommendation is a gift, not an imposition — families often feel relief when given clear guidance.

3 · Key phrases

On prognosis: "I'm hoping for the best but preparing for the worst." "I wish the situation were different." "I'm worried this illness is very serious." On CPR: "CPR is designed to restart a heart that stops suddenly in an otherwise healthy person. In [their] situation, it would not restore [them] to health." On ventilators: "The breathing machine can buy time, but it can't fix the underlying problem." On comfort: "Allow natural death" (not "withdraw care"); "Focus on comfort" (not "do nothing"); "We will never abandon you."

4 · Common scenarios

Patient with capacity
Advantages: honors autonomy directly, avoids surrogate burden, patient knows their own values. Approach: assess understanding → share prognosis → explore values ("What matters most?") → make a recommendation → document carefully. If the patient doesn't want to discuss it: "I want to respect your wishes. Can you tell me who should make decisions if you become too sick to speak for yourself?" Always identify a surrogate even when patient has capacity.
Surrogate decision-maker
Key concept: substituted judgment — "Our goal is to figure out what [they] would want, not what you want for them." Questions: "Did [they] ever tell you what [they] would want in a situation like this?" "If [they] could see [themselves] right now, what would [they] say?" "What did [they] value most in life?" Relieve the burden: "You're not making this decision — you're helping us understand what [they] would want"; "Whatever happens, you didn't cause this illness." Identify the legal decision-maker (DPOA, next of kin by state law).
Family conflict
Strategies: refocus on the patient ("Let's think about what [patient] would want"); identify the decision-maker ("Who is legally authorized to decide?"); hold a family meeting (private room, all stakeholders, chaplain/social work if available); acknowledge the difficulty ("You all love [them]"); consider a time-limited trial — "Let's try [intervention] for 72 hours and reassess." You cannot please everyone — focus on the patient.
"We want everything done"
First understand why: "Tell me more about what 'everything' means to you." Often: don't give up, don't let them suffer, don't miss anything. Reframe: "I hear you — you want us to do everything that will help. So do I. What I'm worried about is doing things that would hurt [them] without helping." Be honest: CPR would not bring them back to the life they had. Offer: "I will promise to do everything that will help. And I won't do anything that will only cause suffering."
Uncertain prognosis
"I wish I could tell you exactly what will happen. I can't. But I can tell you what I'm seeing and what I'm worried about." Consider a time-limited trial: "Let's try [intervention] for 48-72 hours. If we see improvement, we'll continue. If not, we'll talk again about what makes sense." Document the plan: clear goals, specific endpoints, who reassesses and when. Revisit early and often — prognosis becomes clearer with time.

5 · Documentation and POLST

  • Who was present at the conversation
  • Patient's capacity (or why surrogate is speaking)
  • Prognosis communicated
  • Values and goals expressed
  • Specific recommendation made
  • Decision reached
  • Questions/concerns addressed

Code status order set: Full Code, DNR/DNI, DNR only (intubation acceptable), Comfort measures only / Allow natural death. Code status ≠ level of care — DNR patients can still receive aggressive treatment.

POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment): a portable medical order, not an advance directive. Follows the patient across settings. Signed by physician/NP/PA AND patient/surrogate. Complete when: serious illness with limited life expectancy, clear goals have been established, and the patient wants decisions documented as orders. Not appropriate for: healthy patients, patients who haven't had a goals conversation, or as a substitute for the conversation. POLST does not replace advance directives; they complement each other.

6 · Transitions of care

After the conversation: communicate code status at all handoffs, update orders in the EMR, complete a POLST if appropriate, and arrange follow-up if goals are uncertain. Involve palliative care early if available; hospice can be consulted from the ED — don't wait until "nothing more can be done."

Transition to comfort
Comfort-focused care IS active treatment: symptom management (pain, dyspnea, nausea), emotional support, family presence, dignity and respect. Continue medications for comfort, allow family at bedside, consider chaplain/spiritual care, treat distressing symptoms aggressively. Consider inpatient hospice, home hospice with rapid discharge, or inpatient palliative care. "We will never abandon you."
DNR with active treatment
DNR does not mean 'do not treat': antibiotics, IV fluids, vasopressors (if aligned with goals), dialysis, surgery, and ICU admission may all continue. Clarify specific wishes — intubation preferences (DNI is separate from DNR), vasopressor preferences, dialysis preferences, feeding tube preferences. Re-evaluate as illness progresses; goals may change.
Full code — goals clarified
Full code is appropriate when patient/surrogate has clear understanding of prognosis, values align with aggressive intervention, there is a reasonable chance of meaningful recovery, or the patient prefers to "try everything." Document that the conversation occurred, understanding of prognosis was confirmed, values support full intervention, and patient/surrogate voiced the preference. Plan to revisit if situation changes or patient deteriorates. Full code is not "default" — it's a choice.
