Button Battery Ingestion: Locate, Time-Critical Removal, Disposition

Any age presenting after suspected or confirmed button/coin-cell battery ingestion, ED/urgent care. Covers esophageal, gastric, and distal battery location management plus delayed complication monitoring. Excludes non-battery foreign-body ingestion protocols and detailed co-ingested-magnet surgical management.

⚠ Esophageal tissue injury begins within 15 minutes of impaction. Obtain STAT X-ray (AP + lateral: head, neck, chest, abdomen), keep NPO until location is confirmed, and call the battery hotline 1-800-498-8666 (24/7).
⚠ Esophageal battery = emergent endoscopy. Target removal within 2 hours (maximum 12 hours). Honey/sucralfate DELAY tissue injury but do NOT prevent it — do NOT delay endoscopy for pre-treatment or for NPO status. Contraindicated if age <12 months (aspiration risk), suspected perforation or mediastinitis, battery >12 hours in esophagus, or dysphagia preventing safe swallowing.
⚠ Aortoesophageal fistula (AEF) can occur up to 28 days post-removal. A "sentinel bleed" (minor hematemesis) can precede sudden massive hemorrhage — do not dismiss minor bleeding in a patient with a history of esophageal battery impaction.
⚠ Suspected AEF: do NOT delay CTA chest imaging. Immediate cardiothoracic surgery consultation and massive transfusion protocol standby.
⚠ Do NOT induce vomiting or give ipecac. Do NOT attempt blind balloon catheter removal. Do NOT give activated charcoal, laxatives, or PEG solution. Do NOT check blood/urine metal levels or attempt chelation. Do NOT delay removal even if honey/sucralfate has been given.

1 · Initial assessment

CRITICAL TIME WINDOW: Esophageal tissue injury begins within 15 minutes. Esophageal batteries must be removed within 2 hours (ideally). Aortoesophageal fistula can occur up to 28 days post-removal.

  • Lithium coin cells (3V) — most dangerous
  • ≥20mm diameter — highest risk for esophageal impaction

  2. Keep NPO until esophageal position ruled out
  3. Battery hotline: 1-800-498-8666 (24/7)
Radiographic signs
"Double-rim" or "halo" on AP view. "Step-off" on lateral view (distinguishes from a coin).
Symptomatic / unknown location
Red flags: drooling, dysphagia, odynophagia, chest pain, vomiting (especially hematemesis), stridor/respiratory distress, refusal to eat, fever (suggests mediastinitis). If symptomatic and battery not visible on X-ray, consider CT with contrast — battery may have passed but caused injury. IV access, NPO, emergent GI/ENT consultation, do NOT induce vomiting, prepare for emergent endoscopy.

4 · Esophageal battery management

This is a TIME-CRITICAL emergency. Pre-endoscopy protection is only for battery <12 hours in the esophagus.

Honey (age ≥12 months)
10 mL orally every 10 minutes × 6 doses
PO
Use commercial honey only. Delays tissue injury but does NOT prevent it.
Sucralfate (ED alternative)
1 g (10 mL suspension) PO every 10 minutes × 3 doses
PO
Alternative to honey.
Contraindications to honey/sucralfate
Age <12 months (aspiration risk). Suspected perforation or mediastinitis. Battery >12 hours in esophagus. Dysphagia preventing safe swallowing.
  • Operating room preferred over endoscopy suite (rapid conversion to open surgery, better imaging)
  • Team: GI/ENT for endoscopy, anesthesia, cardiothoracic surgery on standby, pediatric surgery available
  • Gentle removal to minimize additional trauma; inspect for perforation after removal
  • Do NOT delay for NPO status
  • Admit for observation (minimum 24-48 hours)
  • CTA chest if mid-esophageal impaction (AEF risk)
  • NPO initially → clear liquids when stable

5 · Gastric battery management

Risk levelCriteria
High riskAge <6 years AND battery ≥15mm; OR battery ≥20mm (any age); OR symptomatic patient; OR co-ingested magnet
Medium riskAge 6-12 years OR battery 12-15mm — requires closer monitoring
Low riskAge ≥12 years AND battery ≤12mm; asymptomatic; no magnet co-ingestion; reliable caregiver for follow-up
  • Asymptomatic: can observe initially; symptomatic: consult GI for early removal
  • Repeat X-ray at Day 4; if still in stomach at Day 4, endoscopic removal recommended
  • Gastric mucosal damage can occur within 48-96 hours — early removal reduces injury risk
  • Discharge instructions: normal diet/activity, return for vomiting/abdominal pain/bloody stool/fever, inspect stools, follow-up arranged for Day 4 imaging
  • Home observation: normal diet/activity, inspect stools for battery passage, no inpatient observation needed
  • If passage not confirmed by Day 10-14: repeat X-ray (most batteries pass within 4-7 days)
  • Return precautions: abdominal pain, vomiting, black/bloody stools, fever, decreased appetite
  • GI consultation for endoscopy consideration — higher risk of mucosal injury
  • Repeat X-ray at 48 hours
  • If not progressing past pylorus: remove endoscopically. If progressed to duodenum: follow distal protocol

6 · Distal battery management

  • Once past the stomach, serious complications are rare
  • Home observation, normal diet and activity, inspect stools for battery passage
  • Repeat X-ray at Day 10-14 if passage not confirmed; no routine imaging if asymptomatic
  • Surgical removal indications: symptoms develop (obstruction, perforation), battery fails to progress on serial imaging, signs of intestinal injury
  • Most batteries pass uneventfully within 4-7 days; small-intestine transit usually complete within 72 hours

7 · Complications

  • Immediate (first 24-48 hours): esophageal perforation → pneumomediastinum; mediastinitis → fever, tachycardia, sepsis; hemorrhage from vessel erosion
  • Delayed (days 3-28+): aortoesophageal fistula (AEF, up to 28 days, mid-esophageal impaction highest risk); tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF, up to 9 days post-removal); esophageal stenosis (weeks to months); vocal cord paralysis
  • Warning signs post-removal: chest pain (mid-epigastric/substernal), hemoptysis or hematemesis, fever/tachycardia, new stridor or respiratory distress
Aortoesophageal fistula — catastrophic, high mortality
Risk factors: mid-esophageal impaction (aortic arch level), battery ≥20mm, prolonged impaction (>2 hours). Presentation: sentinel bleed (minor hematemesis preceding massive hemorrhage), chest/back pain, sudden cardiovascular collapse. Diagnosis: CTA chest (preferred) — do NOT delay imaging if suspected. Management: immediate cardiothoracic surgery consultation, massive transfusion protocol standby, may require ECMO bridge, endovascular stenting or open repair.
  • Signs of perforation: pneumomediastinum on X-ray/CT, subcutaneous emphysema, severe chest pain, mediastinal widening
  • Mediastinitis: fever, tachycardia, chest pain, dyspnea, sepsis syndrome, odynophagia
  • Management: broad-spectrum antibiotics (cover oral + GI flora), NPO, TPN consideration, surgical consultation, CT chest with contrast

  • Monitoring: vitals q4h (watch for sepsis), NPO initially, advance diet when stable (clear liquids → soft diet), serial exams for abdominal/chest pain

  • DISCHARGE with close follow-up: asymptomatic gastric/distal batteries meeting low-risk criteria, reliable caregiver, clear return precautions given, follow-up imaging arranged
