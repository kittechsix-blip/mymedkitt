Unilateral Facial Pain: Dangerous Buckets First, Then Pattern Recognition

ED evaluation of patients presenting with unilateral facial pain as the chief complaint. Functions as a triage/pattern-recognition hub — screens for eye/orbit, neurovascular, deep-space infection, giant cell arteritis, and zoster emergencies, then sorts benign-appearing phenotypes (trigeminal neuralgia, cluster/TAC, dental, sinus/ear, TMJ, migraine). Defers to the disease-specific myMedKitt consult (AACG, cervical artery dissection, dental pain hub, trigeminal neuralgia, cluster headache, etc.) for detailed workup and dosing.

⚠ Unilateral facial pain is a chief complaint, not a diagnosis. Document eye, neurovascular, dental/deep infection, GCA, zoster, and primary headache/neuralgia pattern before discharge.
⚠ Time-critical exclusion: pick the single most dangerous active pattern first — eye/orbit, neurovascular, deep-space infection, GCA, and zoster red flags come before benign pattern labels.
⚠ Red painful eye, halos, vision loss, proptosis, painful EOM, or V1 zoster near the eye requires urgent ophthalmology thinking.
⚠ Neck pain, Horner syndrome, thunderclap onset, focal neurologic deficit, transient neurologic symptoms, or posterior headache should trigger neurovascular/dissection evaluation — do not let a normal facial or dental exam close the case when the history has neurologic or vascular structure.
⚠ Fever, toxic appearance, trismus, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth swelling, immunocompromise, or odontogenic swelling should trigger deep-space infection and airway evaluation. Do not send an airway-risk patient supine to CT without an airway plan.
⚠ Age >50 with new temporal/scalp/jaw symptoms, visual symptoms, PMR symptoms, or constitutional syndrome should trigger GCA evaluation and empiric steroids when suspicion is meaningful — do not wait for biopsy/ultrasound before steroids when vision is at risk.
⚠ Do not call this "sinus" or "TMJ" until the eye, neurovascular, infection, GCA, and zoster lanes are actively considered.
⚠ Admit, transfer, or keep under specialty service when there is threatened vision, orbital cellulitis, AACG, neurovascular diagnosis, suspected CNS infection, SAH/ICH/stroke, GCA with visual symptoms, deep-space infection, airway concern, immunocompromised severe infection, uncontrolled pain/vomiting, unsafe ambulation/neurologic exam, or inability to obtain needed urgent follow-up.

1 · First pass — name the dangerous buckets before you narrow

Goal: decide whether this is dangerous eye/orbit, neurovascular, deep infection, GCA, zoster, cranial neuralgia, TAC/cluster, dental, sinus/ear, TMJ, or migraine/headache phenotype. Do not call this "sinus" or "TMJ" until the eye, neurovascular, infection, GCA, and zoster lanes are actively considered.[1][2][4][7][8]

Eye / orbit emergency lane
Open AACG, Orbital Cellulitis, or Monocular Vision Loss Hub based on the exam. Immediate exam: visual acuity, pupils, EOM, IOP when angle closure is plausible, fluorescein/slit lamp, lid/orbit exam, proptosis, fever, cranial nerves. High-risk patterns: mid-dilated pupil, corneal haze, halos, nausea/vomiting, very high IOP (AACG); painful/restricted EOM, diplopia, proptosis, fever, eyelid edema (orbital cellulitis or cavernous sinus process); V1 rash, tip-of-nose involvement, red eye, photophobia, decreased vision (HZO with eye involvement). Disposition: ophthalmology now for AACG, HZO eye involvement, orbital cellulitis, vision loss, or uncertain ocular emergency. CT orbit/sinuses with contrast when orbital cellulitis or deep extension is possible. Do not discharge without visual acuity and an ophthalmology plan when objective eye risk is present.[5][10][11]
Neurovascular / dissection lane
Open Cervical Artery Dissection, Stroke, SAH, ICH, or Anisocoria based on the dominant finding. Dissection clues: unilateral face/head/neck pain, partial Horner syndrome, transient monocular vision loss, TIA/stroke symptoms, posterior headache/neck pain, recent neck trauma/manipulation, cough/vomiting/exertion trigger. Imaging: CTA head/neck is the practical ED first-line study when dissection, stroke, or aneurysmal process is plausible. Non-contrast CT head first if thunderclap, ICH, SAH, or severe sudden headache is the dominant concern. MRI/MRA can follow when CTA/CT is unrevealing but suspicion remains. Do not let a normal facial or dental exam close the case when the history has neurologic or vascular structure.[4][5][6]
Dental / deep-space infection lane
Open Dental Pain Hub, Deep Neck Infection, or Orbital Cellulitis. Airway and source screen: trismus, muffled voice, drooling, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth elevation, neck swelling, immunocompromise, diabetes, toxic appearance, odontogenic swelling, periorbital spread. Imaging: CT face/neck with IV contrast when deep-space infection, orbital extension, Ludwig angina, osteomyelitis, abscess, or complicated odontogenic infection is possible. Do not send an airway-risk patient supine to CT without an airway plan. Treatment principles: analgesia, source control, antibiotics when systemic involvement or spreading infection is present, OMFS/ENT/dental consult as indicated. Uncomplicated pulpitis without swelling/systemic illness usually needs dental source control, not empiric antibiotics.[8]
Giant cell arteritis lane
Age >50 with new unilateral temple/scalp/facial pain triggers GCA evaluation — especially with jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, visual symptoms, diplopia, PMR shoulder/hip symptoms, constitutional symptoms, abnormal temporal artery, or unexplained inflammatory syndrome. ED workup: ESR, CRP, CBC with platelets, CMP. Normal inflammatory markers reduce likelihood but do not fully exclude GCA when the clinical story is high risk. Treatment: if suspected, start glucocorticoids and arrange urgent rheumatology/ophthalmology follow-up. If visual symptoms, diplopia, or threatened vision are present, treat as vision-threatening GCA and involve ophthalmology/rheumatology urgently. Do not wait for biopsy/ultrasound before steroids when clinical suspicion is meaningful and vision is at risk.[7]
Zoster / cranial neuropathy lane
Patterns: dermatomal burning or allodynia before rash, vesicles, V1 forehead/scalp involvement, nose-tip involvement, red eye/photophobia/vision change, ear vesicles, hearing symptoms, vertigo, or facial weakness. HZO eye risk: any V1 rash plus ocular symptoms, red eye, decreased vision, photophobia, dendritic/pseudodendritic lesions, or tip-of-nose involvement needs careful eye exam and ophthalmology plan. Treatment: antiviral therapy is most useful early but should not be withheld solely because symptoms are beyond 72 hours if new lesions, immunocompromise, ocular involvement, or severe disease exists. Treat pain, avoid topical steroids unless ophthalmology directs, and escalate Ramsay Hunt/facial palsy cases. Pre-eruptive zoster can mimic trigeminal neuralgia or dental pain — recheck skin/eye and give return precautions if no rash yet.[10]

Trigeminal neuralgia pattern
Open Trigeminal Neuralgia. Classic phenotype: recurrent unilateral brief electric-shock pains in trigeminal distribution, abrupt onset/termination, severe intensity, triggerable by innocuous stimuli, no objective sensory loss. MRI / secondary-cause triggers: sensory loss, bilateral symptoms, age <40, constant pain between paroxysms, hearing loss, brainstem signs, multiple sclerosis history, cancer/immunosuppression, atypical V1-predominant presentation, or first presentation with abnormal neurologic exam. ED treatment bridge: carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine is evidence-supported first-line for classic TN. Screen ancestry risk for **HLA-B*1502** before carbamazepine in patients with relevant Asian ancestry; arrange neurology follow-up and imaging plan.[1][3]
Cluster / TAC pattern
Open Cluster Headache. Cluster phenotype: strictly unilateral orbital/supraorbital/temporal severe attacks lasting 15-180 minutes, with ipsilateral autonomic signs or restlessness/agitation. Attacks often cluster by circadian/circannual pattern. ED treatment: high-flow 100% oxygen by non-rebreather and a triptan when not contraindicated. Treat pain while still screening first/atypical presentations for secondary causes. Image/consult triggers: first-ever cluster phenotype, abnormal neuro exam, atypical duration, persistent Horner outside attacks, older age at onset, cancer/immunosuppression, thunderclap, or non-remitting pain.[2][4]
Dental / odontogenic pattern
Open Dental Pain Hub. Look for: caries, cracked tooth, percussion sensitivity, focal gingival swelling, fluctuance, facial cellulitis, immunocompromise, fever, trismus, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth elevation, and airway symptoms. Antibiotics: uncomplicated pulpitis or localized apical periodontitis without systemic involvement generally needs dental treatment and analgesia, not antibiotics. Antibiotics are appropriate when there is systemic involvement, spreading cellulitis, deep-space concern, immunocompromise, or source control delay with infection signs. Do not miss: deep neck infection, Ludwig angina, orbital spread, osteomyelitis, cavernous sinus thrombosis.[8]
Sinus / ear pattern
Bacterial rhinosinusitis criteria: persistent symptoms >10 days without improvement, severe fever with purulent nasal discharge or facial pain for at least 3-4 consecutive days, or double-worsening after initial improvement. Avoid the trap: unilateral facial pain alone is not enough to diagnose bacterial sinusitis. Dental, migraine, TN, cluster/TAC, HZO, and eye disease commonly masquerade as "sinus" pain. Image/urgent concern: orbital symptoms, severe frontal headache, neurologic signs, altered mental status, meningismus, immunocompromise, toxic appearance, proptosis, painful EOM, or failure of appropriate therapy. Ear clues: vesicles/facial weakness suggests Ramsay Hunt; severe otitis externa in diabetes/immunocompromise raises malignant otitis externa concern; mastoid tenderness/swelling raises mastoiditis concern.[9]
TMJ / myofascial pattern
Typical: jaw clicking/locking, chewing-related pain, masseter/temporalis tenderness, bruxism, limited opening from pain rather than trismus, normal eye/neuro exam, no fever, no dental swelling. Do not overcall TMJ when: true trismus, fever, facial swelling, dental abscess, floor-of-mouth elevation, neurologic signs, temporal artery symptoms, vision change, or first severe headache is present. ED management: NSAID/acetaminophen if safe, soft diet, heat/ice, avoid extreme jaw opening, dental/OMFS follow-up for persistent locking, malocclusion, trauma, or severe dysfunction. TMJ is a diagnosis after eye, neurovascular, infection, GCA, dental, and zoster concerns are not present.[4][8]
Migraine / primary headache pattern
Open Headache Hub or Migraine. Migraine can present as unilateral face, jaw, sinus, dental, or orbital pain. Diagnosis is stronger with prior similar attacks, photophobia/phonophobia, nausea, movement sensitivity, aura history, family history, and normal eye/neuro/dental exam. Still image or escalate when: first/worst, thunderclap, neurologic deficit, pregnancy/postpartum, cancer/immunosuppression, fever/meningismus, papilledema, age >50 new headache, trauma, anticoagulation, or abnormal ocular findings. Treat symptoms and reassess — a benign discharge requires objective improvement, normal neuro/eye exam, and clear return precautions.[4][5]

6 · Reassess and disposition

After treatment and targeted workup, choose the safest disposition lane. Disposition hinges on objective danger, diagnostic certainty, reassessment, and follow-up reliability.[4][5]

DispositionCriteria
Admit / transferObjective emergency, unsafe exam, specialty procedure, IV antibiotics, neurovascular diagnosis, or threatened vision — threatened vision, orbital cellulitis, AACG, neurovascular diagnosis, suspected CNS infection, SAH/ICH/stroke, GCA with visual symptoms, deep-space infection, airway concern, immunocompromised severe infection, uncontrolled pain/vomiting, unsafe ambulation/neurologic exam, or inability to obtain needed urgent follow-up
Observation / recheckUncertain diagnosis, persistent severe pain, needs serial eye/neuro/airway exam, or specialty decision pending — good endpoints are stable vitals, documented repeat exam, pain controlled with an outpatient plan, no progression of swelling/rash/neuro signs, and a specific follow-up route
Discharge with safety netClear benign pattern, normal eye/neuro/mouth/skin exam, pain controlled, reliable follow-up — reasonable only when: normal/explained eye exam; no focal neuro deficit, AMS, thunderclap, Horner, meningismus, or dissection story; no fever/toxic appearance/deep-space infection/trismus/dysphagia/floor-of-mouth swelling/spreading cellulitis; GCA and zoster eye risk considered when age/rash pattern fits; pain controlled and oral intake adequate; diagnosis-specific follow-up and return precautions understood

For a first severe unilateral facial pain without a confident benign phenotype, use observation or targeted imaging rather than a vague discharge diagnosis.[4][5][8][9]

7 · Return precautions

Return precautions must match the dangerous lanes
Return immediately for vision change, red eye, eye swelling, new rash near eye/nose/ear, facial weakness, weakness/numbness, speech trouble, confusion, severe sudden headache, neck pain with neurologic symptoms, fever, vomiting, worsening swelling, trouble swallowing, trouble opening mouth, drooling, shortness of breath, uncontrolled pain, or inability to obtain urgent dental/ophthalmology/neurology follow-up. Give the patient the likely working diagnosis and the dangerous diagnoses actively screened for.[4][8][10]

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls7 items
Do NOT call this "sinus" or "TMJ" until the eye, neurovascular, infection, GCA, and zoster lanes are actively considered.
Do NOT let a normal facial or dental exam close the case when the history has neurologic or vascular structure (Horner, transient neuro symptoms, posterior headache/neck pain, thunderclap).
Do NOT send an airway-risk patient supine to CT without an airway plan — trismus, drooling, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth elevation, and neck swelling are airway threats first.
Do NOT wait for biopsy/ultrasound before starting glucocorticoids in suspected GCA when clinical suspicion is meaningful and vision is at risk.
Do NOT diagnose bacterial sinusitis from unilateral facial pain alone — dental, migraine, TN, cluster/TAC, HZO, and eye disease commonly masquerade as "sinus" pain.
Do NOT overcall TMJ when true trismus, fever, facial swelling, dental abscess, floor-of-mouth elevation, neurologic signs, temporal artery symptoms, vision change, or first severe headache is present.
Do NOT use opioids as the main plan for recurrent neuralgia/headache, or antibiotics for uncomplicated dental pulpitis or viral URI; do not discharge before repeat eye/neuro/gait assessment if pain was severe or atypical.

Image/urgent concern: orbital symptoms, severe frontal headache, neurologic signs, altered mental status, meningismus, immunocompromise, toxic appearance, proptosis, painful EOM, or failure of appropriate therapy. Ear clues: vesicles/facial weakness suggests Ramsay Hunt; severe otitis externa in diabetes/immunocompromise raises malignant otitis externa concern; mastoid tenderness/swelling raises mastoiditis concern.[9]

Typical: jaw clicking/locking, chewing-related pain, masseter/temporalis tenderness, bruxism, limited opening from pain rather than trismus, normal eye/neuro exam, no fever, no dental swelling. Do not overcall TMJ when: true trismus, fever, facial swelling, dental abscess, floor-of-mouth elevation, neurologic signs, temporal artery symptoms, vision change, or first severe headache is present. ED management: NSAID/acetaminophen if safe, soft diet, heat/ice, avoid extreme jaw opening, dental/OMFS follow-up for persistent locking, malocclusion, trauma, or severe dysfunction. TMJ is a diagnosis after eye, neurovascular, infection, GCA, dental, and zoster concerns are not present.[4][8]

Open Headache Hub or Migraine. Migraine can present as unilateral face, jaw, sinus, dental, or orbital pain. Diagnosis is stronger with prior similar attacks, photophobia/phonophobia, nausea, movement sensitivity, aura history, family history, and normal eye/neuro/dental exam. Still image or escalate when: first/worst, thunderclap, neurologic deficit, pregnancy/postpartum, cancer/immunosuppression, fever/meningismus, papilledema, age >50 new headache, trauma, anticoagulation, or abnormal ocular findings. Treat symptoms and reassess — a benign discharge requires objective improvement, normal neuro/eye exam, and clear return precautions.[4][5]

4 · One-glance algorithm

Facial pain: screen dangerous buckets first — Eye/orbit, neurovascular, inflammatory (GCA), infection, zoster vs the diagnosis-after-exclusion buckets (TN, cluster/TAC, migraine, dental, sinusitis, TMJ).

Any time-critical red-flag pattern? — Pick the most dangerous active pattern first; return to phenotype sorting only after urgent threats are addressed.

Eye / orbit emergency — AACG, orbital cellulitis, HZO eye involvement. Visual acuity, pupils, EOM, IOP if plausible, fluorescein. Ophthalmology now.

Neurovascular / dissection — Dissection, stroke/TIA, SAH/ICH, cavernous sinus thrombosis. CTA head/neck first-line; non-contrast CT head if thunderclap.

Deep-space infection — Airway/source screen, CT face/neck with contrast, antibiotics for systemic/spreading disease, OMFS/ENT/dental source control.

No danger signal — what does the pain behave like? — Use pain behavior and exam localization once dangerous lanes are cleared.

Giant cell arteritis — Age >50, jaw claudication, visual symptoms. ESR/CRP/CBC/CMP. Empiric glucocorticoids when suspicion is meaningful — do not wait for biopsy if vision is at risk.

Zoster / cranial neuropathy — Dermatomal burning/vesicles, HZO eye risk. Antivirals; do not withhold beyond 72h if red flags present.

Trigeminal neuralgia — Electric-shock V2/V3 triggered pain. Carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine first-line; HLA-B*1502 screen; MRI if age <40 or atypical.

Cluster headache / TAC — Unilateral orbital attacks 15-180 min + autonomic signs. High-flow 100% O2 + triptan; image atypical/first presentations.

Dental / odontogenic — Caries, percussion pain, gingival swelling. Dental source control is definitive; antibiotics only for systemic/spreading disease.

Rhinosinusitis — >10 days persistent, or severe fever+purulence >=3-4 days, or double-worsening. Unilateral pain alone is not diagnostic.

TMJ / myofascial — Jaw click/lock, masseter tenderness, normal eye/neuro/dental exam. Diagnosis after dangerous lanes excluded.

Migraine / primary headache — Photophobia, nausea, prior similar attacks. Still image first/worst, thunderclap, or abnormal exam.

5 · Testing, treatment bridge (applies after any lane)

Lane | Study / test

Eye/orbit | Visual acuity, fluorescein/slit lamp, IOP when angle closure plausible, CT orbit/sinuses with contrast for orbital cellulitis/extension

Neurovascular | Non-contrast CT head for sudden severe/SAH/ICH concern; CTA head/neck for dissection, aneurysm, stroke/TIA pattern, Horner, or transient neurologic symptoms; MRI/MRA if CTA/CT unrevealing and suspicion remains

Deep infection | CT face/neck with IV contrast when trismus, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth swelling, neck swelling, toxic appearance, immunocompromise, or deep-space spread is possible

GCA | ESR, CRP, CBC with platelets, CMP; do not delay steroids for biopsy/ultrasound when vision risk is present

TN | MRI brain/trigeminal protocol when secondary features or new diagnosis needing outpatient confirmation

Sinus | No routine CT for uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis; image when orbital/intracranial complication is plausible

Analgesia: acetaminophen, NSAID if safe, targeted nerve pain plan when TN/zoster likely, antiemetic/migraine regimen when migraine phenotype.[2][3][4]

Time-sensitive treatment | Action

AACG | IOP-lowering regimen and ophthalmology

Cluster | High-flow oxygen and triptan if not contraindicated

GCA | Glucocorticoids when suspected, urgent ophtho/rheum for vision symptoms

HZO | Antiviral therapy and ophthalmology when eye risk

Deep infection | Broad antibiotics, airway plan, CT with contrast, source control

Dissection/stroke/SAH/ICH | Use the disease-specific pathway

Opioids as the main plan for recurrent neuralgia/headache; antibiotics for uncomplicated dental pulpitis or viral URI; discharge before repeat eye/neuro/gait assessment if pain was severe or atypical.[2][3][4][7][8][10]

6 · Reassess and disposition

After treatment and targeted workup, choose the safest disposition lane. Disposition hinges on objective danger, diagnostic certainty, reassessment, and follow-up reliability.[4][5]

Disposition | Criteria

Admit / transfer | Objective emergency, unsafe exam, specialty procedure, IV antibiotics, neurovascular diagnosis, or threatened vision — threatened vision, orbital cellulitis, AACG, neurovascular diagnosis, suspected CNS infection, SAH/ICH/stroke, GCA with visual symptoms, deep-space infection, airway concern, immunocompromised severe infection, uncontrolled pain/vomiting, unsafe ambulation/neurologic exam, or inability to obtain needed urgent follow-up

Observation / recheck | Uncertain diagnosis, persistent severe pain, needs serial eye/neuro/airway exam, or specialty decision pending — good endpoints are stable vitals, documented repeat exam, pain controlled with an outpatient plan, no progression of swelling/rash/neuro signs, and a specific follow-up route

Discharge with safety net | Clear benign pattern, normal eye/neuro/mouth/skin exam, pain controlled, reliable follow-up — reasonable only when: normal/explained eye exam; no focal neuro deficit, AMS, thunderclap, Horner, meningismus, or dissection story; no fever/toxic appearance/deep-space infection/trismus/dysphagia/floor-of-mouth swelling/spreading cellulitis; GCA and zoster eye risk considered when age/rash pattern fits; pain controlled and oral intake adequate; diagnosis-specific follow-up and return precautions understood

For a first severe unilateral facial pain without a confident benign phenotype, use observation or targeted imaging rather than a vague discharge diagnosis.[4][5][8][9]

7 · Return precautions

Return immediately for vision change, red eye, eye swelling, new rash near eye/nose/ear, facial weakness, weakness/numbness, speech trouble, confusion, severe sudden headache, neck pain with neurologic symptoms, fever, vomiting, worsening swelling, trouble swallowing, trouble opening mouth, drooling, shortness of breath, uncontrolled pain, or inability to obtain urgent dental/ophthalmology/neurology follow-up. Give the patient the likely working diagnosis and the dangerous diagnoses actively screened for.[4][8][10]

8 · Do NOT

Critical pitfalls

Do NOT call this "sinus" or "TMJ" until the eye, neurovascular, infection, GCA, and zoster lanes are actively considered.

Do NOT let a normal facial or dental exam close the case when the history has neurologic or vascular structure (Horner, transient neuro symptoms, posterior headache/neck pain, thunderclap).

Do NOT send an airway-risk patient supine to CT without an airway plan — trismus, drooling, dysphagia, floor-of-mouth elevation, and neck swelling are airway threats first.

Do NOT wait for biopsy/ultrasound before starting glucocorticoids in suspected GCA when clinical suspicion is meaningful and vision is at risk.

Do NOT diagnose bacterial sinusitis from unilateral facial pain alone — dental, migraine, TN, cluster/TAC, HZO, and eye disease commonly masquerade as "sinus" pain.

Do NOT overcall TMJ when true trismus, fever, facial swelling, dental abscess, floor-of-mouth elevation, neurologic signs, temporal artery symptoms, vision change, or first severe headache is present.

Do NOT use opioids as the main plan for recurrent neuralgia/headache, or antibiotics for uncomplicated dental pulpitis or viral URI; do not discharge before repeat eye/neuro/gait assessment if pain was severe or atypical.
