Febrile Seizure: Chase Mimics, Not Epilepsy

Children 6 months to 5 years in the ED with a seizure accompanied by fever. Excludes neonates and infants under 6 months, known epilepsy, an afebrile seizure, or a seizure caused by CNS infection or another identified cause — each needs a different pathway.

⚠ Your job is NOT to rule out epilepsy — it is to detect dangerous mimics: bacterial meningitis, HSV encephalitis, febrile status epilepticus, and non-accidental trauma (NAT).
⚠ Simple FS = generalized + <15 min + a single episode in 24h + age 6 months to 5 years. AAP 2011 (reaffirmed 2025): no routine LP, labs, EEG, or imaging for a simple febrile seizure.
⚠ LP only for a reason: meningeal signs, persistent altered mentation, petechiae/purpura, ill appearance, antibiotic pretreatment, or under-vaccinated age <12 months. Do NOT delay empiric antibiotics for the LP.
⚠ Active seizure ≥5 minutes = status. Give a full weight-based benzodiazepine (lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV or midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM/IN); the #1 error is benzo UNDERdosing. Redose once at 5 minutes.
⚠ ESETT-era second-line agents — levetiracetam 60 mg/kg, fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg, or valproate 40 mg/kg — are equally effective; pick by safety profile.
⚠ Antipyretics do NOT prevent recurrence (AAP 2008, reaffirmed). Give acetaminophen or ibuprofen for comfort only; counsel parents accordingly.

4 · Workup — chase mimics, not diagnoses

Simple FS: no routine workup
AAP 2011 (reaffirmed 2025) for a simple febrile seizure: no routine LP, no routine labs, no routine EEG, no routine neuroimaging. Complex FS gets a targeted workup driven by the features that make it complex.[1]

LP is the single highest-stakes decision here. In the post-Hib/PCV era, bacterial meningitis presenting as a simple febrile seizure is <0.2%.[11]

  • Meningeal signs at any age
  • Persistent altered mentation >1 hour after the seizure
  • Bulging fontanelle, or petechiae / purpura
  • Antibiotic pretreatment within the prior 5–7 days (can mask meningitis)
  • Toxic appearance, or under-vaccinated age <12 months with concerning features
If meningitis is on the table — do NOT delay antibiotics for the LP
Stabilize ABCs, then give empiric meningitis-dose antibiotics: ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV + vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV. Add ampicillin if <1 month, and acyclovir if HSV encephalitis is a concern. CT first only if signs of raised ICP.[11]
Imaging, EEG, and labs — when they actually help

CT (urgent, before LP): focal deficit with persistent neuro findings, signs of raised ICP, suspected NAT, or suspected HSV / abscess / hemorrhage. MRI (when stable): complex or recurrent FS, or febrile status (FEBSTAT).[7]

EEG does not predict recurrence or epilepsy in simple FS. Reserve it for suspected non-convulsive status, prolonged AMS in febrile status, or recurrent complex events with an abnormal exam.[4]

Labs for the seizure itself: bedside glucose and sodium (hyponatremic seizures in infants); calcium/magnesium only for recurrent seizures or a suggestive history. Fever labs follow the age-stratified pediatric-fever algorithm, not the seizure.[4]

5 · Fever control & disposition

Antipyretics are for comfort, NOT prevention
AAP 2008 (reaffirmed): scheduled or symptomatic acetaminophen and ibuprofen do not reduce febrile-seizure recurrence. Telling parents aggressive fever control will prevent the next seizure is incorrect and only creates anxiety.[3]

Simple FS — discharge home
Most simple febrile seizures go home with reassurance and return precautions once the child is back to baseline, tolerating PO, has a reliable caregiver, and a benign fever source is identified.[1]
  • Return-precaution triggers: another seizure
  • Persistent lethargy, trouble waking, or altered mentation
  • Stiff neck, severe headache, or photophobia
  • Persistent vomiting or a non-blanching rash
Recurrence & counseling — what to tell parents

Recurrence is ~30% overall (about 50% if the first event was before 12 months). Lifetime epilepsy risk after a simple FS is 1-2% — counsel parents that most children grow out of these.[8]

No anticonvulsant prophylaxis after simple or most complex FS; rescue diazepam is reserved for after febrile status and is neurology-coordinated. Continue routine vaccinations on schedule — vaccine-associated FS is benign.[12]
