Febrile Seizure: Chase Mimics, Not Epilepsy

Children 6 months to 5 years in the ED with a seizure accompanied by fever (≥38°C), with no CNS infection or other identified cause (AAP definition). Excludes infants <6 months, known epilepsy with breakthrough seizure, an afebrile seizure, or a seizure already explained by CNS infection — each needs a different framework.

⚠ Anchoring on "febrile seizure" before excluding dangerous mimics is the most common error — bacterial meningitis, HSV encephalitis, febrile status epilepticus, and non-accidental trauma (NAT) must be actively excluded, not assumed away.
⚠ Antipyretics do NOT prevent recurrence (AAP 2008, reaffirmed). Give acetaminophen or ibuprofen for comfort only — telling families aggressive fever control prevents the next seizure is incorrect and creates anxiety.
⚠ Benzodiazepine underdosing is the most common error in pediatric status epilepticus — give the full weight-based dose on the first attempt and redose once at 5 minutes if the seizure continues.
⚠ Do NOT delay empiric antibiotics for the LP when bacterial meningitis is suspected — give meningitis-dose antibiotics (± dexamethasone) first if the child is seriously ill.
⚠ No anticonvulsant prophylaxis after simple or most complex febrile seizures — side effects (cognitive, hepatic, behavioral) outweigh a modest reduction in recurrence; rescue diazepam is reserved for after febrile status, neurology-coordinated.

2 · Simple FS — home, no workup

  • Returned to neurologic and behavioral baseline
  • Tolerating PO, reliable caregiver understands return precautions
  • Identified benign source of fever (or appropriate fever workup completed)
  • No concerning exam findings; outpatient follow-up arranged
No routine LP, labs, EEG, or imaging
AAP 2011 (reaffirmed 2025): a simple febrile seizure needs no routine LP, labs, EEG, or neuroimaging.[1] Discharge with reassurance and return precautions.
  • Another seizure
  • Persistent altered mentation, lethargy, or trouble waking
  • Stiff neck, severe headache, or photophobia
  • Persistent vomiting, dehydration, or a non-blanching rash
  • Fever >5 days without improvement

Imaging, EEG, and labs — when they help3 items

CT (urgent, before LP): focal deficit, signs of ↑ICP, suspected NAT, suspected HSV/abscess/hemorrhage. MRI (when stable): complex/recurrent FS, or febrile status (FEBSTAT — hippocampal injury risk).[4]

EEG does not predict recurrence or epilepsy in simple FS. Reserve for suspected non-convulsive status, prolonged AMS in febrile status, or recurrent complex events with an abnormal exam.[4]

Labs for the seizure itself: bedside glucose and sodium (hyponatremic seizures, especially <1 year). Fever labs follow the age-stratified Pediatric Fever consult, not the seizure workup.[4]

Overall recurrence risk after first febrile seizure: ~30% (age <12 months at first event is the single largest risk factor). Lifetime epilepsy risk after a simple febrile seizure is 1-2% (vs 0.5-1% baseline) — most children grow out of the susceptibility by age 5.[8][9]

  1. "Your child had a febrile seizure — the most common seizure type in childhood, with an excellent prognosis."
  2. "About 1 in 3 children will have another. That does NOT change the long-term outlook."
  3. "Here is when to come back" — another seizure, stiff neck, non-blanching rash, persistent AMS, breathing trouble, fever >5 days.
  4. Continue routine vaccines on schedule — vaccine-triggered febrile seizure is benign (MMR/MMRV peak 8-14 days, DTaP peak 0-2 days).

4 · Active seizure — full weight-based dosing

Give the **full weight-based dose** on the first attempt, then redose once at 5 minutes if the seizure continues. Outcome tracks time-to-first-benzo and time-to-second-line agent.{{esett2019}}

Lorazepam (IV access): 1.2 IV push over 2 min, max 4 mg — Preferred when IV/IO is available. Onset 1-3 min.

Diazepam (last resort): 6 PR, one time, max 20 mg — Slower onset — use only when midazolam is unavailable.

Fosphenytoin: 240 IV over 10 min, max 1500 mg PE — Requires continuous cardiac monitoring (bradycardia, hypotension).

Valproate: 480 IV over 10 min, max 3000 mg — Avoid in hepatic disease, suspected metabolic disorder, or pregnancy.

Critical pitfalls
