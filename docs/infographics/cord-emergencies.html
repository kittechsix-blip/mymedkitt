Cord Emergencies — ED Management

ED management of umbilical cord-related obstetric emergencies during labor/delivery of a viable pregnancy — umbilical cord prolapse, nuchal cord at delivery, vasa previa, and cord accidents/true knots. Assumes standard delivery mechanics (shoulder delivery, cord clamping, neonatal assessment, placenta, oxytocin) are handled per the Precipitous Delivery consult; this consult covers the cord-specific decision points only.

⚠ Cord palpable/visible after ROM = umbilical cord prolapse, a true emergency — decision-to-delivery target <30 minutes (RCOG)
⚠ Do NOT attempt to reduce a prolapsed cord back into the uterus — historically taught, now discouraged as ineffective and worsening vasospasm
⚠ Tight nuchal cord: attempt the somersault maneuver BEFORE clamp-and-cut — cutting before delivery commits to immediate delivery and risks fetal hypoxia/anemia if shoulder dystocia follows
⚠ Vasa previa with ROM + bleeding + sinusoidal/bradycardic FHT = STAT C-section — fetal blood volume is only 80–100 mL/kg, exsanguination occurs within minutes
⚠ Magnesium sulfate is NOT an appropriate acute tocolytic for cord compression — onset 20+ minutes is too slow; reserve for eclampsia/neuroprotection

Overview

Cord emergencies are time-critical obstetric events — recognition → decompression/repositioning → emergent delivery determines neonatal outcome. Four ED scenarios: umbilical cord prolapse, nuchal cord at delivery, vasa previa, and true knots/cord accidents. Page OB, Anesthesia, and Neonatology immediately for any suspected cord emergency.

Palpable/visible cord after ROM → cord prolapse (1 in 200–500 deliveries). Cord around neck after head delivers → nuchal cord (~20–35% of deliveries). Painless PV bleed + sinusoidal/bradycardic FHT after ROM → vasa previa (1 in 2,500). Unexplained sudden fetal bradycardia without visible cord, abruption, or rupture → cord accident/true knot.

▶ What to do now

Cord prolapse — act now
Page OB/Anesthesia/NICU/OR. Do not leave the patient. Position (knee-chest or left-lateral Trendelenburg), continuous manual elevation of the presenting part, bladder fill if transport delayed, tocolysis. Decision-to-delivery target <30 minutes (RCOG).
Nuchal cord — assess reducibility
Sweep finger around neck after head delivers. Loose or tight-but-reducible → slip over head/shoulder. Too tight → somersault maneuver before clamp-and-cut.
Known vasa previa — planned delivery
Planned C-section at 34 0/7 – 37 0/7 weeks. Avoid digital cervical exam and amniotomy. ROM, labor, bleeding, or NR tracing → STAT C-section regardless of gestational age.
Acute vasa previa — STAT C-section
Every minute matters — fetal blood volume only 80–100 mL/kg. Page OB/Anesthesia/NICU, prepare O-negative neonatal blood, do NOT perform digital exam or amniotomy. Do not delay C-section for Apt test results.
Cord accident — in-utero resuscitation
Left lateral tilt, O2, IV fluid bolus, stop oxytocin, vaginal exam for occult prolapse, tocolysis if no improvement. Failure to improve in 5–10 minutes → deliver.

Umbilical Cord Prolapse

Epidemiology: 0.1–0.6% of deliveries (~1% in breech). Perinatal mortality <10% with prompt C-section; out-of-hospital mortality remains 38–44%. Risk factors: breech (especially footling), transverse/oblique lie, unengaged presenting part, polyhydramnios, prematurity, multiparity, long cord >80 cm, second twin. ~50% are iatrogenic (AROM at high station, amnioinfusion, ECV, IUPC/scalp electrode placement).

  • OB — decision-to-delivery goal <30 min (many centers target <15 min)
  • Anesthesia — STAT, for general or spinal
  • NICU / Neonatology — prepared for resuscitation at delivery
  • OR team — open the room, call the surgical tech
  • Activate massive transfusion protocol if maternal bleeding (abruption or uterine rupture comorbidity)
  • Knee-chest (genupectoral) — most gravity assistance; best for stationary OR prep
  • Trendelenburg with left lateral tilt — best for transport and IV access
  • Exaggerated Sims — left lateral with hips elevated on pillows; good for rural transfer
  • All positions: left lateral tilt ≥15° to decompress the IVC. Never transport supine.

Nuchal Cord at Delivery

Present in ~20–35% of all deliveries; most clinically insignificant. Tight nuchal cords (unable to slip over head) occur in ~6%. Collins classification: Type A ("unlocked/free") can spontaneously unwind and slips over the head easily; Type B ("locked") cannot spontaneously unwind and carries higher risk for fetal distress and stillbirth.

  1. Keep fetal head flexed and close to mother's thigh/perineum — chin to chest
  2. Deliver the anterior shoulder with gentle downward traction
  3. Deliver the posterior shoulder with gentle upward traction
  4. As the body delivers, allow the fetus to "somersault" up and over the perineum while the head stays near the thigh
  5. Baby ends face-up on the mother's abdomen/thigh with cord still intact and wrapped
  6. Unwind the cord AFTER complete delivery
Why somersault beats clamp-and-cut
Preserves placental transfusion (~30% of neonatal blood volume). Avoids iatrogenic neonatal hypovolemia and anemia — devastating if shoulder dystocia then occurs. Cutting a nuchal cord before delivery is associated with neonatal anemia, HIE, and cerebral palsy when followed by delayed delivery.
Reference — clamp and cut is LAST RESORT ONLY (if somersault fails)
Only if the somersault maneuver has failed and delivery cannot otherwise progress. Cutting commits you to immediate, unobstructed delivery — if shoulder dystocia then develops, the fetus has no placental circulation → acute hypoxia and anemia. Technique if committed: double-clamp with two Kelly clamps close together, cut between them, deliver immediately, anticipate possible shoulder dystocia, have the neonatal team ready for possible volume resuscitation (10 mL/kg NS or O-neg blood). Before cutting, ask: "Is delivery truly obstructed?" In most cases, the somersault maneuver succeeds if attempted properly.

Cord managed — continue with delivery per standard steps: unwind any remaining cord loops, skin-to-skin unless resuscitation needed, delayed cord clamping 30–60 sec if vigorous (unless the nuchal cord was cut — no benefit), APGAR at 1 and 5 min. If neonate pale, hypotonic, HR <100 → neonatal resuscitation, may need volume for anemia.

Document number of loops, tightness, maneuver used, and any complications. Placental exam for cord length, vessel count (2 arteries + 1 vein), and true knots.

Vasa Previa

Antenatal diagnosis: transvaginal US with color Doppler identifies fetal vessels crossing over or within 2 cm of the internal cervical os, typically 2nd trimester. Undiagnosed acute presentation — classic triad after ROM: painless vaginal bleeding (fetal blood), sinusoidal or bradycardic fetal heart tracing, and pulsating vessels palpable through the dilated cervix (do NOT repeat digital exam if suspected). Fetal blood volume is only 80–100 mL/kg (~250 mL at term) — exsanguination occurs within minutes.

Planned C-section at 34 0/7 – 37 0/7 weeks (SMFM #37). Antenatal corticosteroids for lung maturity at 28–32 wks. Avoid digital cervical exam and amniotomy. If ROM without bleeding → STAT C-section regardless of gestational age. If labor, bleeding, or NR fetal tracing → STAT C-section. If stable/not in labor/intact membranes → admit, confirm US, steroids if not given, continuous FHR monitoring.

  • Page OB, Anesthesia, NICU — activate massive transfusion (neonatal side)
  • Prepare neonatal blood: O-negative, CMV-negative, irradiated, 10 mL/kg ready at delivery
  • Maternal large-bore IV × 2, type & crossmatch, notify blood bank
  • High-flow O2 10 L/min NRB
  • Left lateral tilt ≥15°
  • Do NOT perform digital cervical exam or amniotomy
  1. Mix the bloody fluid with a small amount of water in a test tube (hemolyzes RBCs)
  2. Centrifuge briefly
  3. Transfer 5 mL supernatant to a new tube
  4. Add 1 mL of 1% KOH to the supernatant
  5. Wait 1–2 minutes: pink/red = fetal hemoglobin (confirms vasa previa); brown/yellow = maternal hemoglobin
  6. Do NOT delay delivery to wait for results if fetal compromise is present

Cord Accidents & True Knots

Differential for fetal bradycardia: cord compression (nuchal, body entanglement, true knot, cord around limb), occult cord prolapse (re-examine immediately), placental abruption, uterine rupture (especially prior cesarean), maternal hypotension, hyperstimulation (oxytocin tachysystole). True knots ~1% of pregnancies — risk factors: long cord >70 cm, polyhydramnios, small fetus, monoamniotic twins, advanced maternal age, male fetus. Increases stillbirth risk ~4-fold but most remain loose. Diagnosis rarely antenatal — usually a postnatal finding.

  1. Positioning — left lateral tilt ≥15°; trial right lateral or knee-chest if not improving
  2. Maternal oxygen — 10 L/min non-rebreather mask
  3. IV fluid bolus — 500 mL–1 L crystalloid
  4. Stop oxytocin immediately — tachysystole is a major reversible cause
  5. Vaginal exam — rule out occult cord prolapse
  6. Tocolysis if no improvement — terbutaline 0.25 mg SC or NTG 50–100 mcg IV
  7. Continuous FHR monitoring — expect improvement within 1–2 minutes
  8. Failure to improve in 5–10 minutes → delivery

All monoamniotic-monochorionic (mono-mono) twins have cord entanglement — historical stillbirth rate 30–70%, now 10–20% with intensive antenatal surveillance. Standard: inpatient continuous FHR monitoring from 24–28 weeks, antenatal corticosteroids at 28 weeks, planned C-section at 32 0/7 – 34 0/7 weeks. If a mono-mono twin pregnancy presents with sudden fetal bradycardia at any gestational age → STAT C-section; do NOT attempt vaginal delivery. Distinguish from dichorionic twins (standard delivery rules) and monochorionic-diamniotic twins (no cord entanglement).

Resuscitation successful (FHR normalizes) → continue monitoring, investigate/treat reversible causes, OB consult. Resuscitation fails or events recur → Category 1 or 2 C-section, or operative vaginal delivery if fully dilated and +2 or lower with experienced provider. At delivery: inspect the cord (true knots, loops, length, insertion), cord blood gas, APGAR, placental pathology if abnormal.

Critical Pitfalls — Do NOT (Reference)

Critical pitfalls — reference only
Do NOT attempt to reduce a prolapsed cord back into the uterus. Do NOT leave cord exposed or handle it repeatedly (vasospasm). Do NOT clamp/cut a tight nuchal cord as first-line — try the somersault maneuver first. Do NOT transport a cord prolapse patient supine. Do NOT rupture membranes in known/suspected vasa previa. Do NOT administer oxytocin during active cord prolapse. Do NOT perform a digital cervical exam in suspected vasa previa with PV bleeding.

  • Continuous FHR monitoring — expect improvement within 1–2 minutes
  • Failure to improve in 5–10 minutes → delivery

All monoamniotic-monochorionic (mono-mono) twins have cord entanglement — historical stillbirth rate 30–70%, now 10–20% with intensive antenatal surveillance. Standard: inpatient continuous FHR monitoring from 24–28 weeks, antenatal corticosteroids at 28 weeks, planned C-section at 32 0/7 – 34 0/7 weeks. If a mono-mono twin pregnancy presents with sudden fetal bradycardia at any gestational age → STAT C-section; do NOT attempt vaginal delivery. Distinguish from dichorionic twins (standard delivery rules) and monochorionic-diamniotic twins (no cord entanglement).

Resuscitation successful (FHR normalizes) → continue monitoring, investigate/treat reversible causes, OB consult. Resuscitation fails or events recur → Category 1 or 2 C-section, or operative vaginal delivery if fully dilated and +2 or lower with experienced provider. At delivery: inspect the cord (true knots, loops, length, insertion), cord blood gas, APGAR, placental pathology if abnormal.

Critical Pitfalls — Do NOT (Reference)

Do NOT attempt to reduce a prolapsed cord back into the uterus. Do NOT leave cord exposed or handle it repeatedly (vasospasm). Do NOT clamp/cut a tight nuchal cord as first-line — try the somersault maneuver first. Do NOT transport a cord prolapse patient supine. Do NOT rupture membranes in known/suspected vasa previa. Do NOT administer oxytocin during active cord prolapse. Do NOT perform a digital cervical exam in suspected vasa previa with PV bleeding.
