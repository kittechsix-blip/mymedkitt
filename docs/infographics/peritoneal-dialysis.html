Triage — six problem domains

PD patients arrive sick from one of six problem domains. Identify the dominant complaint first — the workup branches early. Always drain a fresh exchange on arrival — the effluent is the single most useful diagnostic specimen.

  • When was the last exchange?
  • CAPD or APD?
  • Recent catheter trauma?
  • Last cloudy bag?
Always drain a fresh exchange
Always drain a fresh exchange on arrival — the effluent is the diagnostic specimen.

ED Procedure — Drain PD Fluid Safely

The PD catheter is already in place — the job is to use it correctly, sterilely, and capture the right specimens.

  1. Mask + cap on you AND the patient. Sterile gloves.
  2. Wipe transfer-set connection with chlorhexidine ×30 s.
  3. Connect an empty drain bag to the outflow port. Bag below abdomen.
  4. Open clamp → drain by gravity (200–300 mL/min, total 1.5–2.5 L over 10–20 min).
  5. Sample mid-stream effluent: 3 mL purple-top EDTA for cell count + diff (dwell must be ≥2 h); Gram stain tube; ≥5 mL into BOTH aerobic + anaerobic blood culture bottles directly at bedside.
  6. Close clamp. Cap transfer set with fresh povidone-iodine MiniCap.
  7. Do not refill if peritonitis suspected — next instill should include empiric IP antibiotics + heparin 500 U/L.

Diagnose PD-Associated Peritonitis

ISPD diagnostic criteria — need 2 of 3: (1) clinical features (abdominal pain ± fever); (2) cloudy effluent with WBC >100/µL after ≥2 h dwell, >50% PMN; (3) positive effluent culture. Inoculate 5–10 mL of effluent directly into two blood culture bottles at the bedside.

Catheter Infections — Exit-Site vs Tunnel

TypeFindings
Exit-site infectionPurulent drainage at the exit site ± erythema. Crust alone does not equal infection.
Tunnel infectionErythema, edema, or tenderness along the subcutaneous catheter tract — frequently occult. Ultrasound shows fluid around the cuff. Highest peritonitis risk.

Obtain exit-site swab for culture before starting antibiotics. Pseudomonas + S. aureus are the most aggressive organisms.

Empiric oral therapy (start before culture if mild): S. aureus suspected → dicloxacillin 500 mg PO QID or cephalexin 500 mg PO QID (MRSA → trimethoprim-sulfa or doxycycline). Pseudomonas suspected (green drainage, biofilm) → oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID × 3 weeks; add a second agent (gentamicin IP or oral) if severe. Duration: 2 weeks minimum, 3 weeks for Pseudomonas or S. aureus.

Mechanical Complications

Mechanical PD problems do not need antibiotics but can derail dialysis fast. Localize first.

  1. Constipation (most common cause) — KUB radiograph for stool burden + catheter tip position; polyethylene glycol or lactulose; avoid milk of magnesia in ESRD.
  2. Catheter kink or external clamp — visual check, reposition external segment.
  3. Fibrin plug — flush with heparinized saline 5000 U in 10 mL; adding heparin 500 U/L to all bags prevents recurrence.
  4. Catheter tip malposition (out of pelvis) — KUB shows tip cephalad; laxatives + ambulation first, fluoroscopic manipulation if persistent.
  5. Omental wrap — surgical omentopexy or catheter replacement.
Do not continue exchanges
Do not continue exchanges if drain is <50% of fill volume — risk of overfill leak or hernia.

Early leak (<30 days post-insertion): pericatheter wetness, scrotal/labial edema. Stop PD for 1–2 weeks → switch to temporary HD while the tract heals; resume with low-volume supine exchanges. Late leak: usually a hernia or abdominal wall defect — CT with intraperitoneal contrast localizes the tract. Genital edema: patent processus vaginalis or inguinal hernia tracking dialysate → surgical repair.

Leaks are not benign
Leaks predispose to peritonitis and worsen ultrafiltration.

Examine for incarceration / strangulation
Tender, irreducible, skin changes, peritonitis — surgical emergency. If reducible: low fill volumes + supine exchanges + elective repair; PD usually can resume 2 weeks post-repair. A new hernia is NOT a peritonitis substitute — still send effluent.

Sudden right-sided pleural effusion in a PD patient = pleuro-peritoneal communication. ~90% right-sided. Dyspnea may be the only symptom; fever is absent.

Treatment
Stop PD immediately. Transition to HD. Thoracoscopic pleurodesis or surgical repair of diaphragmatic defect for definitive management. PD can sometimes resume after successful pleurodesis.

Metabolic Emergencies

PD removes solutes slowly compared to HD. Severe metabolic derangements often need temporary HD rather than aggressive PD. Volume overload is the most common cause of hospitalization in PD patients.

ModalityK+ clearance
PD (CAPD, hourly 1.5% dextrose exchanges)10–15 mEq/h
HD50–80 mEq/h

Treat the membrane first, then move K+ around. Standard ED algorithm applies. For K >6.5 with ECG changes, arrange HD — not aggressive PD. Avoid kayexalate — does not work fast enough; sodium load worsens overload. Patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate are for outpatient maintenance only. If K >6.5 + ECG changes + no immediate HD access: 1.5% dextrose hourly exchanges + standard medical management bridges to HD.

Causes: peritoneal membrane dysfunction (high transporter status), missed exchanges, dietary indiscretion, residual renal function decline.

  • 4.25% dextrose dialysate (most hypertonic available) — short 2-h dwells maximize ultrafiltration.
  • Icodextrin for one long dwell (8–12 h) — sustained UF without glucose absorption rebound.
  • Furosemide IV 80–240 mg — useful if residual urine output >100 mL/day.
  • Bridge to HD if dyspnea, hypoxia, or hypertensive emergency.
Furosemide
80–240 mg IV
Useful if residual urine output >100 mL/day.

PD dialysate absorbs ~60–100 g glucose per day (more with 4.25%). Diabetic PD patients may need 25–50% higher insulin doses than during pre-dialysis CKD. Standard IV insulin + fluids for DKA / HHS. Avoid additional dextrose-containing dialysate during treatment. Icodextrin is glucose-sparing — switch to it if hyperglycemia is dialysate-driven.

Maltose interference
Icodextrin metabolites cause falsely elevated glucose on GDH-PQQ glucometers. Use plasma glucose, not fingerstick, during icodextrin therapy.

  • Consider transplant evaluation.
  • Tamoxifen + steroids show modest benefit in case series; surgical enterolysis at experienced centers for obstruction.

Indication to abandon PD acutely

Refractory peritonitis (no improvement at day 5)

Fungal peritonitis

Relapsing or recurrent peritonitis (≥2 episodes)

Tunnel infection with deep-cuff involvement

Hydrothorax (not amenable to pleurodesis)

Encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis

Persistent UF failure

  • Temporary HD catheter (internal jugular preferred; avoid subclavian — preserves future fistula sites).
  • Remove PD catheter within 24–48 h for fungal or refractory peritonitis; can defer in stable patients.
  • Permanent HD access (AV fistula > AV graft > tunneled catheter) — start the conversation in the ED.
  • Transplant referral if not already listed.

Full management algorithm

PD Complication — Triage — PD patients arrive sick from one of six problem domains. Always drain a fresh exchange on arrival — the effluent is the diagnostic specimen.

ED procedure: drain PD fluid safely — Sterile technique, gravity drain 200–300 mL/min, sample for cell count + Gram stain + blood culture bottles.

Diagnose peritonitis — 2 of 3 ISPD criteria? — Clinical features + WBC >100/µL with >50% PMN + positive culture. Need 2 of 3.

Empiric IP antibiotics within 6 h — Gram-positive (cefazolin or vancomycin) + gram-negative (ceftazidime or gentamicin), or cefepime monotherapy. Add heparin 500 U/L if cloudy.

Day 3–5 reassessment — Effluent should clear and pain improve within 72 h of effective therapy.

Targeted therapy by organism — Coag-negative Staph 2 weeks; S. aureus/Pseudomonas/Enterococcus 3 weeks; consider surgical cause if polymicrobial.

Refractory or recurrent peritonitis? — Refractory = failure to clear by day 5. Relapsing/recurrent = new episode within 4 weeks.

Fungal peritonitis — catheter out — Mortality >25%, catheter loss ~40%. Remove catheter within 24–48 h + fluconazole or echinocandin.

Non-infectious cloudy effluent — WBC <100 or no PMN predominance — eosinophilic, chemical, chyle, malignancy, or fibrin. No antibiotics.

Exit-site vs tunnel infection? — Tunnel infection has the highest peritonitis risk — image with ultrasound.

Exit-site treatment — oral antibiotics — Dicloxacillin/cephalexin for S. aureus, ciprofloxacin for Pseudomonas. 2–3 weeks. Do not remove catheter for isolated exit-site infection.

Tunnel infection — high risk — Concurrent peritonitis in ~40%. Broad IP coverage; plan catheter removal if no improvement at 2 weeks.

Mechanical complication — localize — Outflow failure, leak, hernia, or hydrothorax — no antibiotics needed but can derail dialysis fast.

Outflow failure — stepwise workup — Constipation (most common), kink, fibrin plug, tip malposition, omental wrap.

Dialysate leak — Early leak (<30 d) = stop PD, temporary HD. Late leak = hernia/defect, CT with IP contrast.

Abdominal wall hernia — 10–25% incidence. Examine for incarceration/strangulation — surgical emergency if present.

PD-related hydrothorax — Sudden right-sided effusion (~90%). Pleural fluid glucose > serum confirms. Stop PD, transition to HD.

Metabolic emergency — which type? — PD clears solutes slowly — severe derangements often need HD bridge rather than aggressive PD.

Hyperkalemia in PD patient — Standard ED hyperkalemia algorithm. For K >6.5 with ECG changes, arrange HD, not aggressive PD.

Volume overload / UF failure — 4.25% dextrose short dwells, icodextrin long dwell, furosemide if residual urine, bridge to HD if decompensating.

Hyperglycemia from dialysate — Standard DKA/HHS management. Use plasma glucose, not fingerstick, during icodextrin therapy.

Severe or late complication? — Bloody effluent, suspected EPS, or PD failure — can be life-threatening or end PD as a modality.

Hemoperitoneum — Most causes benign (menstrual, cyst rupture, exercise). Serious causes need CT + hemoglobin/coags.

Encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis — Rare but mortality 25–50% at 1 year. Fibrotic cocoon encases bowel. Stop PD permanently.

PD failure → HD bridge — Refractory/fungal/relapsing peritonitis, deep-cuff tunnel infection, hydrothorax, EPS, or persistent UF failure — abandon PD acutely.

Disposition — PD patients have higher admission rates than HD patients for infectious complications. Threshold to admit is low.

Admit — Renal / Medicine — Sepsis, hypotension, fungal suspicion, refractory disease, severe pain. Nephrology consult.

Admit — ICU — Hemodynamic instability, severe hyper-K with arrhythmia, large hydrothorax, perforated viscus.

Home with PD nurse follow-up — Mild peritonitis or exit-site infection in a reliable patient with same-day nurse visit.

Critical Actions (always-visible reference)

  • Send PD effluent for cell count, gram stain, culture in blood culture bottles
  • Start empiric IP antibiotics within 6 h — do not wait for culture
  • Cover gram-positive (cefazolin or vancomycin) + gram-negative (ceftazidime or aminoglycoside)
  • Add heparin 500 U/L to dialysate when effluent is cloudy
  • Treat hyper-K >6.5 first — do not wait for HD bridge
  • Suspect fungal peritonitis if effluent stays cloudy at 5 days — pull the catheter
  • Refractory or relapsing peritonitis = catheter removal + transition to HD

Disposition

Disposition | Criteria | Plan

Admit — Renal/Medicine | Sepsis, hypotension, fungal suspicion, refractory disease, severe pain | Continue IP antibiotics under nephrology guidance. Daily effluent cell counts + cultures until clear.

Admit — ICU | Hemodynamic instability, severe hyper-K with arrhythmia, large hydrothorax, perforated viscus | Hemodynamic monitoring, vasopressors if needed, urgent HD access planning. Surgical consult if indicated.

Home with PD nurse follow-up | Mild peritonitis or exit-site infection in a reliable patient with same-day nurse visit | Discharge with 24-h nephrology and PD nurse follow-up. Clear return precautions.

Requires: reliable patient, same- or next-day home PD nurse visit, ability to perform additional exchanges, clear return precautions (fever, worsening pain, no clearing by 72 h).
