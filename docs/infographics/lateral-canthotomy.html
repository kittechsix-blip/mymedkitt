Lateral Canthotomy & Inferior Cantholysis for Orbital Compartment Syndrome

Adults and children with suspected orbital compartment syndrome (retrobulbar hemorrhage) in the ED or trauma bay. Excludes stable orbital trauma without OCS signs, and definitive OR-level orbital decompression (oculoplastics/ophthalmology domain, reached only after this bedside procedure fails).

⚠ Orbital compartment syndrome is a CLINICAL diagnosis — do not delay decompression for CT, tonometry, or ophthalmology when the picture is obvious. Perform the procedure first, image after.
⚠ Vision loss is a LATE sign, present in fewer than half of OCS cases — act on proptosis, tense lids, and RAPD, not on blindness.
⚠ The CANTHOLYSIS decompresses the orbit (~30 mmHg drop); the canthotomy (skin cut) alone barely helps (~14 mmHg). Never stop at the skin cut.
⚠ Release the INFERIOR crus first; reserve the superior crus for persistent OCS — the lacrimal artery and gland bleed superiorly.
⚠ Retinal ischemia becomes irreversible at roughly 90–120 minutes — decompress within 2 hours of symptom onset.
⚠ Suspected globe rupture with a soft, non-tense orbit: do NOT perform canthotomy — shield the eye (no patch, no pressure) and get emergent ophthalmology. Suspected rupture with a tense, vision-threatened orbit: proceed with canthotomy and cantholysis while scrupulously avoiding any pressure on the globe.

1 · Recognize orbital compartment syndrome

The orbit is a closed bony box (~30 mL) sealed anteriorly only by the eyelids and orbital septum. A small added volume of retrobulbar blood spikes orbital and intraocular pressure, throttling the optic nerve and occluding the central retinal artery. It is the cantholysis, not the canthotomy, that decompresses the orbit: canthotomy alone drops IOP only ~14 mmHg; adding cantholysis drops it ~30 mmHg.

Relative proptosisGlobe bulges forward — compare to the other side
Tense, tight eyelidsResist opening; "rock-hard" globe resists retropulsion
RAPD (Marcus Gunn pupil)Relative afferent pupillary defect in the injured eye
Do not wait for vision loss
Vision loss is a LATE sign — present in fewer than half of OCS cases. A patient with proptosis, a tense orbit, and an RAPD has OCS until proven otherwise even if acuity is still measurable. Supporting signs: severe eye pain, ophthalmoplegia, markedly elevated IOP, chemosis, subconjunctival hemorrhage, cherry-red macula.

2 · Decide to decompress

Never delay decompression to obtain tonometry when the clinical picture is obvious, and never measure IOP if globe rupture is suspected. Normal IOP is 10–21 mmHg. IOP >30 mmHg with symptoms → call ophthalmology, prepare to decompress. IOP >40 mmHg → decompress now. Retinal ischemia becomes irreversible at roughly 90–120 minutes from symptom onset.

Decompress now
OCS is clinically obvious (proptosis + tense orbit + RAPD) or the IOP threshold is met. Do not wait for CT, tonometry, or ophthalmology — screen quickly for globe rupture, then proceed.
Equivocal — temporize and re-examine
Soft orbit, normal acuity and IOP — not clearly OCS yet. Serial exams every 15–30 min (visual acuity, pupil/RAPD, IOP, proptosis and lid tension); elevate head of bed; reverse anticoagulation if present and feasible; urgent ophthalmology consult and CT orbit to characterize the injury, never a reason to delay if the eye declares itself. Temporizing adjuncts (IV acetazolamide, mannitol, topical IOP-lowering drops) have a limited, secondary role and do not substitute for decompression. Return to the decompression pathway immediately if acuity drops, an RAPD appears, IOP climbs above threshold, or the orbit becomes tense.

3 · Screen for globe rupture

Globe rupture is the main contraindication — canthotomy and pressure on a ruptured globe extrudes intraocular contents. The signs overlap with OCS and a missed OCS also blinds, so screen quickly.

No rupture signs — proceed
Classic proptotic, high-pressure globe with no enophthalmos, teardrop pupil, hyphema, or Seidel sign — proceed to setup and the procedure.
Rupture likely, orbit NOT tense — shield, do not cut
Low IOP, enophthalmos — the orbit is not under compartment tension. Do NOT perform canthotomy. Protect the globe: rigid eye shield (no patch, no pressure), antiemetics, analgesia, tetanus, IV antibiotics, NPO. Emergent ophthalmology for operative repair; CT orbit.
Rupture possible but orbit IS tense — decompress gently
Both OCS and rupture are possible and the orbit is tense or vision is threatened. Proceed with canthotomy and inferior cantholysis while scrupulously avoiding any pressure on the globe — keep forceps and scissors directed away from the globe, lift tissue outward, never push inward. A threatened eye from OCS will be lost without decompression. Involve ophthalmology emergently, but do not let the consult delay decompression of a tense, vision-threatened orbit.

4 · Setup, canthotomy & cantholysis

  • Antiseptic, sterile drape, gauze, saline
  • Topical ophthalmic anesthetic drops (proparacaine/tetracaine)
  • Lidocaine 1–2% WITH epinephrine on a 25-gauge needle
  • Straight hemostat / Kelly clamp (for the crush step)
  • Iris / Stevens tenotomy scissors (preferred over a blade for control)
  • Toothed forceps ×2 (to grasp the lid and tension the crus)
  • Tono-Pen for pre/post IOP if available
Lidocaine 1–2% with epinephrine
1–2 mL local infiltration
25-gauge needle, lateral canthus, directed away from the globe
Fixed local volume, not weight-scaled. Topical anesthetic drops to the globe first. The canthal skin is relatively insensate after topical drops — do not let anesthesia setup stall the procedure in a rapidly failing eye.
  1. Lay a straight hemostat across the lateral canthus from the canthal angle to the orbital rim and crush for ~1 minute (devascularizes tissue, marks the incision line)
  2. With toothed forceps lifting the skin away from the globe, cut horizontally through the crushed lateral canthus toward the orbital rim, 2 cm maximum (temporal branch of the facial nerve runs nearby)
The skin cut alone does not decompress
Canthotomy only exposes the canthal tendon — drop in IOP from canthotomy alone is ~14 mmHg. You are not done; the next step is the one that works.
  1. Grasp the lower lid at the lateral "last lash" with toothed forceps and pull it up and out (toward the ceiling, away from the face) to put the inferior crus on tension
  2. Point the scissors inferiorly and posteriorly — toward the patient's nose, along the lateral orbital rim, away from the globe
  3. "Strum" the taut tendon with the closed scissor tips — it twangs like a plucked guitar string; you will work largely by feel
  4. Cut the inferior crus until the lower lid is completely free
Success = the swinging eyelid
The lateral lower lid now distracts freely from the globe with no tendon palpable on a forceps tug — an added ~30 mmHg IOP drop. A bloodless cut means the correct avascular tendon plane; brisk bleeding suggests too superior or too deep. The #1 failure is incomplete cantholysis — if the orbit stays tight, re-explore and finish dividing the inferior crus before doing anything else.

5 · Reassess & superior crus

Recheck immediately after the inferior cantholysis. Endpoints of success: IOP reduced (target <40 mmHg, ideally <30), softening of the previously rock-hard globe with reduced proptosis, improving visual acuity and resolving RAPD (can begin within minutes), and a freely swinging eyelid.

Decompressed — move to disposition
IOP <40 (ideally <30), globe soft, lid swings free. Procedure successful — move to disposition.
Still tense — release the superior crus
Confirm the inferior crus is fully released first. Grasp the upper lid laterally with toothed forceps, distract it away and superiorly to tension the superior crus, then cut it — staying directed away from the globe. The lacrimal gland, lacrimal artery, and levator aponeurosis run superiorly — higher bleeding risk and risk of post-procedure ptosis, which is why the superior crus is reserved for persistent OCS, not done routinely. If still not decompressed after both crura are released, this is refractory OCS — emergent ophthalmology/oculoplastics for OR orbital decompression. Medical adjuncts (mannitol, acetazolamide, HOB elevation) are temporizing only.

6 · Disposition & aftercare

  • Emergent ophthalmology / oculoplastics consult — EM success rates are comparable to ophthalmology; do not wait for them to perform it
  • CT orbit AFTER decompression to characterize fractures and associated injury — never a reason to have waited
  • The lid does not need immediate repair — lateral canthal reconstruction / tarsal strip is done later by ophthalmology with good cosmetic and functional results
  • Admit for serial exams: monitor for re-accumulation, recurrent pressure rise, associated globe/retinal injury
  • If OCS recurs or persists, reassess, complete any unreleased crus, and escalate to OR
  • Document: time of onset, time of decompression, pre/post IOP and visual acuity, RAPD status, which crura were released
Adjuncts do not replace decompression
Topical IOP drops, mannitol, acetazolamide, and steroids are adjuncts only — none replaces surgical decompression. The ophthalmology consult runs parallel to, never before, the procedure.
