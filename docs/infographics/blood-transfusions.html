Blood Transfusions: MTP Activation, Component Therapy, Reactions & Adjuncts

Adults and pediatric patients receiving blood component transfusion in the ED, ICU, or OR — massive transfusion protocol activation, component selection thresholds, transfusion reaction recognition/management, special-population transfusion (AIHA, Jehovah's Witness, pediatric), and adjunct therapy (TXA, calcium, PCC). Not a substitute for institutional blood bank protocols, complex crossmatch/antibody workup, or hematology consultation.

⚠ MTP activation: ABC score ≥2 (75% sensitivity, 86% specificity) OR Shock Index (HR/SBP) ≥1.0 OR ongoing hemorrhagic shock despite 2 L crystalloid — use 1:1:1 PRBC:FFP:platelet ratio, give O-negative PRBCs first, send type & screen but do NOT delay transfusion for it.
⚠ TXA must be given within 3 hours of injury (maximal benefit <1 hour: 32% mortality reduction) — 1 g IV bolus over 10 min, then 1 g IV over 8 hours (max 2 g total). Given beyond 3 hours, TXA may INCREASE death risk and is not recommended.
⚠ Give calcium every 4 units of PRBCs during massive transfusion — 1 g calcium gluconate (peripheral OK) OR 1 g calcium chloride (CENTRAL LINE ONLY). Target ionized calcium >1.1 mmol/L. Hypocalcemia impairs the coagulation cascade, platelet function, and cardiac contractility.
⚠ Calcium chloride is CENTRAL-LINE-ONLY — peripheral administration causes severe tissue necrosis. Use calcium gluconate if only peripheral access is available.
⚠ Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (usually ABO incompatibility) can occur with just a few milliliters of incompatible blood: STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY, verify patient/product identifiers, give aggressive crystalloid + sodium bicarbonate for renal protection, notify blood bank.
⚠ TACO vs TRALI both present with dyspnea and hypoxia during/after transfusion — an elevated BNP means TACO (diuretics work); a normal/low BNP means TRALI (diuretics do NOT help and may worsen shock). Treating the wrong one delays correct therapy.

MTP definition
[1][2] Massive Transfusion Protocol: ≥10 units PRBCs in 24h OR ≥4 units in 1 hour with ongoing bleeding. Activate on ABC score ≥2 (75% sensitivity, 86% specificity), Shock Index ≥1.0 (AUROC 0.83, Sens 67.7%, Spec 81.3%), or ongoing hemorrhagic shock despite 2 L crystalloid — don't wait for labs.

3 · Massive Transfusion Protocol

Activation. Massive Transfusion Protocol: ≥10 units PRBCs in 24h OR ≥4 units in 1 hour with ongoing bleeding. ABC score components (1 point each): HR ≥120 bpm, SBP ≤90 mmHg, penetrating torso injury, positive FAST — Score ≥2 predicts massive transfusion need with 75% sensitivity, 86% specificity. Shock Index (HR/SBP) SI ≥1.0 is a stronger predictor (AUROC 0.83, Sens 67.7%, Spec 81.3%). Also activate for ongoing hemorrhagic shock despite 2L crystalloid, persistent SBP <90 despite fluids, or visible ongoing hemorrhage + hemodynamic instability.[1][2][3]

1:1:1 ratio (standard MTP): 1 PRBC : 1 FFP : 1 platelet unit — approximates whole blood composition, evidence supports survival benefit. Activation steps: call blood bank ("Activate MTP"), give patient location + 2 identifiers, first cooler arrives ~10 min, send type & screen without delaying transfusion. Initial resuscitation: O-negative PRBCs (uncrossmatched), limit crystalloid, warm all products, give TXA within 3 hours, calcium every 4 units. Monitor labs q30-60 min: CBC, PT/INR, fibrinogen, iCa, lactate; TEG/ROTEM if available.[1][2][3]

TACO — diagnosis & treatment detail3+ criteria within 12h, treatment steps, prevention

Diagnosis (3+ within 12h of transfusion): dyspnea, orthopnea, cough, hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% RA), hypertension, tachycardia, peripheral edema, elevated BNP/NT-proBNP, abnormal echo. Treatment: STOP transfusion, sit upright, supplemental O2, Furosemide 20-40 mg IV, monitor urine output, vasodilator if hypertensive. Prevention: slow rate (<1 mL/kg/hr high-risk), consider concurrent diuretic in heart failure/renal disease.[6][7]

TRALI — diagnosis & treatment detailImmune mechanism, supportive care only, mortality

Immune (80-85%): donor plasma antibodies (anti-HLA/anti-HNA) activate recipient neutrophils → capillary leak → protein-rich alveolar flooding (ARDS-like). Diagnosis: during or within 6h of transfusion, sudden dyspnea, severe hypoxemia, fever, hypotension, bilateral infiltrates, NO JVD, normal BNP. Treatment: STOP transfusion, supportive care only, O2/ventilation if needed, DO NOT give diuretics. Usually resolves 48-96 hours. Mortality: 6-12%. Prevention: male-only plasma programs, leukoreduction.[6][7]

  1. STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY
  2. Maintain IV access (new line); verify patient ID vs. blood product
  3. Aggressive crystalloid — maintain urine output >200 mL/hr
  4. Sodium bicarbonate 250-500 mg IV (alkalinize urine, protect kidneys)
  5. Furosemide or mannitol for diuresis
  6. Treat DIC: FFP, cryo, platelets
  7. Notify blood bank, repeat type & screen

Mechanism: ABO incompatibility (most common cause) — host antibodies bind donor RBCs → intravascular hemolysis, can occur with just a few milliliters of blood. Classic triad (rarely all present): fever, flank/back pain, dark red/brown urine. Labs: DAT (Direct Antiglobulin Test, also called Direct Coombs) = POSITIVE, free hemoglobin, low haptoglobin, elevated LDH/indirect bilirubin. Prognosis: survivable with aggressive support; depends on degree of kidney injury.[8]

Febrile non-hemolytic reaction (FNHTR)Most common reaction — fever, DAT negative, antipyretic

Most common transfusion reaction. Mechanism: cytokine accumulation during storage (especially platelets) or recipient HLA alloantibodies against donor WBCs. Diagnosis (of exclusion): Fever: ≥38°C OR rise ≥1°C from baseline within 4h (moderate/severe: ≥39°C or rise ≥1.5°C with chills/rigors); DAT negative; hemolysis labs normal. Must rule out hemolytic reaction and TRALI first. Treatment: pause transfusion, assess, antipyretic (acetaminophen); if no other serious reaction, can resume. Only ~10% have recurrent episodes. Prevention: prestorage leukoreduction, fresher blood, premedication in high-risk patients.[6]

Severe allergic/anaphylaxis: Epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg IM. IgA deficiency (rare) is an important cause of severe/recurrent reactions — prevention is IgA-deficient or washed products.[6]

Metabolic complications
Hypocalcemia (citrate toxicity): risk factors = massive transfusion, liver dysfunction; iCa <1.1 mmol/L associated with mortality; impairs coagulation cascade, platelet function, cardiac contractility. Hyperkalemia: stored RBCs leak K+ (irradiation accelerates it); pediatric risk highest. Dextrose 0.5-1 g/kg + insulin 0.1 units/kg IV. Adult max: 25-50 g dextrose, 5-10 units insulin; monitor ECG (peaked T-waves, wide QRS). Hypothermia: cold products worsen coagulopathy — warm all products.[9][10]

6 · Special situations

Indications: acute hemorrhage requiring immediate transfusion, type unknown, pretransfusion testing too slow. Protocol: physician authorization, provide patient name/location + 2 identifiers, blood available in ~10 minutes; send type & screen without delaying transfusion. O negative — safest universal donor, use for all females of childbearing age and when type truly unknown. O positive acceptable for males >16 years and females >50 years (past childbearing) — risk of Rh immunization lower than death risk. Hemolysis risk ~0.1% overall with O neg blood, higher if previously transfused or pregnant. Transition to type-specific once type & screen results available.[11]

Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)Incompatible crossmatch, emergency vs elective transfusion

Challenge: patient's autoantibodies react with ALL normal RBCs — all crossmatches will be "incompatible," which may mask underlying alloantibodies. Most AIHA patients tolerate "incompatible" RBCs without significant increase in hemolysis. Emergency transfusion (life-threatening anemia): use O-negative if type unknown — avoiding hemodynamic collapse outweighs hemolysis risk; transfuse if symptomatic despite Hgb level. Elective transfusion: select "least incompatible" unit, extended phenotyping. Transfusion triggers: symptomatic anemia, restrictive threshold Hgb <7 g/dL (asymptomatic). First-line medical treatment: corticosteroids (70-85% effective), rituximab (80-90% effective), splenectomy (2/3 effective for refractory).[12]

7 · Adjuncts

Tranexamic Acid — antifibrinolytic. Lysine analog blocks plasminogen binding, inhibits fibrinolysis, reduces blood loss by ~1/3. Timing is critical: <1 hour from injury: 32% mortality reduction; 1-3 hours: 21% mortality reduction; >3 hours: May INCREASE death risk (NOT recommended). Safety: 30,000+ patients show no increased VTE or adverse events.[13]

Tranexamic Acid (TXA)
1 g IV bolus in 100 mL NS over 10 min, then 1 g IV over 8 hours (max 2 g total)
IV
Slow infusion prevents hypotension. Do not delay MTP activation to give TXA — give concurrently.

Citrate anticoagulant chelates ionized calcium; iCa <1.1 mmol/L associated with mortality. Calcium required for coagulation cascade (factors II, VII, IX, X), platelet activation, and cardiac contractility. Give 1 g calcium every 4 units PRBCs. Monitor ionized calcium every 30-60 min or after each cooler — target iCa >1.1 mmol/L. Liver dysfunction: citrate metabolized by liver → higher accumulation, monitor more frequently.[9]

4-factor PCC (Kcentra): Factors II, VII, IX, X (preferred); 3-factor PCC has Factors II, IX, X (± VII). FDA indication: warfarin reversal with acute bleeding or urgent procedure. Off-label: DOAC reversal, factor deficiency bleeding, trauma coagulopathy adjunct. Always co-administer Vitamin K 10 mg IV (PCC effect lasts ~6h; vitamin K provides durable reversal). Advantages over FFP: faster, lower volume, more complete reversal. Cautions: contains heparin (avoid in HIT), thromboembolism risk with high doses.[14]

Standard dose 10-15 mL/kg (3-4 units adult), achieves ~30% factor replacement. Warfarin reversal: 5-8 mL/kg (10% factor replacement acceptable). Takes 20-30 min to thaw — order early. NOT indicated for volume expansion, heparin reversal (use protamine), or DOAC reversal (use specific antidotes or PCC).[4][5]

5 · Complications

Dimension | TACO | TRALI

Mechanism | Volume overload — heart can't handle the extra fluid | Donor antibodies activate recipient neutrophils → capillary leak

Timing | During transfusion or within 12 hours | During transfusion or within 6 hours

Key findings | JVD present, hypertensive, elevated BNP | NO JVD, often hypotensive, normal BNP

BNP | BNP elevated → TACO (heart is stretched) | BNP normal/low → TRALI (heart is fine, lungs are injured)

Treatment | Diuretics WORK — Furosemide 20-40 mg IV | Supportive only — diuretics DO NOT help

TACO — diagnosis & treatment detail

Diagnosis (3+ within 12h of transfusion): dyspnea, orthopnea, cough, hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% RA), hypertension, tachycardia, peripheral edema, elevated BNP/NT-proBNP, abnormal echo. Treatment: STOP transfusion, sit upright, supplemental O2, Furosemide 20-40 mg IV, monitor urine output, vasodilator if hypertensive. Prevention: slow rate (<1 mL/kg/hr high-risk), consider concurrent diuretic in heart failure/renal disease.[6][7]

Furosemide: 20-40 mg IV IV — For TACO — diuresis works because the mechanism is volume overload, unlike TRALI.

TRALI — diagnosis & treatment detail

Immune (80-85%): donor plasma antibodies (anti-HLA/anti-HNA) activate recipient neutrophils → capillary leak → protein-rich alveolar flooding (ARDS-like). Diagnosis: during or within 6h of transfusion, sudden dyspnea, severe hypoxemia, fever, hypotension, bilateral infiltrates, NO JVD, normal BNP. Treatment: STOP transfusion, supportive care only, O2/ventilation if needed, DO NOT give diuretics. Usually resolves 48-96 hours. Mortality: 6-12%. Prevention: male-only plasma programs, leukoreduction.[6][7]

  • STOP TRANSFUSION IMMEDIATELY
  • Maintain IV access (new line); verify patient ID vs. blood product
  • Aggressive crystalloid — maintain urine output >200 mL/hr
  • Sodium bicarbonate 250-500 mg IV (alkalinize urine, protect kidneys)
  • Furosemide or mannitol for diuresis
  • Treat DIC: FFP, cryo, platelets
  • Notify blood bank, repeat type & screen

Mechanism: ABO incompatibility (most common cause) — host antibodies bind donor RBCs → intravascular hemolysis, can occur with just a few milliliters of blood. Classic triad (rarely all present): fever, flank/back pain, dark red/brown urine. Labs: DAT (Direct Antiglobulin Test, also called Direct Coombs) = POSITIVE, free hemoglobin, low haptoglobin, elevated LDH/indirect bilirubin. Prognosis: survivable with aggressive support; depends on degree of kidney injury.[8]

Febrile non-hemolytic reaction (FNHTR)

Most common transfusion reaction. Mechanism: cytokine accumulation during storage (especially platelets) or recipient HLA alloantibodies against donor WBCs. Diagnosis (of exclusion): Fever: ≥38°C OR rise ≥1°C from baseline within 4h (moderate/severe: ≥39°C or rise ≥1.5°C with chills/rigors); DAT negative; hemolysis labs normal. Must rule out hemolytic reaction and TRALI first. Treatment: pause transfusion, assess, antipyretic (acetaminophen); if no other serious reaction, can resume. Only ~10% have recurrent episodes. Prevention: prestorage leukoreduction, fresher blood, premedication in high-risk patients.[6]

Severity | Findings | Treatment

Mild | Itching, urticaria, localized swelling | Pause transfusion, diphenhydramine 25-50 mg IV, resume if resolved

Moderate | Pruritus, urticaria, flushing, non-airway angioedema | Antihistamine, consider corticosteroid, may resume if resolved

Severe / anaphylaxis | Airway edema, bronchospasm, hypotension, cardiovascular collapse | STOP transfusion immediately, Epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg IM, airway management, IV antihistamine + corticosteroid

Severe allergic/anaphylaxis: Epinephrine 0.3-0.5 mg IM. IgA deficiency (rare) is an important cause of severe/recurrent reactions — prevention is IgA-deficient or washed products.[6]

Hypocalcemia (citrate toxicity): risk factors = massive transfusion, liver dysfunction; iCa <1.1 mmol/L associated with mortality; impairs coagulation cascade, platelet function, cardiac contractility. Hyperkalemia: stored RBCs leak K+ (irradiation accelerates it); pediatric risk highest. Dextrose 0.5-1 g/kg + insulin 0.1 units/kg IV. Adult max: 25-50 g dextrose, 5-10 units insulin; monitor ECG (peaked T-waves, wide QRS). Hypothermia: cold products worsen coagulopathy — warm all products.[9][10]

6 · Special situations

Indications: acute hemorrhage requiring immediate transfusion, type unknown, pretransfusion testing too slow. Protocol: physician authorization, provide patient name/location + 2 identifiers, blood available in ~10 minutes; send type & screen without delaying transfusion. O negative — safest universal donor, use for all females of childbearing age and when type truly unknown. O positive acceptable for males >16 years and females >50 years (past childbearing) — risk of Rh immunization lower than death risk. Hemolysis risk ~0.1% overall with O neg blood, higher if previously transfused or pregnant. Transition to type-specific once type & screen results available.[11]

Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)

Challenge: patient's autoantibodies react with ALL normal RBCs — all crossmatches will be "incompatible," which may mask underlying alloantibodies. Most AIHA patients tolerate "incompatible" RBCs without significant increase in hemolysis. Emergency transfusion (life-threatening anemia): use O-negative if type unknown — avoiding hemodynamic collapse outweighs hemolysis risk; transfuse if symptomatic despite Hgb level. Elective transfusion: select "least incompatible" unit, extended phenotyping. Transfusion triggers: symptomatic anemia, restrictive threshold Hgb <7 g/dL (asymptomatic). First-line medical treatment: corticosteroids (70-85% effective), rituximab (80-90% effective), splenectomy (2/3 effective for refractory).[12]

7 · Adjuncts

Tranexamic Acid — antifibrinolytic. Lysine analog blocks plasminogen binding, inhibits fibrinolysis, reduces blood loss by ~1/3. Timing is critical: <1 hour from injury: 32% mortality reduction; 1-3 hours: 21% mortality reduction; >3 hours: May INCREASE death risk (NOT recommended). Safety: 30,000+ patients show no increased VTE or adverse events.[13]

Tranexamic Acid (TXA): 1 g IV bolus in 100 mL NS over 10 min, then 1 g IV over 8 hours (max 2 g total) IV — Slow infusion prevents hypotension. Do not delay MTP activation to give TXA — give concurrently.

Citrate anticoagulant chelates ionized calcium; iCa <1.1 mmol/L associated with mortality. Calcium required for coagulation cascade (factors II, VII, IX, X), platelet activation, and cardiac contractility. Give 1 g calcium every 4 units PRBCs. Monitor ionized calcium every 30-60 min or after each cooler — target iCa >1.1 mmol/L. Liver dysfunction: citrate metabolized by liver → higher accumulation, monitor more frequently.[9]

Calcium Gluconate: 1 g IV, repeat every 4 units PRBCs IV (peripheral OK) — Give with the first unit of blood. Enter units transfused above for the number of 1 g doses needed.

Calcium Chloride: 1 g IV, repeat every 4 units PRBCs IV (CENTRAL LINE ONLY) — More effective per gram (~270 mg elemental Ca) but causes tissue necrosis if given peripherally. Use ONLY with confirmed central access.

4-factor PCC (Kcentra): Factors II, VII, IX, X (preferred); 3-factor PCC has Factors II, IX, X (± VII). FDA indication: warfarin reversal with acute bleeding or urgent procedure. Off-label: DOAC reversal, factor deficiency bleeding, trauma coagulopathy adjunct. Always co-administer Vitamin K 10 mg IV (PCC effect lasts ~6h; vitamin K provides durable reversal). Advantages over FFP: faster, lower volume, more complete reversal. Cautions: contains heparin (avoid in HIT), thromboembolism risk with high doses.[14]
