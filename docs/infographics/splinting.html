Splint Recommendations (Adults)

Adults with extremity fractures, dislocations, or soft-tissue injuries requiring emergency department splinting (source title: 'Splint Recommendations (Adults)'). Assumes any angulated fracture has already been reduced before splinting — this reference covers splint selection and application technique, not reduction technique. The supracondylar guidance is explicitly for the adult fracture pattern, not pediatric supracondylar fractures.

⚠ Pre- AND post-splint neurovascular exam required — document both (sensation, motor, vascular, color/temperature).
⚠ Reduce angulated fractures BEFORE splinting.
⚠ Mold plaster with flat palms (thenar eminences) — NEVER fingertips — fingertip pressure creates points that can cause skin necrosis.
⚠ This is a splint, not a circumferential cast — do not wrap plaster all the way around in the acute setting; two slabs allow for swelling.
⚠ Gutter splints: MCP joints MUST be flexed 70–90° — immobilizing in extension lets the collateral ligaments shorten, causing permanent MCP stiffness.
⚠ 'Fight bite' rule: ANY laceration over the MCP joint from a punch is an open joint until proven otherwise → irrigate, antibiotics, hand surgery consult.

Overview & Critical Actions

Hub-and-spoke reference for adult extremity splinting: pick a body region below, then the specific fracture or injury, to reach the application technique. General splinting principles and the full quick-reference table are always available below regardless of region.

  1. Pre-splint neurovascular exam and documentation
  2. Reduce angulated fractures before splinting
  3. Apply stockinette, then padding (avoid wrinkles)
  4. Mold splint with flat palms, not fingertips
  5. Post-splint neurovascular check and documentation
  6. Position of function: wrist extended, MCP flexed

Humerus

FRACTURE → SPLINT GUIDE
• Proximal humerus → Sling / Cuff & Collar
• Humeral shaft → Coaptation Splint (→ functional brace at 1–2 weeks)
• Supracondylar (adult) → Long Arm Posterior or Double Sugar Tong

  • Proximal humerus fracture (non-displaced or minimally displaced)
  • Greater tuberosity fracture
  • Shoulder dislocation post-reduction

MATERIALS: Standard triangular arm sling OR cuff and collar; swathe (elastic bandage around torso) for added stability if needed.

APPLICATION: Arm rests in sling with elbow at 90° flexion, forearm supported across the body, wrist neutral (not dangling). If a swathe is used, wrap elastic bandage around upper arm and torso to prevent arm abduction — pad the axilla to prevent skin breakdown.

POSITION: Elbow at 90°, arm adducted against the body. Allow gentle pendulum (Codman) exercises after 1–2 days to prevent shoulder stiffness.

PROFHER trial
PROFHER trial (2015): sling alone is equivalent to surgery for most displaced proximal humerus fractures in adults. Avoid prolonged full immobilization (>2 weeks) — frozen shoulder risk. No imaging needed for isolated greater tuberosity fractures with <5mm displacement.
  • Humeral shaft fracture (midshaft diaphyseal)
  • Initial stabilization before functional bracing

MATERIALS: 6-inch plaster or fiberglass (10–12 layers), stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage, arm sling.

APPLICATION: U-shape from the axilla, down the medial arm, around the elbow, back up the lateral arm to the deltoid insertion. Extra padding in the axilla and over the olecranon. Mold snugly around the medial arm, elbow, and lateral arm; secure with elastic bandage wrapped distally to proximally; place the arm in a sling with the elbow at 90°.

POSITION: Elbow at 90° flexion, arm adducted against the torso, forearm neutral rotation.

Transition to functional brace at 1–2 weeks
Sarmiento (2000): 98% union rate with functional bracing, mean angulation <8°. FISH trial (2020): functional bracing non-inferior to surgery for closed humeral shaft fractures. Do NOT wrap plaster circumferentially — this is a splint, not a cast; a common mistake is splinting too short. Check radial nerve function before and after application — wrist drop = radial nerve palsy.

Elbow / Proximal Forearm (reached from Humerus or Forearm)

  • Supracondylar fracture (adult)
  • Olecranon fracture (non-operative)
  • Coronoid fracture
  • Proximal radius/ulna fracture
  • Elbow dislocation post-reduction

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (10–12 layers), stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Posterior slab from the proximal palmar crease to mid-upper arm; extra padding over the olecranon and ulnar styloid; mold at the elbow to maintain 90° flexion; ensure fingers and thumb have full ROM; must NOT extend past the proximal palmar crease.

POSITION: Elbow at 90° flexion, forearm neutral rotation (thumb up), wrist in slight extension (15–20°).

Coverage and neurovascular check
Splint should cover >50% of the arm circumference for stability. For supracondylar fractures: confirm the anterior humeral line bisects the capitellum on post-splinting lateral x-ray. Check median, radial, and ulnar nerve function plus radial pulse and capillary refill.
  • Supracondylar fracture with concurrent distal forearm fracture
  • Highly unstable distal radius/ulna fractures needing rotational + AP control
  • Complex elbow fracture-dislocations (temporary stabilization)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster (10 layers) — two separate slabs; stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage per slab.

APPLICATION: First slab — forearm sugar tong from dorsal MCP joints, around the elbow, to the palmar crease (standard sugar tong). Second slab — arm sugar tong from lateral upper arm, around the elbow, to medial upper arm for added medial-lateral stability. Apply stockinette and Webril before EACH slab; mold the wrist in slight flexion + ulnar deviation if a distal fracture is present.

POSITION: Elbow at 90° flexion, forearm neutral rotation, wrist slight flexion + ulnar deviation if distal fracture present.

Maximum stability — monitor for compartment syndrome
Provides the most stability of any upper-extremity splint (AP, medial-lateral, and rotational control) but is bulky and uncomfortable. Two slabs allow for swelling. Monitor for compartment syndrome — especially with combined fractures. Mark the elastic bandage layers so the patient can loosen it if swelling increases.

Forearm

FRACTURE → SPLINT GUIDE
• Olecranon / coronoid → Long Arm Posterior
• Radius/ulna proximal or midshaft → Long Arm Posterior, or Double Sugar Tong (unstable/complex)
• Radius distal, isolated → Thumb Spica or Volar
• Radius/ulna distal, complex → Sugar Tong (A+P preferred at our institution)
• Ulna styloid → Sugar Tong (A+P preferred)

INSTITUTION PREFERENCE: A+P (anterior + posterior) splints are preferred over sugar tong for forearm and distal radius/ulna fractures — two separate plaster slabs with no wrapping around the elbow give equivalent stabilization with less bulk and better patient comfort.

  • Distal radius and/or ulna fracture
  • Colles fracture (dorsally displaced distal radius)
  • Smith fracture (volar displaced distal radius)
  • Barton fracture

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster (10 layers), stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

SUGAR TONG APPLICATION: Slab from dorsal MCP joints, around the flexed elbow (90°), to the palmar crease; extra padding over the olecranon and both styloids; mold the wrist in slight flexion (10°) + ulnar deviation for Colles, neutral for Smith.

A+P ALTERNATIVE (PREFERRED): Instead of wrapping around the elbow — a posterior slab from the dorsal MCP joints to the proximal forearm, plus an anterior (volar) slab from the palmar crease to the proximal forearm, each secured with elastic bandage. Provides equivalent AP stability without elbow restriction, better comfort, easier to adjust.

POSITION: Wrist 10° flexion + ulnar deviation (dorsal displacement); forearm neutral rotation; elbow 90° flexion (sugar tong only).

A+P vs sugar tong
A+P eliminates the need to wrap around the elbow while maintaining AP stability. Sugar tong prevents pronation/supination — use when rotational control is critical. For Colles, mold the volar slab into slight flexion and ulnar deviation with 3-point pressure. Check capillary refill, sensation, and finger ROM after application.

Wrist & Hand

CARPAL BONES: Scaphoid, trapezium, lunate fracture → Thumb Spica. Lunate dislocation → Sugar Tong (A+P preferred). Triquetrum, pisiform, trapezoid, capitate, hamate → Volar Splint.

SOFT TISSUE: UCL injury (gamekeeper/skier thumb), thumb MCP dislocation, De Quervain tenosynovitis → Thumb Spica.

METACARPALS: 1st metacarpal (Bennett, Rolando) → Thumb Spica. 2nd/3rd metacarpal → Radial Gutter or Volar. 4th/5th metacarpal (boxer fracture) → Ulnar Gutter.

PHALANGES: 1st phalanx (thumb) → Thumb Spica. 2nd/3rd proximal or middle → Radial Gutter or buddy taping. 4th/5th proximal or middle → Ulnar Gutter or buddy taping. Distal phalanx → aluminum U-shaped (stack) splint.

BUDDY TAPING: Tape the injured finger to an adjacent finger with padding between; allows protected ROM; appropriate for stable, non-displaced proximal/middle phalanx fractures.

ALUMINUM FINGER SPLINT: Moldable metal splint for distal phalanx fractures and mallet finger — keep the DIP in extension (stack splint), leave the PIP free.

  • 4th metacarpal fracture
  • 5th metacarpal fracture (boxer fracture)
  • 4th / 5th proximal phalanx fracture
  • 4th / 5th middle phalanx fracture (non-displaced)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage, cotton finger-separator padding between 3rd and 4th fingers.

APPLICATION: Slab along the ulnar side of the forearm and hand, from mid-forearm to the fingertips of the 4th and 5th digits; extra padding over the ulnar styloid and 5th MCP; MCP joints must be flexed to 70–90°, IP joints in slight flexion (10–20°).

POSITION: Wrist 20–30° extension; MCP joints at 70–90° flexion (the most important positioning element); IP joints slight flexion.

MCP flexion 70–90° is critical
The collateral ligaments of the MCP joints are taut in flexion and lax in extension. Immobilizing in extension lets the ligaments shorten → permanent MCP stiffness ("intrinsic minus" hand). Boxer fractures (5th MC neck) with <40° angulation and no rotational deformity: ulnar gutter with ortho follow-up.
Fight bite rule
Always check for rotational malalignment — flexed fingers should all point toward the scaphoid tubercle. ANY laceration over the MCP from a punch is an open joint until proven otherwise → irrigate, antibiotics, hand surgery consult. Leave the 3rd finger free when possible to improve function.
  • 2nd metacarpal fracture
  • 3rd metacarpal fracture
  • 2nd / 3rd proximal phalanx fracture
  • 2nd / 3rd middle phalanx fracture (non-displaced)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage, cotton finger-separator padding between 3rd and 4th fingers.

APPLICATION: Mirror image of the ulnar gutter, applied to the radial side — slab from mid-forearm to the fingertips of the 2nd and 3rd digits; extra padding over the radial styloid and 2nd MCP; MCP joints at 70–90° flexion, IP joints slight flexion (10–20°).

POSITION: Wrist 20–30° extension; MCP joints at 70–90° flexion; IP joints slight flexion.

Less angulation tolerance than 4th/5th
Same principles as ulnar gutter — MCP flexion is critical to prevent collateral ligament contracture. Always check for rotational malalignment before splinting. 2nd and 3rd metacarpal fractures tolerate LESS angulation than 4th/5th (more rigid carpometacarpal joints) — if angulation >10°, refer to ortho (may need operative fixation). A volar splint can substitute for non-displaced 2nd/3rd MC fractures if a gutter splint is unavailable.

Thumb Spica & Volar (reached from Forearm or Wrist & Hand)

  • Scaphoid fracture (confirmed or suspected — snuffbox tenderness)
  • Trapezium fracture
  • Lunate fracture
  • 1st metacarpal fracture (Bennett, Rolando)
  • Thumb MCP dislocation post-reduction
  • UCL injury (gamekeeper/skier thumb)
  • De Quervain tenosynovitis
  • Isolated distal radius fracture (alternative to volar)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette with a thumb hole cut, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Slab along the radial side of the forearm, wrapping around the thumb to include the 1st metacarpal and thumb to the IP joint (mid-forearm to just past the thumb IP joint); extra padding over the radial styloid and 1st MCP; mold the thumb in a "C" position (abducted, slightly opposed, as if holding a can); ensure the 2nd–5th fingers have full ROM.

POSITION: Wrist 15–20° extension; thumb abducted, slightly opposed (functional position); thumb IP joint included (scaphoid) or free (soft tissue only).

Suspected scaphoid, negative x-ray
For suspected scaphoid fracture with negative initial x-rays: immobilize in thumb spica for 10–14 days, then repeat imaging (MRI or repeat x-ray). Anatomical snuffbox tenderness has high sensitivity (~90%) but low specificity — err on the side of splinting. Thumb spica immobilizes the thumb; volar does not. For UCL injuries, include the thumb MCP but leave the IP free to prevent stiffness.
  • Non-displaced or minimally displaced distal radius fracture
  • Triquetrum fracture (dorsal chip)
  • Pisiform, trapezoid, capitate, hamate fracture
  • 2nd / 3rd metacarpal fracture (alternative to radial gutter)
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (night splint)
  • Wrist sprain
  • Tendon injury (initial stabilization)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Slab along the volar surface of the forearm, from the proximal palmar crease to mid-forearm; extra padding over the ulnar styloid; mold with gentle pressure; ensure thumb and all fingers have full ROM.

POSITION: Wrist in 15–20° extension (position of function); fingers free — must be able to make a full fist; thumb completely free.

Simplest ED splint
Does NOT control pronation/supination — if rotational control is needed, use sugar tong or A+P. For adult buckle (torus) fractures: volar splint with ortho follow-up in 1 week. Must not extend past the proximal palmar crease — blocking MCP flexion causes finger stiffness. Can be converted to a short arm cast at follow-up.

Lower Extremity

FRACTURE → SPLINT GUIDE
• Distal tibia or distal fibula → Posterior Short Leg (± stirrup for added stability)
• Ankle sprain grade 2/3 → Stirrup Splint
• Talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiform → Posterior Short Leg
• Metatarsal (2nd–5th) → Posterior Short Leg or hard-soled shoe
• Lisfranc → Posterior Short Leg (non-weight bearing, ortho follow-up)

5th METATARSAL SPECIAL CASES: Zone 1 (tuberosity/pseudo-Jones) → hard-soled shoe, weight bear as tolerated. Zone 2 (Jones fracture, metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction) → Posterior Short Leg, non-weight bearing, ortho follow-up (high nonunion risk). Zone 3 (shaft stress fracture) → Posterior Short Leg, non-weight bearing.

  • Distal tibia fracture (non-operative)
  • Distal fibula (lateral malleolus) fracture
  • Bimalleolar / trimalleolar ankle fracture (initial stabilization)
  • Talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiform fracture
  • Metatarsal fracture (non-displaced)
  • Jones fracture (5th MT zone 2)
  • Lisfranc injury
  • Achilles tendon rupture (initial, with ankle in plantarflexion)

MATERIALS: 6-inch plaster (12–15 layers for adults), stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Patient prone with knee flexed 90° or seated with foot hanging off the stretcher; extra padding over the malleoli, calcaneus, and Achilles tendon; posterior slab from toes to the tibial tuberosity, folded at the toes for a smooth foot plate; mold the ankle at 90° dorsiflexion (neutral); secure with elastic bandage from toes proximally.

ADDING A STIRRUP (bimalleolar/trimalleolar): U-shaped slab from medial mid-calf, under the heel, to lateral mid-calf, applied over the posterior slab before the elastic bandage.

POSITION: Ankle at 90° (neutral dorsiflexion) — prevents equinus contracture; subtalar joint neutral. Exception: Achilles rupture — 20° plantarflexion.

Workhorse lower-extremity splint
Must extend to the toes to prevent metatarsal/forefoot fractures from shifting. Extra Webril over the Achilles prevents pressure necrosis. Plaster should be wider than the posterior leg, wrapping the edges around the sides for better contouring. Always assess dorsalis pedis pulse, posterior tibial pulse, and sensation in all foot compartments.
  • Ankle sprain grade 2 (moderate — partial ligament tear)
  • Ankle sprain grade 3 (severe — complete ligament tear)
  • Isolated lateral malleolus fracture (stable, non-displaced)
  • Supplemental stability for posterior short leg splint

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: U-shaped slab from medial mid-calf, under the heel, to lateral mid-calf; extra padding over both malleoli; mold at 90° dorsiflexion; toes remain visible and mobile.

POSITION: Ankle at 90° (neutral dorsiflexion); subtalar joint neutral — not inverted or everted.

STIRRUP ALONE vs. WITH POSTERIOR SLAB: Stirrup alone — grade 2/3 ankle sprains, allows some plantarflexion/dorsiflexion for early mobilization. Stirrup + posterior slab — fractures, maximum stability.

Early protected motion for sprains
For ankle sprains, stirrup alone allows early protected motion, which improves healing compared to rigid immobilization. The U-shape prevents inversion/eversion — the mechanism of most ankle sprains. Air splint (Aircast) can substitute for stable grade 2 sprains and is easier for the patient to remove for icing/exercises. Ottawa Ankle Rules: no splint needed if negative — patients can weight-bear with support. Always assess anterior drawer, squeeze test (syndesmosis), and base of 5th metatarsal for an associated fracture.

General Splinting Principles & Quick Reference

MATERIALS
Plaster — moldable, sets in 3–5 min, exothermic reaction; better for fractures requiring 3-point molding; standard 8–12 layers depending on site.
Fiberglass — lighter, stronger, water-resistant, sets faster; better for stable fractures where molding is not critical; more expensive.
Stockinette — cotton tube applied directly to skin, protects from plaster irritation.
Webril (cast padding) — soft cotton roll, standard 2–4 layers.
Elastic bandage (ACE wrap) — secures the splint, wrapped distally to proximally, snug but not tight.

  • 2–4 layers Webril over the entire splinted area
  • Extra padding over bony prominences (malleoli, olecranon, styloids, calcaneus)
  • Minimal padding directly over the fracture site when molding is needed — excessive padding defeats the mold
  • Cotton between fingers for gutter splints to prevent maceration
  1. Dip plaster in lukewarm water (not hot — the exothermic reaction adds heat and can burn)
  2. Squeeze out excess water gently — do not wring
  3. Smooth layers together on a flat surface to remove air pockets
  4. Apply to the limb and mold with flat palms (thenar eminences) — never fingertips
  5. Hold the mold until the plaster sets (3–5 minutes) — do not move hands during setting
  6. Secure with elastic bandage — snug, not constricting

POSITION OF FUNCTION (unless otherwise specified): Wrist 15–20° extension; MCP joints 70–90° flexion (gutter splints — prevents collateral ligament contracture); IP joints slight flexion (10–20°); elbow 90° flexion; ankle 90° dorsiflexion (neutral — prevents equinus contracture); thumb abducted, slightly opposed ("holding a can").

  • Sensation: light touch in each nerve distribution (median, ulnar, radial for upper; deep peroneal, superficial peroneal, tibial, sural for lower)
  • Motor: thumb opposition (median), finger abduction (ulnar), wrist/finger extension (radial), toe dorsiflexion (deep peroneal), toe plantarflexion (tibial)
  • Vascular: capillary refill <2 seconds, pulses palpable (radial/ulnar for upper; dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial for lower)
  • Color and temperature: compare to the uninjured side
  • Document before AND after splinting
A+P institution preference
Our institution prefers A+P (anterior + posterior) splints over sugar tong for forearm fractures — two separate plaster slabs (one dorsal, one volar) rather than one continuous slab wrapping around the elbow. Equivalent AP stability without restricting elbow motion; less bulky, better patient comfort. Use sugar tong only when rotational control (pronation/supination) is specifically required.
Discharge instructions — compartment syndrome return precautions
Elevate above the level of the heart; ice 20 minutes on/off (over padding, not directly on skin); wiggle fingers/toes frequently to prevent stiffness and monitor for compartment syndrome; loosen the elastic bandage if swelling increases (mark the outer edge). Return immediately for: increasing pain not relieved by elevation and medication; numbness, tingling, or inability to move fingers/toes; fingers/toes turning blue, white, or cold; a splint that feels too tight or causes pressure pain — these are compartment syndrome warning signs, a surgical emergency.

Apply stockinette and Webril before EACH slab; mold the wrist in slight flexion + ulnar deviation if a distal fracture is present.

POSITION: Elbow at 90° flexion, forearm neutral rotation, wrist slight flexion + ulnar deviation if distal fracture present.

Provides the most stability of any upper-extremity splint (AP, medial-lateral, and rotational control) but is bulky and uncomfortable. Two slabs allow for swelling. Monitor for compartment syndrome — especially with combined fractures. Mark the elastic bandage layers so the patient can loosen it if swelling increases.

Forearm

FRACTURE → SPLINT GUIDE
• Olecranon / coronoid → Long Arm Posterior
• Radius/ulna proximal or midshaft → Long Arm Posterior, or Double Sugar Tong (unstable/complex)
• Radius distal, isolated → Thumb Spica or Volar
• Radius/ulna distal, complex → Sugar Tong (A+P preferred at our institution)
• Ulna styloid → Sugar Tong (A+P preferred)

INSTITUTION PREFERENCE: A+P (anterior + posterior) splints are preferred over sugar tong for forearm and distal radius/ulna fractures — two separate plaster slabs with no wrapping around the elbow give equivalent stabilization with less bulk and better patient comfort.

  • Distal radius and/or ulna fracture
  • Colles fracture (dorsally displaced distal radius)
  • Smith fracture (volar displaced distal radius)
  • Barton fracture

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster (10 layers), stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

SUGAR TONG APPLICATION: Slab from dorsal MCP joints, around the flexed elbow (90°), to the palmar crease; extra padding over the olecranon and both styloids; mold the wrist in slight flexion (10°) + ulnar deviation for Colles, neutral for Smith.

A+P ALTERNATIVE (PREFERRED): Instead of wrapping around the elbow — a posterior slab from the dorsal MCP joints to the proximal forearm, plus an anterior (volar) slab from the palmar crease to the proximal forearm, each secured with elastic bandage. Provides equivalent AP stability without elbow restriction, better comfort, easier to adjust.

POSITION: Wrist 10° flexion + ulnar deviation (dorsal displacement); forearm neutral rotation; elbow 90° flexion (sugar tong only).

A+P eliminates the need to wrap around the elbow while maintaining AP stability. Sugar tong prevents pronation/supination — use when rotational control is critical. For Colles, mold the volar slab into slight flexion and ulnar deviation with 3-point pressure. Check capillary refill, sensation, and finger ROM after application.

Wrist & Hand

CARPAL BONES: Scaphoid, trapezium, lunate fracture → Thumb Spica. Lunate dislocation → Sugar Tong (A+P preferred). Triquetrum, pisiform, trapezoid, capitate, hamate → Volar Splint.

SOFT TISSUE: UCL injury (gamekeeper/skier thumb), thumb MCP dislocation, De Quervain tenosynovitis → Thumb Spica.

METACARPALS: 1st metacarpal (Bennett, Rolando) → Thumb Spica. 2nd/3rd metacarpal → Radial Gutter or Volar. 4th/5th metacarpal (boxer fracture) → Ulnar Gutter.

PHALANGES: 1st phalanx (thumb) → Thumb Spica. 2nd/3rd proximal or middle → Radial Gutter or buddy taping. 4th/5th proximal or middle → Ulnar Gutter or buddy taping. Distal phalanx → aluminum U-shaped (stack) splint.

BUDDY TAPING: Tape the injured finger to an adjacent finger with padding between; allows protected ROM; appropriate for stable, non-displaced proximal/middle phalanx fractures.

ALUMINUM FINGER SPLINT: Moldable metal splint for distal phalanx fractures and mallet finger — keep the DIP in extension (stack splint), leave the PIP free.

  • 4th metacarpal fracture
  • 5th metacarpal fracture (boxer fracture)
  • 4th / 5th proximal phalanx fracture
  • 4th / 5th middle phalanx fracture (non-displaced)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage, cotton finger-separator padding between 3rd and 4th fingers.

APPLICATION: Slab along the ulnar side of the forearm and hand, from mid-forearm to the fingertips of the 4th and 5th digits; extra padding over the ulnar styloid and 5th MCP; MCP joints must be flexed to 70–90°, IP joints in slight flexion (10–20°).

POSITION: Wrist 20–30° extension; MCP joints at 70–90° flexion (the most important positioning element); IP joints slight flexion.

The collateral ligaments of the MCP joints are taut in flexion and lax in extension. Immobilizing in extension lets the ligaments shorten → permanent MCP stiffness ("intrinsic minus" hand). Boxer fractures (5th MC neck) with <40° angulation and no rotational deformity: ulnar gutter with ortho follow-up.

Always check for rotational malalignment — flexed fingers should all point toward the scaphoid tubercle. ANY laceration over the MCP from a punch is an open joint until proven otherwise → irrigate, antibiotics, hand surgery consult. Leave the 3rd finger free when possible to improve function.

  • 2nd metacarpal fracture
  • 3rd metacarpal fracture
  • 2nd / 3rd proximal phalanx fracture
  • 2nd / 3rd middle phalanx fracture (non-displaced)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage, cotton finger-separator padding between 3rd and 4th fingers.

APPLICATION: Mirror image of the ulnar gutter, applied to the radial side — slab from mid-forearm to the fingertips of the 2nd and 3rd digits; extra padding over the radial styloid and 2nd MCP; MCP joints at 70–90° flexion, IP joints slight flexion (10–20°).

POSITION: Wrist 20–30° extension; MCP joints at 70–90° flexion; IP joints slight flexion.

Same principles as ulnar gutter — MCP flexion is critical to prevent collateral ligament contracture. Always check for rotational malalignment before splinting. 2nd and 3rd metacarpal fractures tolerate LESS angulation than 4th/5th (more rigid carpometacarpal joints) — if angulation >10°, refer to ortho (may need operative fixation). A volar splint can substitute for non-displaced 2nd/3rd MC fractures if a gutter splint is unavailable.

Thumb Spica & Volar (reached from Forearm or Wrist & Hand)

  • Scaphoid fracture (confirmed or suspected — snuffbox tenderness)
  • Trapezium fracture
  • Lunate fracture
  • 1st metacarpal fracture (Bennett, Rolando)
  • Thumb MCP dislocation post-reduction
  • UCL injury (gamekeeper/skier thumb)
  • De Quervain tenosynovitis
  • Isolated distal radius fracture (alternative to volar)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette with a thumb hole cut, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Slab along the radial side of the forearm, wrapping around the thumb to include the 1st metacarpal and thumb to the IP joint (mid-forearm to just past the thumb IP joint); extra padding over the radial styloid and 1st MCP; mold the thumb in a "C" position (abducted, slightly opposed, as if holding a can); ensure the 2nd–5th fingers have full ROM.

POSITION: Wrist 15–20° extension; thumb abducted, slightly opposed (functional position); thumb IP joint included (scaphoid) or free (soft tissue only).

For suspected scaphoid fracture with negative initial x-rays: immobilize in thumb spica for 10–14 days, then repeat imaging (MRI or repeat x-ray). Anatomical snuffbox tenderness has high sensitivity (~90%) but low specificity — err on the side of splinting. Thumb spica immobilizes the thumb; volar does not. For UCL injuries, include the thumb MCP but leave the IP free to prevent stiffness.

  • Non-displaced or minimally displaced distal radius fracture
  • Triquetrum fracture (dorsal chip)
  • Pisiform, trapezoid, capitate, hamate fracture
  • 2nd / 3rd metacarpal fracture (alternative to radial gutter)
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (night splint)
  • Wrist sprain
  • Tendon injury (initial stabilization)

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster or fiberglass (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Slab along the volar surface of the forearm, from the proximal palmar crease to mid-forearm; extra padding over the ulnar styloid; mold with gentle pressure; ensure thumb and all fingers have full ROM.

POSITION: Wrist in 15–20° extension (position of function); fingers free — must be able to make a full fist; thumb completely free.

Does NOT control pronation/supination — if rotational control is needed, use sugar tong or A+P. For adult buckle (torus) fractures: volar splint with ortho follow-up in 1 week. Must not extend past the proximal palmar crease — blocking MCP flexion causes finger stiffness. Can be converted to a short arm cast at follow-up.

Lower Extremity

FRACTURE → SPLINT GUIDE
• Distal tibia or distal fibula → Posterior Short Leg (± stirrup for added stability)
• Ankle sprain grade 2/3 → Stirrup Splint
• Talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiform → Posterior Short Leg
• Metatarsal (2nd–5th) → Posterior Short Leg or hard-soled shoe
• Lisfranc → Posterior Short Leg (non-weight bearing, ortho follow-up)

5th METATARSAL SPECIAL CASES: Zone 1 (tuberosity/pseudo-Jones) → hard-soled shoe, weight bear as tolerated. Zone 2 (Jones fracture, metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction) → Posterior Short Leg, non-weight bearing, ortho follow-up (high nonunion risk). Zone 3 (shaft stress fracture) → Posterior Short Leg, non-weight bearing.

  • Distal tibia fracture (non-operative)
  • Distal fibula (lateral malleolus) fracture
  • Bimalleolar / trimalleolar ankle fracture (initial stabilization)
  • Talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiform fracture
  • Metatarsal fracture (non-displaced)
  • Jones fracture (5th MT zone 2)
  • Lisfranc injury
  • Achilles tendon rupture (initial, with ankle in plantarflexion)

MATERIALS: 6-inch plaster (12–15 layers for adults), stockinette, 3–4 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: Patient prone with knee flexed 90° or seated with foot hanging off the stretcher; extra padding over the malleoli, calcaneus, and Achilles tendon; posterior slab from toes to the tibial tuberosity, folded at the toes for a smooth foot plate; mold the ankle at 90° dorsiflexion (neutral); secure with elastic bandage from toes proximally.

ADDING A STIRRUP (bimalleolar/trimalleolar): U-shaped slab from medial mid-calf, under the heel, to lateral mid-calf, applied over the posterior slab before the elastic bandage.

POSITION: Ankle at 90° (neutral dorsiflexion) — prevents equinus contracture; subtalar joint neutral. Exception: Achilles rupture — 20° plantarflexion.

Must extend to the toes to prevent metatarsal/forefoot fractures from shifting. Extra Webril over the Achilles prevents pressure necrosis. Plaster should be wider than the posterior leg, wrapping the edges around the sides for better contouring. Always assess dorsalis pedis pulse, posterior tibial pulse, and sensation in all foot compartments.

  • Ankle sprain grade 2 (moderate — partial ligament tear)
  • Ankle sprain grade 3 (severe — complete ligament tear)
  • Isolated lateral malleolus fracture (stable, non-displaced)
  • Supplemental stability for posterior short leg splint

MATERIALS: 4-inch plaster (8–10 layers), stockinette, 2–3 layers Webril, elastic bandage.

APPLICATION: U-shaped slab from medial mid-calf, under the heel, to lateral mid-calf; extra padding over both malleoli; mold at 90° dorsiflexion; toes remain visible and mobile.

POSITION: Ankle at 90° (neutral dorsiflexion); subtalar joint neutral — not inverted or everted.

STIRRUP ALONE vs. WITH POSTERIOR SLAB: Stirrup alone — grade 2/3 ankle sprains, allows some plantarflexion/dorsiflexion for early mobilization. Stirrup + posterior slab — fractures, maximum stability.

For ankle sprains, stirrup alone allows early protected motion, which improves healing compared to rigid immobilization. The U-shape prevents inversion/eversion — the mechanism of most ankle sprains. Air splint (Aircast) can substitute for stable grade 2 sprains and is easier for the patient to remove for icing/exercises. Ottawa Ankle Rules: no splint needed if negative — patients can weight-bear with support. Always assess anterior drawer, squeeze test (syndesmosis), and base of 5th metatarsal for an associated fracture.

Region → Splint Map

Select body region — Pre-splint neurovascular exam, reduce angulations, apply stockinette then padding, mold with flat palms, post-splint NV check.

Humerus — Proximal humerus: sling; shaft: coaptation (U-shape); supracondylar: long arm posterior or double sugar tong.

Forearm — A+P splints preferred over sugar tong at our institution — two separate slabs without elbow wrap, better comfort.

Wrist & Hand — Scaphoid/1st MC: thumb spica; 4th/5th MC (boxer): ulnar gutter at 70–90° MCP flexion; distal phalanx: aluminum stack splint.

Lower Extremity — Posterior short leg for most ankle/foot fractures; stirrup for sprains; Jones fracture (5th MT zone 2) needs NWB and ortho.

Sling / Cuff & Collar — Sling with arm at 90° elbow — PROFHER trial: equivalent to surgery for most displaced proximal humerus fractures.

Coaptation Splint — U-shape from axilla around elbow to deltoid — transition to functional brace at 1–2wk (98% union rate); check radial nerve.

Long Arm Posterior — Posterior slab from palmar crease to mid-upper arm at 90° elbow; extra padding over olecranon and ulnar styloid.

Double Sugar Tong — Two overlapping U-shaped slabs for maximum upper extremity stability — forearm + arm sugar tong combined; monitor for compartment syndrome.

Sugar Tong (+ A+P alt) — Dorsal hand around elbow to palmar crease; A+P alternative uses two separate slabs without elbow restriction — equivalent stability.

Thumb Spica — Radial slab including thumb to IP joint in C-position (holding a can); suspected scaphoid with neg XR: splint 10–14d then re-image.

Volar Splint — Simplest ED splint — volar forearm slab in 15–20° wrist extension; must NOT extend past proximal palmar crease (blocks MCP flexion).

Ulnar Gutter — MCP at 70–90° flexion is CRITICAL — collateral ligaments taut in flexion; immobilizing in extension causes permanent stiffness.

Radial Gutter — Mirror of ulnar gutter on radial side; 2nd/3rd MC tolerate LESS angulation (>10° needs ortho); always check rotational alignment.

Posterior Short Leg — Posterior slab toes to tibial tuberosity at 90° ankle (neutral) — workhorse lower extremity splint; add stirrup for bimalleolar.

Stirrup Splint — U-shape medial to lateral under heel at 90° ankle — prevents inversion/eversion; alone for sprains, add posterior slab for fractures.

Long Arm Posterior and Double Sugar Tong are reached from both Humerus and Forearm; Thumb Spica and Volar are reached from both Forearm and Wrist & Hand — each appears once above and carries the same technique regardless of entry path.

General Splinting Principles & Quick Reference

MATERIALS
Plaster — moldable, sets in 3–5 min, exothermic reaction; better for fractures requiring 3-point molding; standard 8–12 layers depending on site.
Fiberglass — lighter, stronger, water-resistant, sets faster; better for stable fractures where molding is not critical; more expensive.
Stockinette — cotton tube applied directly to skin, protects from plaster irritation.
Webril (cast padding) — soft cotton roll, standard 2–4 layers.
Elastic bandage (ACE wrap) — secures the splint, wrapped distally to proximally, snug but not tight.

  • 2–4 layers Webril over the entire splinted area
  • Extra padding over bony prominences (malleoli, olecranon, styloids, calcaneus)
  • Minimal padding directly over the fracture site when molding is needed — excessive padding defeats the mold
  • Cotton between fingers for gutter splints to prevent maceration
  • Dip plaster in lukewarm water (not hot — the exothermic reaction adds heat and can burn)
  • Squeeze out excess water gently — do not wring
  • Smooth layers together on a flat surface to remove air pockets
  • Apply to the limb and mold with flat palms (thenar eminences) — never fingertips
  • Hold the mold until the plaster sets (3–5 minutes) — do not move hands during setting
  • Secure with elastic bandage — snug, not constricting

POSITION OF FUNCTION (unless otherwise specified): Wrist 15–20° extension; MCP joints 70–90° flexion (gutter splints — prevents collateral ligament contracture); IP joints slight flexion (10–20°); elbow 90° flexion; ankle 90° dorsiflexion (neutral — prevents equinus contracture); thumb abducted, slightly opposed ("holding a can").

  • Sensation: light touch in each nerve distribution (median, ulnar, radial for upper; deep peroneal, superficial peroneal, tibial, sural for lower)
  • Motor: thumb opposition (median), finger abduction (ulnar), wrist/finger extension (radial), toe dorsiflexion (deep peroneal), toe plantarflexion (tibial)
  • Vascular: capillary refill <2 seconds, pulses palpable (radial/ulnar for upper; dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial for lower)
  • Color and temperature: compare to the uninjured side
  • Document before AND after splinting

Our institution prefers A+P (anterior + posterior) splints over sugar tong for forearm fractures — two separate plaster slabs (one dorsal, one volar) rather than one continuous slab wrapping around the elbow. Equivalent AP stability without restricting elbow motion; less bulky, better patient comfort. Use sugar tong only when rotational control (pronation/supination) is specifically required.

Elevate above the level of the heart; ice 20 minutes on/off (over padding, not directly on skin); wiggle fingers/toes frequently to prevent stiffness and monitor for compartment syndrome; loosen the elastic bandage if swelling increases (mark the outer edge). Return immediately for: increasing pain not relieved by elevation and medication; numbness, tingling, or inability to move fingers/toes; fingers/toes turning blue, white, or cold; a splint that feels too tight or causes pressure pain — these are compartment syndrome warning signs, a surgical emergency.
