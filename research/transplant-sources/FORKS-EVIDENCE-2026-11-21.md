## 1. Adenosine for SVT in a heart transplant recipient

### What was read
**ISHLT 2023 heart guideline (saved PDF, JHLT 2023;42(5)):**
- PDF p.15 (narrative, Task Force 1): "Use of adenosine post-transplant was previously a relative contraindication due to presumed risk of prolonged AV block in the denervated heart. However, a recent study suggests minimal risk when low initial doses are used (25 mcg/kg; 1.5 mg if ≥ 60 kg) and therapy is gradually increased." Cites ref 126 = Flyer JN et al., Circulation 2017;135:2485-93.
- PDF p.20 (early postoperative care table): "New Recommendation Adenosine use may be considered in HT recipients with supraventricular tachyarrhythmias if they are closely monitored and low doses are administered (25 mcg/kg: 1.5 mg if ≥ 60 kg). Class IIb, Level of Evidence C".
- PDF p.79 (arrhythmia narrative): "Adenosine may be considered to terminate SVT in heart transplant recipients, but should only be administered with caution at low doses (e.g., 3 mg in adults) due to increased risk of severe bradycardia or asystole (refer to Task Force 1 ...).642" Ref 642 = Ellenbogen 1990. So the "3 mg" statement is an example in a narrative, sourced to the 1990 supersensitivity study (which gave no 3 mg dose; see below), and it points the reader back to the 1.5 mg recommendation.
- PDF p.96: "the denervated heart is highly sensitive to adenosine, and the use of standard doses to treat atrial tachyarrhythmias may result in prolonged asystole." (carried-forward 2010 text.)

**Flyer et al., Circulation 2017;135:2485-2493 (PMID 28450351, PMC5539538, NIH author manuscript, full text saved).**
- Population: 80 "healthy" heart transplant recipients age 6 months to 25 years (median 13.6 y, median weight 50.4 kg; 32 patients (40%) were ≥60 kg), single centre, routine catheterization 2015-16. Excluded: rejection treatment, IV inotropes, "less than two weeks post-transplant", CAV, conduction disease, LVEF <50%. "The median duration from transplant was 4.5 years (range 25 days - 19.9 years)"; 20 patients (25%) were <2 y from transplant.
- Route and setting: "rapidly administered via central access"; pacing catheter in place; no tachycardia (healthy, normal hemodynamics, sedation/anesthesia in some).
- Doses: <60 kg weight-based escalation 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200 mcg/kg; ≥60 kg: 0.8, 1.5, 3, 6, 12 mg. Dose given incrementally "until AV block was achieved or clinically significant asystole (...≥12 seconds...)".
- Outcomes: "None of the 80 patients (0%; 95% CI 0-3%) experienced clinically significant asystole requiring rescue ventricular pacing". "AV block (efficacy) was observed in 77 patients (96%; 95% CI 89-99%)". Cumulative AV block: "0% ... after 12.5mcg/kg; 12% ... after 25mcg/kg; 31% ... after 50mcg/kg; 72% ... after 100mcg/kg; and 96% ... after 200mcg/kg (or the maximum doses for ≥60kg)". Median longest RR interval 1.9 s (IQR 1.4-3.2); mean adenosine effect 4.3 s. 
- Conclusion (abstract): "Adenosine induces AV block in healthy pediatric and young adult heart transplant recipients with minimal risk when using low initial doses (25mcg/kg, or 1.5mg if ≥ 60kg) and gradually escalating therapy."
- Time since transplant: "this study did not reveal any relationship between effective adenosine dose and time from transplant, and suggests that adenosine can be safe as early as 25 days post-transplant." Also notes sympathetic reinnervation "as early as two to four years" and that reinnervation timing is not well understood.
- Authors' limitations: "Adenosine was tested in healthy transplant patients in normal hemodynamic settings; while it might be assumed that results would be similar in acutely ill, tachycardic patients, this has yet to be demonstrated"; "patients with acute onset of tachycardia are less likely to have central access ... larger doses may be required when injected through smaller and more distant peripheral veins"; study "may have underestimated" the efficacious dose.
- Key reading for the page: 1.5 mg / 25 mcg/kg was the STARTING step of an escalation; 25 mcg/kg terminated nothing by itself in most (AV block in 12% cumulative at that step). Adults ≥60 kg were a subset (n=32, mostly young adults). No adult-only or SVT-terminating-efficacy data. Study did not test SVT termination, only AV block.

**Ellenbogen KA et al., Circulation 1990;81:821-828 (PMID 2306833). ABSTRACT ONLY (Europe PMC JSON saved).** 28 orthotopic transplant patients and 9 controls; "incremental doses of intravenous adenosine (37-112 micrograms/kg)"; "the donor sinus node demonstrated a threefold to fourfold increased response to adenosine as compared with the recipient sinus node and a threefold to sixfold increased response as compared with control subjects"; "the donor AV node demonstrated a threefold to fivefold increase in PR interval as compared with control subjects"; "The duration of sinus node slowing in the denervated atria was threefold to fivefold longer" (abstract truncated at 250 words). Time since transplant and any asystole data are not in the abstract. Doses tested were 37-112 mcg/kg (about 2.6 to 7.8 mg in a 70 kg adult), not 3 mg flat.

**Adenosine labels (DailyMed, saved: dailymed/adenosine-henryschein_d28ff4f3.xml, Revised February 2023).** No statement on heart transplant or denervation. I counted zero occurrences of "transplant"/"denerv" in this label and in the Sagent (setid 546642f2..., revised March 2025) and Fresenius (e660f71c..., revised February 2023) generic labels (counts only; those two not saved). "Adenocard" brand SPL not on DailyMed (search returned none). Label dosing: "Initial dose: 6 mg given as a rapid intravenous bolus"; "The dose recommendation is based on clinical studies with peripheral venous bolus dosing. Central venous (CVP or other) administration ... has not been systematically studied." Label interaction text: "Adenosine effects are potentiated by dipyridamole. Thus, smaller doses of adenosine may be effective in the presence of dipyridamole." (Flyer stopped dipyridamole 3 days before testing.)

**EHRA 2025 antiarrhythmic compendium (Europace; PMC12367031; saved).** Mentions transplant only for atropine ("may be ineffective in heart transplant recipients due to vagal denervation"). Adenosine section has no transplant dosing statement; its interaction table lists "Dipyridamole (potentiates adenosine): Reduce adenosine dose".

**Not read (blocked):** AHA/ACC/HRS 2015 SVT guideline, AHA 2020/2025 ACLS, ERC, ESC 2019 SVT. I therefore cannot report what they say about transplanted hearts, and I am not asserting that they are silent.

### What the evidence supports
- ISHLT's own recommendation (IIb, C) is the low-dose, closely monitored, escalating approach: 25 mcg/kg, 1.5 mg if ≥60 kg. This rests on one prospective study, in healthy children and young adults, central-line, no tachycardia, patients ≥14 days (median 4.5 y) post-transplant, 0/80 needing pacing.
- The "3 mg" is an example in a narrative paragraph referencing a 1990 supersensitivity paper; it sits one step above the 1.5 mg in Flyer's ≥60 kg escalation (0.8, 1.5, 3, 6, 12 mg). The two ISHLT figures are therefore consistent as steps of an escalation more than as competing doses, but ISHLT does not say so.
- Denervated hearts show 3-6 fold greater response (Ellenbogen abstract); the standard 6 mg label dose is the first step of the label and would exceed the escalation tested.

### What it does not settle
- Dose for a hemodynamically unstable adult in SVT given peripherally; efficacy at 1.5 or 3 mg for terminating SVT; the first-line status vs alternative (the ISHLT class is only IIb).
- Early postoperative (<2 weeks) patients were excluded from Flyer; reinnervation timing (years) not tied to any dose change.
- What AHA/ACC/HRS, AHA ACLS, ERC, ESC say (not obtained).

### Options for the page
A. Keep both ISHLT figures with their locations as now (strongest: ISHLT 2023 PDF p.20 and p.79), adding the fact that the "3 mg" paragraph points back to the Task Force 1 recommendation.
B. State the ISHLT recommendation (25 mcg/kg; 1.5 mg if ≥60 kg; IIb, C) as the sourced figure, with the study basis in detail (escalation, central line, healthy, age range), and move "3 mg" to detail as a narrative example (strongest: ISHLT p.20 plus Flyer 2017).
C. Owner-approved editor note on the `svt` node: the 6 mg then 12 mg critical action in the Adult SVT consult is the non-transplant label dose and is not the dose tested in transplant recipients (strongest: adenosine label, Flyer 2017, ISHLT p.96 "standard doses ... prolonged asystole").

## 2. The "8-12 ng/dL" tacrolimus figure (AASLD/AST 2025 liver guideline, Question 23)

**Guideline text** (saved HTML, AASLD_AST_LT_nongraft_complications_2025_LVT0785.html; Liver Transpl 2026;32(8):1157-1193, DOI 10.1097/LVT.0000000000000785; Question 23 rationale, very early post-transplant): "An RCT that compared induction therapy comprising daclizumab, MPA, corticosteroids, along with delayed low-dose tacrolimus (trough levels 8-12 ng/dL), demonstrated improved median eGFR in the daclizumab+delayed low-dose tacrolimus groups without any significant differences in rates of acute cellular rejection (ACR).272"
- Ref 272 in the saved reference list = Yoshida EM, Marotta PJ, Greig PD, et al. Liver Transpl 2005;11:1064-72 (ref 273 = Neuberger ReSpECT, Am J Transplant 2009; 274 = Calmus, Transplantation 2010). Table 4 of the guideline lists "Yoshida et al. 272 N=148 ... DAC+reduced TAC delayed 6 d vs. standard TAC".
- Yoshida 2005 (PMID 16123958, DOI 10.1002/lt.20490). ABSTRACT ONLY (LWW abstract page saved; Europe PMC JSON saved): "comparing daclizumab, delayed low-dose tacrolimus (target trough level 4-8 ng/mL, starting day 4-6), Investigational Arm (n = 72), to standard tacrolimus induction/maintenance dosing, Standard Arm (n = 76)". Unit in the trial abstract: ng/mL (not ng/dL). Range in the trial abstract: 4-8, not 8-12. N 72 + 76 = 148, matching Table 4.
- Other daclizumab trial cited (ref 274, Calmus 2010, abstract only): delayed tacrolimus with daclizumab; abstract states no target range.
- Other occurrence of "8-12" in the saved sources: IATDMCT tacrolimus consensus 2019 describes the TRANSFORM standard-exposure kidney arm as "target C0 of 8-12, 6-10 and 5-8 ng/mL" (an unrelated kidney trial; unit ng/mL).

### What the evidence supports
- The guideline's unit "ng/dL" does not match ng/mL, the unit used in the cited trial abstract and everywhere else in the guideline (e.g., Table "7-10 ng/mL"). ng/dL is a misprint for ng/mL on the evidence available.
- The trial's investigational low-dose arm target in the abstract was 4-8 ng/mL; the guideline's "8-12" does not match the abstract's range for that arm.

### What it does not settle
- Whether 8-12 appears in the Yoshida full text (for example as a standard-arm or early-period target); I could not read the full text. Whether the guideline authors transposed, merged arms, or used another trial.

### Options for the page
A. Keep "8-12 ng/dL" quoted exactly as printed, with an editor note that the unit is probably ng/mL and that the cited trial's abstract gives 4-8 ng/mL (strongest: Yoshida abstract; owner approval needed for the note).
B. Drop the figure from the page and cite only the guideline's qualitative statement (daclizumab plus delayed low-dose tacrolimus, higher eGFR, no difference in rejection), noting the range is not reproduced because it does not match the cited trial (strongest: guideline Q23 plus Yoshida abstract).
C. Hold the figure until the owner supplies the Yoshida full text (Liver Transpl 2005;11:1064-72) so the page quotes the trial's own number.

## 3. Tacrolimus target ranges by organ: who sets them, and what a non-transplant clinician does with an out-of-range level

Searched (full text, saved files): IATDMCT tacrolimus 2019 (2nd consensus), ISHLT 2023, AASLD/AST 2025 (graft-related and non-graft), KDIGO 2009, Prograf label, Zhong 2018 ED review. No sentence found that says target ranges are "centre-specific" or "set by the transplant team". Nearest statements:
- Prograf label 2.6: "Monitoring of tacrolimus blood concentrations in conjunction with other laboratory and clinical parameters is considered an essential aid to patient management for the evaluation of rejection, toxicity, dose adjustments, and compliance. Whole blood trough concentration range can be found in Table 1." Also "The bias may depend upon the specific assay and laboratory" and "Comparison of the concentrations in published literature to patient concentrations using the current assays must be made with detailed knowledge of the assay methods". Factors for frequency: "hepatic or renal dysfunction, the addition or discontinuation of potentially interacting drugs and the post-transplant time."
- IATDMCT 2019: "personalization of the dosing by frequent measurements of whole blood concentrations, to apply target concentration ranges, which have gradually been set at lower levels, based on the results from multicenter trials"; "the recommendations for achieving specific target concentrations of tacrolimus are based on the results obtained from multicenter prospective clinical trials for different types of SOT in specific cohorts of low or high immunological risk patients, also taking the combination with other immunosuppressive drugs into consideration"; "Tacrolimus C0 targets may be higher in adult patients at higher immunological risks (B II)"; on assays: "introducing laboratory- (or method-)specific target ranges ... may pose a hidden danger". Target depends on regimen, risk, time (e.g., TRANSFORM: 4-7, 2-5, 2-4 ng/mL low-exposure vs 8-12, 6-10, 5-8 standard).
- ISHLT 2023 Table 4 title: "Recommended mTOR Inhibitor & CNI Target Levels"; text: "TAC C0 targets used in combination with mTOR inhibitors varied depending on time after cardiac transplant"; pediatric: "lower targets may be used in patients believed to be at low risk for acute rejection". The ISHLT guideline separately says (HLA antibodies) "each transplant center must define the threshold", which is not about drug levels.
- KDIGO 2009 5.1 "We recommend measuring CNI blood levels (1B)... whenever there is a change in medication or patient status that may affect blood levels (2C)" and the CsA background: "the CsA dosage must be individualized to find a balance between high levels that may be toxic and low levels that may be insufficient to prevent rejection."
- Out-of-range level in the emergency setting, for a non-transplant clinician: no guideline statement found in the saved sources. DECISIONS.md already holds owner-approved editor notes on this topic.

### What the evidence supports
- Ranges depend on organ, regimen (mTOR inhibitor, induction), immunological risk, time after transplant and the assay, per IATDMCT and ISHLT; level measurement is an aid to, not a replacement for, clinical and organ-function assessment (Prograf 2.6).
### What it does not settle
- No verbatim "centre-specific / transplant team sets the target" statement in any source read; no guidance for the ED on acting on an out-of-range level.
### Options for the page
A. Keep the ranges side by side with an editor note, already owner-approved, that the team sets the target; do not add a source claim (strongest: the six approved editor notes).
B. Add a sourced line that targets vary by organ, regimen, risk, time and assay, quoting IATDMCT 2019 and Prograf 2.6 (strongest: IATDMCT 2019 executive summary; label 2.6).
C. Ask the owner whether to seek AST or transplant-pharmacy statements outside these sources (none found in this session).

## 4. G-CSF for neutropenia in a solid organ transplant recipient

- KDIGO 2009 (saved PDF p.112, Chapter 22 rationale): "There are several case reports documenting the use of colony-stimulating factors (CSFs) to treat neutropenia in kidney transplant patients (756-758). However, there is potential for harm with treatment. One case report suggested that CSFs may have been associated with worsening graft function (758)." "There is evidence, though, that patients with febrile neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count <500/µL ...) benefit from CSFs along with antibiotics if there is pneumonia, fungal infection, hypotension, sepsis syndrome or multisystem organ failure." "There are not likely to be any RCTs to determine when to give CSFs in KTRs. Guidance for their use will be derived mostly from local clinical practice and oncology guidelines." Graded recommendation 22.3 (Not Graded) addresses underlying causes only.
- CMV consensus 2018 (saved PDF p.16): "In the setting of leukopenia, changing (val)ganciclovir to another agent is not recommended before the addition of granulocyte colony stimulating factor and/or discontinuation of other myelosuppressive therapies (strong, low)."
- CMV consensus 2025 (PMC12180710, adult treatment section): "Instead of reducing antiviral doses, discontinuation of other myelosuppressive therapies should be tried. The use of colony-stimulating factors has been shown to be safe and could be considered." (cites Turgeon 2000). Statement: "Options include consideration of interventions such as the use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor and/or adjusting other myelosuppressive therapies (strong, moderate)" (the grade belongs to the statement, whose main clause is the no-dose-reduction rule).
- Turgeon N et al., Transpl Infect Dis 2000;2:15-21 (PMID 11429005). ABSTRACT ONLY (Europe PMC JSON saved): retrospective, 50 kidney or liver recipients, 100 courses; WBC <3000/mm3 trigger; "Eight G-CSF treatments (8.0%) were followed by episodes of rejection appearing during or within 2 months of treatment; 5 of them were biopsy-documented. No relation was found between the highest WBC obtained during G-CSF therapy and the risk of rejection." "In 40 of 49 courses (81.6%), ganciclovir could be continued at recommended doses." "G-CSF was well tolerated in solid organ transplant recipients."
- Kidney Int Rep 2026 review (PMC13697321; narrative review, not a guideline): "If leukopenia develops, the use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor can be considered."
- AASLD/AST 2025: no G-CSF statement found; ISHLT 2023: no G-CSF statement found; AST IDCOP 2019 chapters: not obtained (blocked).

### What the evidence supports
- Two CMV consensus documents list G-CSF as an option during CMV treatment; KDIGO frames it as case-report-level with a graft-function harm signal and refers to oncology rules (benefit for febrile neutropenia <500 with pneumonia, fungal infection, hypotension, sepsis or organ failure). The only transplant-specific safety data are one retrospective series (rejection within 2 months after 8% of courses, no association with WBC peak) and one case report.
### What it does not settle
- Who, at what count, and whether rejection risk is increased (no controlled data); AST IDCOP view unknown.
### Options for the page
A. Keep "options include G-CSF" as now (CMV 2025), with KDIGO's caution and "no ED rule" line (strongest: CMV 2025 plus KDIGO p.112).
B. Add the KDIGO 2009 rationale quote (case reports, potential graft harm, oncology-derived indication) and the Turgeon rejection figure labelled abstract-only, as detail (strongest: KDIGO p.112; Turgeon abstract).
C. Owner-approved editor note: G-CSF is a decision for the transplant team or infectious diseases (strongest: absence of any ED-level rule).

## 5. Valganciclovir or ganciclovir at low counts

- Labels (already on page): avoid / not recommended below ANC 500, platelets 25,000, haemoglobin 8 (saved DailyMed Valcyte and ganciclovir labels, not re-quoted here).
- CMV consensus 2025 (treatment section): "In the setting of leukopenia, attempts should be made to continue the use of valganciclovir or ganciclovir. ... We do not recommend reducing the dose of valganciclovir or ganciclovir based on leukopenia alone (strong, moderate). In patients who are intolerant to valganciclovir or ganciclovir during the treatment phase, maribavir or foscarnet are recommended second-line treatments (strong, low)." Choice "on the basis of viral load, risk of nephrotoxicity, and severity of CMV disease".
- CMV consensus 2025 prophylaxis: "For D+/R- lung, heart, liver, or kidney transplants where prophylaxis is used, if there is intolerance to valganciclovir or ganciclovir, we suggest switching to preemptive therapy or to letermovir prophylaxis (weak, low)" (same for R+). "In kidney, liver, and pancreas recipients with valganciclovir-related cytopenias during primary prophylaxis, there was a decrease in the use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor and a reduction in the need to modify mycophenolate dosing after patients were switched to letermovir." In the pediatric section only: "Empiric dose reduction for presumed antiviral hematologic toxicity is not recommended (strong, moderate)." (do not apply to adults).
- CMV consensus 2018 (PDF p.16): leukopenia: change agent "not recommended before the addition of granulocyte colony stimulating factor and/or discontinuation of other myelosuppressive therapies (strong, low)"; "In patients who are intolerant to (val)ganciclovir during the treatment phase, foscarnet (FOS) is the recommended second-line agent (strong, very low)."
- AASLD/AST 2025 (non-graft, CMV section): "letermovir can be substituted for valganciclovir if there is persistent neutropenia from valganciclovir prophylaxis (Strong, Level 2)" for liver recipients seronegative for HSV/VZV or on acyclovir cover; "Letermovir should not be routinely prescribed for treatment of active infection."
- KDIGO 2009: no count threshold for CMV drugs found. AST IDCOP 2019 CMV chapter: not obtained.
- None of the 2018 consensus, 2025 consensus, AASLD/AST or KDIGO gives a numeric count at which to switch agents. Search of 2018 and 2025 texts for neutrophil, platelet or WBC numbers found only diagnostic assay thresholds.

### What the evidence supports
- Both consensus documents: continue the antiviral and add G-CSF or reduce other marrow-suppressing drugs before changing agent during treatment; switch only for intolerance (foscarnet, plus maribavir in 2025). For prophylaxis: switch to letermovir or preemptive therapy on intolerance or persistent neutropenia.
### What it does not settle
- Any count at which to switch; how labels' "avoid below ANC 500" reconciles with "attempt to continue" (consensus does not cite the label thresholds); AST IDCOP view.
### Options for the page
A. Keep label and consensus side by side as now (strongest: label text and CMV 2025 treatment statement).
B. Add the 2018 and 2025 switch rules (foscarnet; maribavir or foscarnet) and the prophylaxis letermovir statements as detail, and state that no source gives a count threshold for switching (strongest: CMV 2025; AASLD/AST 2025).
C. Owner-approved editor note that the decision to continue, add G-CSF or change agent belongs to the transplant team or infectious diseases when counts are below the label thresholds.

## 6. Allopurinol with azathioprine

- KDIGO 2009 (PDF p.115, 23.1.2): "We recommend avoiding allopurinol in patients receiving azathioprine. (1B)". Rationale (PDF p.116): "allopurinol and azathioprine used together can result in profound, life-threatening pancytopenia (627,753), and thus this combination should be used with extreme caution, or not at all. If used together, azathioprine should be reduced by at least 50% and frequent complete blood counts should be used to monitor the interaction (806). Further dose reductions may be needed. Mycophenolate does not interact with allopurinol and can be used in place of azathioprine ..."
- Imuran label (saved DailyMed imuran_4d62b6e4.xml, Revised 09/2026; Sebela label Revised 07/2024 has identical wording), Drug Interactions: "One of the pathways for inactivation of azathioprine is inhibited by XO inhibitors (allopurinol or febuxostat). Patients receiving IMURAN and allopurinol concomitantly should have a dose reduction of IMURAN, to approximately 1/3 to 1/4 the usual dose. Concomitant use of IMURAN and febuxostat is not recommended." Also: "a further dose reduction or alternative therapies be considered for patients with low or absent TPMT activity receiving IMURAN and xanthine oxidase inhibitors".
- Uloric (febuxostat) label (saved dailymed/uloric_54de10ef.xml, Revised April 2023, SPL published Dec 2025): "ULORIC is contraindicated in patients being treated with azathioprine or mercaptopurine."
- ACR 2020 gout guideline (Arthritis Care Res 2020, PMC10563586; saved): searched full text for azathioprine, mercaptopurine, transplant, immunosuppress: no occurrences. It names allopurinol first-line ("allopurinol as the preferred first-line ULT, including for those with moderate-to-severe CKD") without addressing transplant or azathioprine.
- Transplant guidelines other than KDIGO: none addressing this in saved sources (ISHLT, AASLD).
### What the evidence supports
- KDIGO (graded 1B, avoid) and the label (reduce to about 1/3 to 1/4) both treat the combination as dangerous; they differ in action (avoid vs reduced-dose co-use). Febuxostat: label says not recommended; its own label says contraindicated.
### What it does not settle
- Whether co-use with dose reduction is acceptable in practice; recipients on azathioprine are now uncommon in some programs; ACR is silent.
### Options for the page
A. Keep both as now (KDIGO 1B avoid; label reduction) (strongest: KDIGO p.115-116; Imuran label).
B. State avoid-first with KDIGO grade, then add the label's co-use dose reduction and febuxostat lines as detail (strongest: KDIGO 1B plus label).
C. Add a febuxostat line quoting both labels (not recommended; contraindicated) and the ACR silence (strongest: Imuran label; Uloric label).

## 7. Allograft pyelonephritis disposition

- KDIGO 2009 (PDF p.72, 14.1.2): "For allograft pyelonephritis, we suggest initial hospitalization and treatment with intravenous antibiotics. (2C)". Rationale: "KTRs with clinical and laboratory evidence suggestive of kidney allograft pyelonephritis should be hospitalized and treated with intravenous antibiotics for at least the initial course of therapy. This is particularly true in early infections (first 4-6 months following kidney transplantation)." Duration: "a less-prolonged course may be required, although patients experiencing relapsing infection should be considered for a more prolonged therapeutic course." (6-week courses were an 1980s practice.)
- AST IDCOP 2019 UTI chapter (Clin Transplant 2019;33:e13507, PMID 30793386): ABSTRACT ONLY (Europe PMC): "some renal transplant recipients with UTI may primarily present with fever, malaise, leukocytosis, or a non-specific sepsis syndrome without symptoms localized to the urinary tract"; "there are no benefits of antibiotics for treatment of AB in KT recipients more than 2 months after post-transplant. Further research is needed on management of AB in the early (<2 months) post-transplant period". Full text blocked.
- Secondhand report of the AST IDCOP and Spanish guideline (Avni-Nachman et al., Transpl Int 2021, doi 10.1111/tri.14144, saved PDF p.2): "recommends that kidney transplant recipients with allograft pyelonephritis should undergo a 14-21 days course of antibiotics, depending on the time elapsed from transplantation. They state that no data are available on short courses (7 days)..." This is the authors' paraphrase, not the guideline text. The cohort (214 hospitalized recipients, median 8 vs prolonged courses) was retrospective and included patients hospitalized in the first 6 months.
- IDSA 2025 complicated UTI guideline update (open; page saved; published July 17, 2025): no occurrence of "transplant" on the page. "The trials supporting this recommendation mostly excluded patients with indwelling urinary catheters, sepsis or septic shock, immunocompromised states, severe renal insufficiency, and functional or structural abnormalities of the urinary tract. Some patients in these subpopulations may need an individualized plan of therapy." Also: "Some patients with cUTI can be managed entirely with oral antibiotics in the outpatient setting." The IV-to-oral and duration (7 days vs longer) recommendations were not shown to me for transplant patients; transplant recipients are not addressed on this page. The full manuscript was not retrieved.
- Early versus late: KDIGO distinguishes (early infections particularly); AST IDCOP abstract distinguishes for asymptomatic bacteriuria only (2 months).
### What the evidence supports
- The only primary statement on disposition is KDIGO (2C, suggest). IDSA 2025 cUTI evidence base excluded immunocompromised patients and the page does not address transplant.
### What it does not settle
- Full AST IDCOP admission/route/duration text (blocked); whether stable late post-transplant patients can be treated as outpatients.
### Options for the page
A. Keep as now: KDIGO 2C quoted, consult lists immunocompromised for admission (strongest: KDIGO p.72).
B. Add KDIGO's early-infection sentence (first 4-6 months) as detail, and the IDSA 2025 exclusion sentence as the reason the Adult UTI outpatient default is not tested in recipients (strongest: KDIGO p.72; IDSA 2025 page).
C. Owner-approved editor note that outpatient treatment is not supported by the guidelines read; disposition is with the transplant team (strongest: KDIGO 2C; absence of transplant statement in IDSA 2025).

## 8. Fever with low neutrophils in a non-cancer transplant recipient

- IDSA 2010 febrile neutropenia guideline (Clin Infect Dis 2011;52:e56-93, PMID 21258094). ABSTRACT ONLY (Europe PMC JSON saved): "It is intended as a guide for the use of antimicrobial agents in managing patients with cancer who experience chemotherapy-induced fever and neutropenia." "It remains true that all patients who present with fever and neutropenia should be treated swiftly and broadly with antibiotics..." (abstract sentence; the abstract continues beyond what I displayed). Scope: cancer, chemotherapy-induced.
- Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 (PMC8486643; saved): "The recommendations in this document are intended to provide guidance for the clinician caring for adult patients with sepsis or septic shock in the hospital setting." No statement on neutropenia in non-cancer patients or on transplant recipients' applicability (the page mentions neutropenia only in the antifungal risk-factor table and "Solid organ transplantation" as a risk factor for Candida and mold).

## 9. Calcium-channel blockers for hypertension in a recipient on a calcineurin inhibitor

- KDIGO 2021 BP in CKD (Kidney Int 2021;99(3S); PDF saved, from kdigo.org), Chapter 4: "Recommendation 4.1: We recommend that a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker (CCB) or an ARB be used as the first-line antihypertensive agent in adult kidney transplant recipients (1C)." Practice Point 4.1 target "<130 mm Hg systolic and <80 mm Hg diastolic". Rationale: "only the dihydropyridine CCB group caused a 38% reduction in graft loss (RR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.43-0.90)"; non-dihydropyridine "not statistically significant (RR: 0.91 ...)". The chapter text contains no occurrence of "calcineurin"; no CNI interaction statement.
- ESH 2023 (J Hypertens 2023; LWW full text, saved; section 19.3): "In kidney transplanted patients, DHP-CCBs have been consistently associated with benefits such as improved graft survival and minimization of the vasoconstrictive effects of calcineurin inhibitors at the preglomerular site." "Thus, CCBs can be preferentially used in the early posttransplantation period." No class/level in this section; no diltiazem or verapamil CNI-interaction sentence found.
- ISHLT 2023: hypertension narrative (PDF p.76): "Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), and calcium channel blockers generally represent first-line therapy..." (no class/level on that sentence). PDF p.15: "Amiodarone and calcium antagonists can increase calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) levels. Therefore, CNI dose adjustment and level monitoring are required." Table 5 (PDF p.44) lists "Diltiazem" and "Verapamil" (Cardiovascular, "Increase immunosuppression levels"); no dihydropyridine is named. Pediatric: "blood pressure control with a calcium channel blocker or ACEI is warranted (Class I, Level of Evidence: C)" (children, not adults).
- AASLD/AST 2025 (non-graft, Question 15): "In liver transplant recipients with no evidence of cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease, use of amlodipine or felodipine as first-line antihypertensive therapy is recommended (Strong, Level 5)." Rationale: "use of calcium channel blockers (amlodipine and felodipine) in LT recipients is reasonable given the mechanism of action, which involves reversal of renal vasoconstriction, and minimal interaction with CNI."
- Labels (saved): tacrolimus labels name verapamil, diltiazem, nifedipine, nicardipine (one mention each); cyclosporine labels (Neoral, Gengraf, Sandimmune) name diltiazem, nicardipine, verapamil as raising levels and, separately, nifedipine for gingival hyperplasia: "The concomitant use of nifedipine should be avoided in patients in whom gingival hyperplasia develops". Sirolimus label names diltiazem and verapamil (7 mentions each) and nicardipine. Amlodipine and felodipine: zero mentions in all eight CNI/mTOR labels searched (Prograf, Astagraf XL, Envarsus XR, Neoral, Gengraf, Sandimmune, sirolimus, Zortress).
- ESC 2024 and ESC/ESH 2018: blocked; not read.
### What the evidence supports
- Named as interacting (raising levels): diltiazem and verapamil (labels, ISHLT Table 5); nicardipine and nifedipine (tacrolimus labels; nicardipine also cyclosporine). Not named as interacting in any label or guideline read: amlodipine, felodipine. Only AASLD/AST says amlodipine and felodipine have minimal interaction (Strong, Level 5, liver recipients). KDIGO 2021 (1C, kidney) recommends dihydropyridine CCB or ARB first-line without a CNI-interaction statement.
### What it does not settle
- A source-graded statement that amlodipine does not change CNI levels in kidney or heart recipients; nicardipine infusion rules in the Hypertensive Emergency consult.
