## 1. Who is at risk (dose and duration thresholds)

**1.1 Threshold statement** (Definitions [P90]-[P92]; no grade)
> "Duration of glucocorticoid therapy to pose risk for adrenal insufficiency —3-4 weeks, or greater" [P91]
> "any dose greater than daily hydrocortisone equivalent of 15-25 mg (4-6 mg prednisone or prednisolone, 3-5 mg methylprednisone, 0.25-0.5 mg dexamethasone)" [P92]
[P90] states oral exposure that poses risk "is expected to at least exceed both of the following thresholds" (duration and dose).

**1.2 Physiologic dose equivalent** (Definitions [P94]): "15-25 mg hydrocortisone, 4-6 mg prednisone or prednisolone, 3-5 mg methylprednisone, 0.25-0.5 mg dexamethasone". Supraphysiologic = "Any dose greater than physiologic daily dose equivalent" [P95]. Long-term = "greater than 3-4 weeks duration with glucocorticoid doses greater than physiologic daily dose equivalent" [P97].

**1.3 Any supraphysiologic dose can suppress** (Pathophysiology [P77]): "Any glucocorticoid dose above the physiologic daily dose equivalent can potentially lead to suppression of the HPA axis."

**1.4 Immunosuppressive doses always suppress** (Table 1 note, [P83]; no grade): "immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory doses considerably exceed the equivalent of endogenous cortisol production and, therefore, invariably result in HPA axis suppression."

**1.5 Short courses** R 2.1 [P44], (very low): "We suggest not to taper glucocorticoids in patients on short-term glucocorticoid therapy of <3-4 weeks, irrespective of the dose." Short-term defined [P96] as under 3-4 weeks.

**1.6 Risk-factor grid** (Table 3, **read from image**): risk of adrenal insufficiency and crisis. Potency: Low = hydrocortisone, cortisone acetate, deflazacort; Moderate = prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone, triamcinolone; High = dexamethasone, betamethasone, fluticasone. Route: High = systemic (oral, intramuscular, intravenous), intra-articular, concurrent use of differently administered glucocorticoid; Moderate = inhaled; Low = nasal, topical, ophthalmic. Dose: Low / Medium / High. Duration: Low <3-4 weeks; Moderate 3-4 weeks-3 months; High >3 months. BMI: Normal / Overweight / Obese. Age: Younger adults (low) / Older adults (high). The Table 3 image has no numeric dose cut-offs for Low/Medium/High.

**1.7 Other situations to be aware of** R 2.9 [P58]-[P64], (very low), "suggest awareness" in: non-oral formulations with symptoms; multiple formulations simultaneously; high-dose inhaled or topical; inhaled or topical >1 year; intra-articular injection in previous 2 months; "receiving concomitant treatment with strong cytochrome P450 3A4 inhibitors."

**1.8 Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors named** (Table 6 footnote d, [P261], text): "boceprevir, ceritinib, clarithromycin, cobicistat, darunavir, idelalisib, indinavir, itraconazole, ketoconazole, lopinavir, mifepristone, nefazodone, nelfinavir, posaconazole, ritonavir, saquinavir, telaprevir, telithromycin, and voriconazole."
Rationale ([P266], no grade): "Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors ... have been shown to significantly increase circulating concentrations of glucocorticoids and hence substantially increase the risk of suppressing HPA axis."
(The guideline does not mention isavuconazole, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, or sirolimus anywhere in the saved text.)

**1.9 Strong CYP3A4 inducers as a crisis trigger** (Table 9, **read from image**): "Initiation of strong cytochrome P450 3A4 inducers, which leads to increased liver metabolism of several glucocorticoids. Strong inducers include apalutamide, carbamazepine, enzalutamide, fosphenytoin, lumacaftor, lumacaftor-ivacaftor, mitotane, phenobarbital, phenytoin, primidone, and rifampicin".

**1.10 Prevalence figures** (Epidemiology [P84], no grade): biochemical glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency pooled 48.7% (95% CI 36.9-60.6) for oral use. Authors caution [P85] that "clinical symptoms are not specific and do not correlate well with biochemical findings."

## 2. How adrenal crisis presents and is recognised

**2.1 Diagnostic definition** (Table 9, **read from image**, row "Diagnosis"): "Hypotension or hypovolemic shock plus at least one of the following: Nausea or vomiting; Severe fatigue; Fever; Impaired consciousness (incl. lethargy, confusion, somnolence, collapse, delirium, coma, and seizures)".

**2.2 Possible laboratory findings, "not required for the diagnosis"** (Table 9, **read from image**): hyponatremia (typically with raised urinary sodium); hyperkalemia; signs of volume depletion (eg raised urea and creatinine); hypoglycemia; lymphocytosis; eosinophilia.

**2.3 General features** (Table 9, **read from image**): "Patients present with a shock out of proportion to the severity of the trigger, if a trigger is identified"; "The shock is typically resistant to inotropes and fluid resuscitation if the adrenal crisis is not recognized and promptly treated with parenteral glucocorticoids". Risk factors: "a history of previous adrenal crises, older age (>65 years), adolescence and transition from pediatric to adult care, and a higher comorbidity burden". "Glucocorticoid tapering down and discontinuation are crucial times".

**2.4 Clinical diagnosis** (R 3.2 Rationale [P330]; no grade): "Adrenal crisis is a clinical diagnosis and should be suspected in patients with current or recent use of any glucocorticoid formulation presenting with hypotension, collapse, or acute abdominal symptoms". "A high degree of clinical suspicion is paramount, as patients may not have been tested ... and adrenal crisis may be the first manifestation of the disease."

**2.5 Triggers** (Table 9, **read from image**): infections (gastrointestinal, genitourinary, respiratory, sepsis); acute illness incl. fever; physical trauma; surgery or other procedures under general, regional or local anesthesia; bowel procedures requiring laxatives/enema; labor and delivery; dental procedures; severe stress and pain; strenuous exercise. Specific to glucocorticoid-induced: abrupt withdrawal after long-term treatment; tapering below physiological replacement doses; switch between inhaled formulations; starting strong CYP3A4 inducers (1.9).

**2.6 Crisis can occur on supraphysiologic oral doses** (R 3.2 Rationale [P295]; no grade): "Adrenal crisis ... can occur in patients taking oral supraphysiologic doses of glucocorticoids, if drug availability suddenly decreases (eg, missed doses, gastroenteritis)." "invariably characterized by volume depletion and vasoplegia resulting in hypotension". "timely therapy is essential and takes precedence over the evaluation for other causes of symptoms".

**2.7 When to consider it** R 3.2 [P72] (Good clinical practice): in current or recent glucocorticoid users not biochemically tested "and present with hemodynamic instability, vomiting, or diarrhea, the diagnosis of adrenal crisis should be considered irrespective of the glucocorticoid type, mode of administration, and dose".

**2.8 Withdrawal syndrome vs insufficiency** (Rationale [P151]-[P152]; no grade): "an insufficient glucocorticoid supply is not expected to occur when the glucocorticoid dose is greater than the physiologic daily dose equivalent. As exceptions, it should be noted that the glucocorticoid requirement may be significantly higher in the case of critical illness or that glucocorticoid absorption is not guaranteed in gastroenteritis." And [P152]: "concerns for spontaneous symptoms and signs of adrenal insufficiency are only a concern once the taper reaches 5-7.5 mg."

## 3. Emergency treatment of suspected adrenal crisis

**3.1 Drug, dose, route** (R 3.2 Rationale [P330]; no grade): "If an established or impending adrenal crisis is suspected, the patient should immediately receive an injection of 100 mg hydrocortisone intravenously or intramuscularly followed by rapid volume resuscitation with intravenous administration of 0.9% saline solution (or equivalent)". "Treatment must not be delayed by laboratory or imaging investigations."

**3.2 Maintenance** ([P330]): confirmed crisis "should be maintained on hydrocortisone at a dose of 200 mg hydrocortisone per 24 hours (preferably by continuous intravenous infusion, alternatively by intravenous or intramuscular injection of 50 mg hydrocortisone every 6 hours) until clinical recovery and further guidance by an endocrinology specialist". "In patients with very high body weight, higher doses might be considered."

**3.3 Other glucocorticoids** ([P330]): "Some centers use equivalent parenteral doses of other glucocorticoids such as methylprednisolone or dexamethasone; head-to-head comparison data ... are lacking."

**3.4 Treat even if diagnosis later excluded** ([P330]): "treatment should be initiated even if an adrenal crisis diagnosis is eventually ruled out." Triggers (infection, trauma) "should be treated where possible."

**3.5 Parenteral route required** R 3.2 [P72] (Good clinical practice): "patients with suspected adrenal crisis should be treated with parenteral glucocorticoids and fluid resuscitation."

**3.6 Mineralocorticoid** R 2.12 [P67], ungraded: "We recommend against the use of fludrocortisone in patients with glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency." Rationale [P275]: "mineralocorticoid function is expected to be preserved".

**3.7 Moderate/major stress, hospital** (Table 8, **read from image**): "Patients requiring hospital admission: Hydrocortisone 100 mg I.V. bolus or I.M. injection immediately, followed by immediate initiation of a continuous infusion of hydrocortisone 200 mg over 24 hours. If a continuous infusion is not feasible, give hydrocortisone 50 mg I.V. boluses every 6 hours. The duration and dose of the glucocorticoid regimen thereafter must be individualized based on the stressor type and the patient's clinical status."
Out-of-hospital persistent vomiting/diarrhea (Table 8): "Hydrocortisone 100 mg I.M. injection immediately, which can be repeated after 6 hours if needed. If symptoms do not resolve or hemodynamic instability develops, admit to hospital for I.V. urgent glucocorticoid and fluid administration."

## 4. Stress-dose guidance (illness and procedures)

**4.1 Who gets stress cover** R 3.1 [P69] (Good clinical practice): "patients with current or recent glucocorticoid use who did not undergo biochemical testing to rule out glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency should receive stress dose coverage when they are exposed to stress."

**4.2 Route by severity** R 3.1A [P70] and R 3.1B [P71], ungraded: oral "in case of minor stress and when there are no signs of hemodynamic instability or prolonged vomiting or diarrhea"; parenteral "in case of moderate to major stress, procedures under general or regional anesthesia, procedures requiring prolonged avoidance or inability of oral intake, or when there are signs of hemodynamic instability or prolonged vomiting or diarrhea."

**4.3 Minor stress** (Table 8, **read from image**). Examples: illness requiring bed rest; illness with fever (out of hospital); illness requiring antibiotics (out of hospital); significant emotional stress. General consideration: "If the patient is already taking hydrocortisone >=40 mg daily prednisone >=10 mg daily, or dexamethasone >=1 mg daily, there is typically no need to increase the dose unless there are signs of hemodynamic instability" (the guideline's printed text omits "or" after "daily" and uses the symbol for >=).
Regimen: "If on prednisone <10 mg total daily dose: increase to 10 mg total daily dose, to be given in one or two divided doses. Continue for 2-5 days until well (or for the duration of antibiotic treatment)." If not on daily glucocorticoids: hydrocortisone 40 mg total daily dose in three divided doses (20 mg on rising, 10 mg 12 midday, 10 mg 5pm), 2-5 days. Dexamethasone <1 mg: increase to 1 mg once daily, 2-5 days.

**4.4 Minor surgery / local anesthesia** (Table 8, **read from image**): "If on prednisone <10 mg total daily dose: increase to 10 mg total daily dose, to be given one hour prior to the procedure. Continue increased dose in patients who remain unwell after the procedure until clinically stable." Bowel procedures not under general anesthesia: "If on daily glucocorticoids: continue normal glucocorticoid dose. Give an equivalent I.V. dose if prolonged nil by mouth."

**4.5 Moderate/major stress threshold** (Table 8, **read from image**): "If the patient is already taking hydrocortisone >=200 mg daily, prednisone >=50 mg daily, or dexamethasone >=6-8 mg daily, there is typically no need to increase the dose". "In patients with suspected reduced absorption (persistent vomiting or diarrhea), nil by mouth, or unable to take tablets, give stress-dose glucocorticoids I.V. or I.M." "High body weight can be taken into consideration as a factor indicating higher dosage requirements".
Examples listed: persistent vomiting or diarrhea; infection requiring hospital admission or I.V. antibiotics (eg sepsis); acute trauma with significant blood loss or hospital admission.

**4.6 Surgery under general/regional anesthesia** (Table 8, **read from image**), both short-recovery/not NPO and NPO/long-recovery rows: "Intra-operative regimen: Hydrocortisone 100 mg I.V. bolus at induction, followed by immediate initiation of a continuous infusion of hydrocortisone 200 mg over 24 hours. If a continuous infusion is not feasible, give hydrocortisone 50 mg I.V. boluses every 6 hours." Postoperative (short recovery): resume oral "at an increased dose for 48 hours (eg, ... prednisone 10 mg/daily in one or two divided doses ...) and then resume the pre-surgical dose." NPO/long recovery: continue 200 mg/24 h infusion while NPO, then the same 48-hour oral step-down once eating. Complications: "maintain an increased oral dose or give stress-dose glucocorticoids I.V. as clinically appropriate." Labor and vaginal delivery: 100 mg I.V. bolus at onset then 200 mg/24 h.

**4.7 Rationale statements** (R 3.1 Rationale [P280], no grade): oral stress dose "double the physiologic replacement dose (ie, hydrocortisone 40 mg daily ...)"; for other glucocorticoids "a dose equivalent to 40 mg hydrocortisone is suggested ... eg, prednisone 10 mg total daily dose to be given in one or two divided doses". The authors state it is "based on clinical practice and the guidelines" and "in the absence of robust evidence ... practices vary considerably among centers and lower doses are also routinely used in patients under moderate or major stress".

**4.8 Potency conversion** (Table 1, text only [P79]): hydrocortisone 20 mg; prednisone 5 mg; prednisolone 5 mg; methylprednisolone 4 mg; dexamethasone 0.5 mg approximate equivalent doses, "a guide only" [P80].

**4.9 Risk persists after stopping** (R 3.1 Rationale [P280]): "Potential adrenal insufficiency and the need for stress dosing should be considered even after discontinuation of glucocorticoid therapy and replacement, particularly in those patients that had not undergone biochemical testing".

## 5. Transplant recipients and prednisone in the maintenance-immunosuppression range

**5.1 Explicitly named, but only as an example of patients who cannot stop glucocorticoids.**
- Introduction [P74]: glucocorticoids used "to the prevention of transplant rejection" (no recommendation).
- Table 4 Rationale [P148] (no grade): "HPA recovery is possible once the glucocorticoid therapy has been tapered to a near-physiologic daily dose (eg, 4-6 mg prednisone). At this time, taper or assessment for HPA recovery could be performed unless glucocorticoids at this dose are required for control of the underlying condition (for example transplant, or polymyalgia rheumatica)."
- R 2.4 Rationale [P160] (R 2.4 "We recommend against routine testing for adrenal insufficiency in patients on supraphysiologic doses of glucocorticoids, or if they are still in need of glucocorticoid treatment for the underlying disease", Good clinical practice): "testing is unnecessary in patients unable to stop glucocorticoid treatment, for example patients with organ transplants and in cases of polymyalgia rheumatica. These patients should be educated on management of glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency (see section R.3)."

**5.2 Does the guideline address low-dose maintenance prednisone?** Not as a transplant topic. There is no transplant-specific stress-dose regimen, no statement on tacrolimus/cyclosporine/mycophenolate, and no dose recommendation for transplant patients. What is usable by inference from general rules the guideline states (the guideline does not apply them to transplant recipients by name, so this is a reading, not a printed transplant statement): (a) a dose above 4-6 mg prednisone is "supraphysiologic" [P92, P95]; (b) the Table 8 minor-stress rule triggers an increase only if prednisone is "<10 mg total daily dose" (4.3), and "typically no need to increase" at >=10 mg unless "signs of hemodynamic instability"; (c) for moderate/major stress "prednisone >=50 mg daily" is the no-increase threshold (4.5); (d) an I.V./I.M. route is required with vomiting, diarrhea, NPO or hemodynamic instability (4.2, 3.7).

**5.3 Dose-range statements that touch 5 mg prednisone:** "The use of supraphysiologic glucocorticoids (prednisone equivalent dose > 5 mg daily) has been associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality (adjusted hazard ratio of 1.97 ...) in rheumatoid arthritis patients ... This association was not observed with daily glucocorticoid doses below 5 mg prednisone equivalent" (Epidemiology [P89]; observational, confounding acknowledged by authors).

**5.4 Tapering table** (Table 4, text only [P147]): 5-10 mg current prednisone: 1 mg decrease every 1-4 weeks; at 5 mg "In absence of clinical symptoms or negative testing for adrenal insufficiency continue 1 mg decrease". Not applicable to a patient who must stay on steroids.

## Ambiguities

- Table 8 printed text "If the patient is already taking hydrocortisone >=40 mg daily prednisone >=10 mg daily, or dexamethasone >=1 mg daily" has a missing "or" after the first item (typographical, in the image too).
- Table 8 minor-stress rows give "<10 mg" triggers for prednisone but the general-considerations cell says ">=10 mg" means no increase; the guideline does not say what to do at exactly 10 mg beyond that.
- Table 3 lists "Concurrent use of differently aadministered glucocorticoid" (typo as printed) under High route risk.
- Grade symbols: the guideline's legend [P38] lists "strong" as the top quality label, which differs from GRADE wording ("high"); I have reported grades per the guideline's own legend.
- The guideline states that clinical stress-dose regimens are "based on clinical practice" [P280]; they are not graded in the printed text (Table 8 has no grade), so nothing in section 4 or 3.7 carries an evidence grade beyond "Good clinical practice" for R 3.1/3.2.
